# Chapter X‡: in which the circle is found to have been drawn on a hidden surface, and the black hole and the universe turn out to be one shape seen from two chairs

*The last of three. [Chapter X](https://cosmicave.org/2026/05/11/x/) showed that a black hole's event horizon is a metric singularity — a single place that a flat map is forced to draw as a long line. [Chapter X‑dagger](#) took the horizon and the central "singularity" together and showed they are the same kind of object, the two ends of one smooth curve — a circle — with the textbook's headline "infinite curvature" number exposed as a label the map paints on, even as it conceded the honest sharper fact: that a deeper curvature genuinely does spike at the centre and not at the horizon, leaving one question unanswered — why is the geometry lopsided between two ends the curve treats alike? This piece answers it. It asks the question the dagger ended on: a circle is never drawn in a void; it is drawn on a surface. **What surface?** The answer is the strangest and simplest thing in the whole trilogy. The surface is the universe — and once you see the circle sitting on it, the black hole and the expanding cosmos stop being two different things, and the lopsided spike turns out to be the mark left by how one of them is drawn.*

---

Every article in this series has run on one trick, and it is worth saying out loud one last time before we use it for the biggest thing yet. The trick is: when something *looks* like a paradox, suspect the map before you suspect the world. A line on a Mercator map that seems to make Greenland the size of Africa is not telling you about Greenland; it is telling you about the projection. We found the event horizon was a place the map draws as a line. We found the central singularity was the same kind of place at the other end of a curve, its headline catastrophe a number the map paints on. Each time, the cure was the same: stop staring at the shadow and ask what is casting it.

This time we are going to ask it of the deepest pair of all. Not the horizon versus the centre — we settled those — but the black hole versus *the universe.* And we are going to find that they are not two things at all.

Before we do, recall exactly where the dagger left the centre, because it is the loose thread this piece is here to tie off. The dagger showed that the textbook's headline catastrophe — the $1/r^6$ number everyone points to — is a label the map paints on, because relabelling the curve's other end moves that number there. But it was careful, and conceded a sharper fact: a *deeper* curvature — the genuine bending of the surface the curve is swept out into — really does climb to infinity at the centre and stays finite at the horizon. So the centre is not "nothing happening, all bookkeeping." The curve is even-handed between its two ends, and yet the geometry comes out lopsided — a real spike at one end, none at the other. The dagger could name that lopsidedness but not explain it. Explaining it — finding what casts *that* shadow — is the last thing the trilogy owes you, and it is on this surface that the answer lives.

Let me build the surface first, because everything rests on actually seeing it.

## The shape of empty space

Start with the simplest possible universe: completely empty, but with the one ingredient that makes our real universe expand. Cosmologists call that ingredient the cosmological constant; you can think of it as a faint, even tension built into space itself, the same everywhere, that makes empty space want to grow. A universe that is *nothing but* that tension has a name — **de Sitter space** — and it has a definite shape.

The shape is a **hyperboloid**: take a vertical sheet of paper, draw the curve you would get by spinning a hyperbola — two arms sweeping apart as they rise and fall away from a narrow waist in the middle — and then spin *that* around its vertical axis. You get a surface like a cooling tower, or an hourglass that never quite pinches shut: infinitely wide at the top and bottom, drawn in to a narrowest circle around the middle. That narrowest circle is called the **throat**. (You have to imagine this living in a space with an extra dimension, the way the surface of a ball lives in three dimensions while being itself only two. Don't worry about picturing the extra dimension; the cooling-tower shape is the honest picture of the part that matters.)

Here is the first thing to hold. This shape is the *whole* of empty expanding space — not a snapshot of it at one moment, but the entire history laid out at once, the way a single sheet of spacetime holds all of time as a finished thing. Most popular accounts of the expanding universe show you space as a balloon being blown up, getting bigger frame by frame. That is one way to slice this surface into "moments." But the surface itself, taken whole, is the cooling tower: finite at the throat, opening without limit toward the top and bottom.

Now do the one thing this whole article turns on. **Look at the hyperboloid straight down its axis, from far above the top.** You are looking down the throat of the cooling tower. What do you see? An infinite flat plane stretching out in every direction — and a **hole** in the middle of it, the throat, a circle you are looking down into.

That hole is the key to everything, and it is a stranger kind of hole than it first appears.

## A hole that is not a puncture

When you hear "a hole in a surface," you picture a puncture — a spot poked out of a sheet, with nothing on the other side, edges you could run your finger around into empty air. The throat is not that. The throat is a **connecting passage.** The cooling tower has a top half and a bottom half, and the throat is the circular waist where they join. The "hole" you see looking down from above is not a place where the surface stops; it is a place where the surface *turns the corner* and heads off to the other half. Looking down the axis, you simply cannot see the far half — it is hidden behind the throat, the way the far side of a doughnut's hole is hidden when you look straight down at it on a table.

So the picture, looking down the axis, is: an infinite plane, with a circular hole at the centre that is really a passage to a hidden lower half you cannot see from here.

This is exactly the situation Chapter X‑dagger left us in without telling us — except there we were looking at a black hole, and here we are looking at empty expanding space. Hold that coincidence lightly for a moment; it is about to become the whole point.

## What a hole does to a line

Now we do the thing that built the circle in the previous article, but this time we will *watch where the circle comes from.*

Stand somewhere out on that infinite plane and look toward the centre. Draw a straight line of sight — the way you naturally fix your gaze, straight ahead. Send it toward the hole.

If your line is aimed *dead* at the centre, it dives straight down the throat and you are in a special, symmetric situation we will come back to. But almost no line is aimed dead-centre. A line aimed even slightly off-centre reaches the throat circle and **cannot go through** — there is no surface in the middle of the hole; the manifold is not there. The only thing the line can do, to stay on the surface, is **run around the throat circle** — bend along the rim — and come out heading toward the hidden lower half.

Follow it all the way and the shape of the journey is striking in its simplicity. The line comes in from far away, meets the ring, runs around it — passing the very back of the throat on the way — and then exits along the same kind of path it came in on, heading off to infinity on the far side. In from one infinity, around the ring, out to the other: that is the whole trip.

Stop and notice what just happened, because it is the secret of the whole construction. *You did nothing but try to look straight ahead on this surface, and the surface forced your line of sight to wrap around a circle.* You did not choose the circle. The hole chose it for you. A straight gaze, meeting a hole it cannot pass through, becomes a curve around the throat.

And now spin the hyperboloid slowly around its axis while you keep watching that wrapped line. As the surface turns, the line your gaze traces — down toward the throat, around the rim, out the other side — sweeps out a closed loop. **That loop is a circle.** Not a metaphor for a circle: the actual closed curve from the previous article, the one with two matching turning points, the one we found *was* the entire structure of a black hole. It is sitting right here, traced out by a straight line of sight meeting a hole on the surface of empty expanding space.

So here is the answer to the question Chapter X‑dagger ended on. The circle is drawn on the de Sitter hyperboloid — on the shape of the empty expanding universe. The black hole's whole structure is one particular curve on the surface of the cosmos.

## The chair you sit in chooses what you see

We now have one surface — the hyperboloid — and one curve on it — the circle, traced by a fixed gaze meeting the hole. And here is where the trilogy's trick pays off at the largest scale it can.

The *same curve* on the *same surface* gives you completely different-looking physics depending on **where you sit to look at it.** Not different geometry — the curve and the surface never change. Different *vantage.* Different chair.

And there really are two chairs, two genuine observers, not just a figure of speech. One observer sits *outside* the surface entirely, off in the surrounding space, and looks *at* the hyperboloid — they see the whole shape, the throat, the hole, the curve cutting across it from the outside. The other observer is *on* the surface, looking *along* it — for them there is no hole to see and nothing to be outside of; there is just the path running on ahead. The two are not looking at different things. They are looking at the one curve from two places, and — this is the part worth holding onto — the two places are at **right angles** to each other. Turn ninety degrees from the one chair and you are sitting in the other. They are perpendicular views of a single object, and the geometry insists on both: neither is the "real" one the other distorts. They are **complementary** — two descriptions that together exhaust the thing, the way the two perpendicular profiles of a shape do.

Sit in the first chair — outside, at a fixed distance from the hole, looking *in* toward the throat — and read the curve the way that vantage presents it: a line coming in from far away, reaching a closest circle it cannot cross (the throat), turning the corner, working inward to the other end. Trace that out and dress it back up into a full geometry, and what you get is **a black hole** — exterior, horizon, interior, central "singularity," the whole catalogue from the first two articles.

Now get up and move to the second chair — on the surface now, looking *along* it the long way, up the throat of the hyperboloid rather than in at it, riding along with the expansion instead of peering across it. Read the very same curve from that chair, and what you get is **an expanding universe** — de Sitter's smooth, growing, horizon-bounded cosmos, with no black hole and no central catastrophe anywhere in it.

Same circle. Same surface. Two chairs, ninety degrees apart. One chair calls the thing a black hole with a horizon and a singularity at its heart; the other chair calls it an expanding universe with none of those things. *And both are right*, because they are looking at one object — they have simply sat down in two complementary places to look at it.

This is the sharpest version of everything the trilogy has been building toward, so let me say it as plainly as it can be said. We are used to treating "black hole" and "expanding universe" as two different objects, each with its own list of features — this one has a horizon and a deadly singularity, that one has a smoothly growing space. The list of features feels like it belongs to the *object.* It does not. The list belongs to the **chair.** The horizon, the singularity, the catastrophe at the centre — these are features of *one particular way of sitting down to look at a circle on a surface.* Move to the other chair and they are simply not there, because there was never an object that "had" them; there was a surface, and a curve, and a viewpoint.

What looks like a curvature singularity — the end of physics, the place the textbooks wall off — to the observer in the first chair *does not exist as such in the geometry the second observer is describing at all.* Be precise about what "as such" is carrying, because there are three things here and only the last of them is a costume. The spike is real. It is a genuine feature of the picture the first chair builds, it does not relabel away, and nothing in what follows will make it go away. But it is a feature of *that picture*, not of the hyperboloid the picture is built from. The hyperboloid is evenly curved — the same gentle bend at every point of it, at the throat and away from it, no spike anywhere — and it is the one surface both chairs are reading. So the spike is not something the second chair is failing to notice on a shared surface. It is something the first chair's picture has and the second chair's simply does not, raised in the one and absent from the other, while the surface underneath them both sits there unspiked. And the spike's *costume* — its reading as an absolute edge where the world stops, a hole torn in reality — is a third thing again, and the first chair's alone: the second chair, reading the same curve on the same surface, meets no endpoint in it at all. The spike is real, and it is the chair's; the catastrophe is the sitting.

## Why one chair sees a catastrophe and the other sees nothing

You should push back here, because there is a real question lurking. If it is genuinely one curve on one surface, why does one vantage manufacture a horrifying infinite-curvature singularity while the other vantage glides along seeing nothing of the kind? A pure relabelling shouldn't be able to do that. Something more than naming must be going on.

It is, and naming it is the last piece of the trilogy.

To turn the curve into a full picture of space, you have to do more than trace it — you have to **sweep it around.** The curve itself is just a radial line, a one-dimensional thread; a real geometry has the two extra directions you can swing around in. So each observer takes their curve and spins it about an axis to fill in those directions, the way spinning a single drawn profile gives you a whole vase. And *which axis you spin about* is set by where you sit.

The cosmic observer — the one looking along the surface — spins the curve about the hyperboloid's **own** central axis, the natural axis of the surface itself. Spinning a thing about its own axis of symmetry costs nothing; it respects the shape it is built from, and what comes back is the smooth expanding universe, with no special point manufactured anywhere.

The black-hole observer does something subtly but decisively different — and here is the catch we have been walking toward for three articles. That observer sits *outside* the surface, looking in, and has **located the hole** — made it a fixed object seen across, in their timelike line of sight. Having located it that way, they can no longer do what the cosmic chair does. The clean sweep about the surface's own axis of symmetry is the *inside* view, the cosmic one; an observer looking *across* the surface rather than along it, with the hole fixed in front of them, cannot spin the symmetric surface about that axis. They are forced onto the only pivot their vantage leaves them: the point the map calls "the centre," $r = 0$. And here is the part the chart hides — $r=0$ **is** a point of the surface. Not the point the name suggests: there is no interior centre, only the throat with the hole in it. The spot the map labels "the centre" is a real point *on the surface* — a point on the throat ring itself, off at the back, where the inward curve runs to its end. It is the very point the dagger watched the sweep punch out into a *hole*: the hole is the swept black-hole picture's doing, while on the surface itself the point is simply, smoothly there. The black-hole observer pivots the whole sweep about *that* point: a genuine point of the surface, but one sitting **off** the surface's axis of symmetry, pressed into playing the part of a centre it never was.

*That* is the move that manufactures the catastrophe — and now we can say exactly how, in a way that honours the sharper fact the dagger conceded. Recall: a deeper curvature genuinely does spike at the centre and not at the horizon. That spike is real; the question the dagger left open was why the geometry is lopsided when the curve is even-handed. Here is the answer. The two ends of the curve are alike as turning points, but only one of them is the point the black-hole chair is forced to pivot its sweep about — $r=0$, the off-axis point at the back of the ring. Pivoting a sweep about a point that is *not* the surface's axis of symmetry — making a centre of a place that was never a centre — forces a distortion the geometry was never built to carry, and that distortion piles up precisely at the pivot. So the spike is not painted from nothing, nor is it evenly shared: it is a *real* feature, concentrated at the one end because that is the off-axis point the forced sweep pivots on. Spin about the surface's true axis — the cosmic chair — and there is no off-axis pivot, so nothing piles up anywhere. The lopsidedness is the difference between the two sweeps.

Here is the cleanest way to feel why a forced off-axis pivot leaves a spike, and it needs only a cone. Roll a flat sheet into a cone. The straight edge you rolled has no bend in it at all — and yet the cone has one point, its tip, that is infinitely sharp. The sharpness did not come from the edge, which is straight; it came from the rolling — from sweeping that edge around an axis the edge runs *into* at the tip. A surface of revolution carries a curvature that is the profile's own bend divided by the distance out to the axis it is spun about, and at the tip that distance is zero. Divide any finite bend by zero and a spike stands up — a point of infinite curvature, sitting exactly where the sweep meets its axis. That is what $r=0$ is. The curve's bend there is no greater than at the horizon — the two ends bend alike. But $r=0$ is the one end the black-hole chair is forced to sweep *about*, the place its own rotation axis runs through; and the identical bend, divided there by a vanishing distance, stands up into a cone tip. The horizon's equal bend sits out at a healthy distance from that same sweep's axis and stays gentle. Same bend — one swept at the axis, one swept away from it. The cosmic chair, spinning about the surface's true axis instead, never brings the curve to its own rotation axis at all, and so raises no tip anywhere. The spike is the cone tip the forced pivot makes.

This is the thing Chapter X‑dagger could point to but not explain. The dagger showed that the textbook's $1/r^6$ headline number is a label, by showing that relabelling the other end would move *that number* — but it was careful to add that the deeper spike does not relabel away, and to leave open why the spike sits where it does. This article closes that: the deeper spike falls at the centre and not the horizon because one observer, forced by the hole into a particular vantage, sweeps their curve about $r=0$ — a real point of the surface, but the wrong one, an off-axis point on its ring pressed into service as a centre — and a spike is what a sweep leaves where it is forced to pivot off the axis.

The asymmetry between the horizon and the singularity — the thing that made one feel removable and the other feel like the end of the world — was never in the *curve*, which treats its two ends alike. The dagger told you that, and told you the lopsidedness had to come from how the flat map is built. Now you can see the exact place it comes from: the difference between spinning about the surface's true axis and being forced to spin about an off-axis point on its ring. The spike is real, and it sits where the forced pivot sits.

## Even the mass belongs to the chair

There is one more thing the two chairs disagree about, and it is the most surprising of all, because it is the thing we are usually surest is a hard fact about the object: the black hole's **mass.**

The mass of a black hole feels like the realest number it has — how much stuff fell in, how hard it pulls, how big its horizon. Surely *that*, at least, is a property of the object and not of the chair. But follow the construction. The first chair — the outside observer looking in at the hole — reads off a mass from how the curve sits in their view, and that reading depends on exactly where they are sitting and how the throat is angled in their sky. Move the chair, and the number moves. The mass is not stamped on the surface; it is something the outside observer *computes from their vantage,* and a differently placed observer computes a different one.

So what, if anything, do *all* the observers agree on? Just one thing: the size of the hole itself — the radius of the throat. That radius is set by the surface alone, the same from every chair, the one number no choice of where-to-sit can touch. And here is the quiet bombshell. What every observer agrees is invariant is not "the mass of the black hole." It is **the size of the hole in the universe.** The mass — the quantity the textbooks treat as the black hole's defining substance — turns out to be the chair's reading of that one invariant size, tilted and scaled by the vantage. The thing that is real is the hole; the mass is how the hole looks from where you happen to be standing.

You can feel how strange that is by holding it next to the ordinary picture. Ordinarily the mass is the cause and the hole is the effect: mass fell in, *therefore* there is a throat of such-and-such size. Read off the surface, the order of dependence looks inverted — the throat, a feature of the empty expanding universe's own shape, is the invariant thing, and what a given observer calls "the mass" is a reading of it from their particular seat. Whether the right move is then to call the hole's size the black hole's *true* mass — to promote the invariant to the title the vantage-dependent number has always held — is a deeper question, and an open one; this article only goes as far as the geometry plainly forces, which is that the size is the invariant and the mass is the view.

## What this leaves us with

So the trilogy closes where it could not have begun. Chapter X showed a single place drawn as a line. Chapter X‑dagger showed two such places were the two ends of one circle, the central catastrophe's headline number a painted label and its deeper spike a real but unexplained lopsidedness. This piece shows the circle was drawn all along on the surface of the empty expanding universe — that the lopsided spike is the mark a sweep leaves when the hole forces it to pivot off the surface's axis, and that "black hole" and "expanding cosmos" are not two objects but one surface read from two chairs, with the entire fearsome inventory of the black hole — horizon, singularity, even its mass — turning out to be the price of sitting in the chair the hole forces — the one made to spin the world about an off-axis point rather than about its true axis. The one thing that survives the choice of chair is the size of the hole itself.

There is something almost unreasonable about how simple the bottom of it is. A surface with a waist. A straight look that meets the waist and is forced into a circle. And then a choice of where to sit. Everything else — the horizon, the singularity, the mass, the place where physics was said to end, the apparent gulf between a dead collapsed star and a living growing cosmos — is what those things look like from one chair rather than another.

The universe, it seems, is not hiding its strangeness in exotic places. It is hiding it in plain sight, in the difference between two ways of looking at a circle.

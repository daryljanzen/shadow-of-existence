# Points, places, and the three kinds of singularity: what the event horizon and the centre of a black hole really have in common

*The middle of three. [Chapter X](https://cosmicave.org/2026/05/11/x/) showed that the event horizon is a metric singularity — the single event, one beat after the infinite future of the outside universe, at which everything that ever falls in arrives at once: infinitely many distinct, causally ordered events with no separation between them. This piece sets that horizon beside the other famous feature of a black hole — the "singularity" at the centre, where the textbooks say physics breaks down — and asks what kind of thing each of them actually is. The answer turns out to dissolve a claim that has stood for sixty years, and it can be seen without any heavy machinery at all, by anyone willing to follow a curve around a circle. There is a third piece still to come, in which that circle turns out to be drawn on something larger; but everything here stands on its own, and we will stop where this installment's result is complete rather than reach for the next one.*

---

Suppose I tell you that two very different-looking things are, underneath, the same kind of object — and that the only reason they look different is the map we happened to draw them on. You would be right to want proof. Not an assertion, not an appeal to authority, but the kind of thing you can see for yourself once it's laid out, the way you can see — once you've compared 1/2 with 99/100 — that 6/7 simply *must* be larger than 5/6, no matter what your memory of the rule says.

That is the kind of proof this article is going to build, about the two singularities of a black hole.

In Chapter X we met one of them: the event horizon. We found it was not a surface sitting out in space waiting to be crossed, but a *metric singularity* — a place where the geometry's ruler has gone to zero, so that infinitely many distinct, causally ordered events all occur at one place, at once, with no distance and no duration between them. It is not even an event at any ordinary moment: it is the very next instant *after* the outside universe's time has run out — the boundary everything infalling reaches together, in the infinite-future limit. The other singularity — the one at the very centre, at the radius the textbooks call $r = 0$ — has a darker reputation. It is supposed to be the place where the curvature becomes infinite, where the manifold itself tears open, where general relativity throws up its hands and says: *here, physics breaks down.*

We are going to look at both of them with the same tools we used in Chapter X — analogy, recognising consistent structure, testing trends at their extremes — and we are going to find something that ought to be surprising. On the underlying curve, the two singularities are the same kind of object: two matching turning points of one smooth arc. And yet the geometry comes out lopsided between them — one end placid, the other carrying a genuine spike of curvature. That lopsidedness, the thing that makes one look like a mere quirk of coordinates and the other like a catastrophe where physics ends, is not put there by the curve, which treats its two ends alike. It is manufactured by the map — by a particular, and as we will see avoidable, choice in how the flat picture is drawn from the curve. The spike is real; its lopsided placement, and its reading as the end of the world, are the map's doing.

To get there we first need to be careful about three words that get used loosely: *point*, *place*, and *singularity*. They are not the same, and the entire result turns on the differences between them.

## What a geometry is made of

Every geometry we have talked about — the flat Euclidean space you walk around in, the Lorentzian spacetime of relativity, the shrinking Andromeda of Chapter X with its metric singularity at the end — is built from two ingredients, and the way we put them together.

The first ingredient is the **manifold**. A manifold is just a set of points and the order they come in. The real number line — the entire set of numbers, rational and irrational, laid out in order — is the simplest example. It runs continuously, with no gaps anywhere between its points. That makes it the prototype of a smooth one-dimensional manifold: a set of points, in order, with nothing missing.

The second ingredient is the **metric**. The metric is the ruler. It is the rule that says how far apart any two points are, and how the manifold curves as you move from one to the next. The manifold gives you the points; the metric gives you the distances between them. Put the two together and you have a geometry. That really is all a geometry is, for our purposes — a set of points, and a ruler laid over them.

Now here is the first distinction we need, and it is the one everything else rests on.

A **point** is a member of the manifold — an actual element of the set. A **place** is a location in a *chart*, a value of the coordinates, a spot on the map where the map says something could be drawn. Most of the time, one place holds exactly one point, and we never notice the difference between the two words. But they can come apart. And when they come apart, you get a singularity.

There are three ways they can come apart. That is the whole story: three kinds of singularity, corresponding to the three things that can happen at a place.

## The first kind: a place with no point (the curvature singularity)

Think of the tangent function — the curve $\tan\theta$ that you may have met in trigonometry. As the angle $\theta$ climbs toward a right angle (toward $\pi/2$, or 90 degrees), the tangent shoots upward, growing without bound, and at exactly $\pi/2$ it simply does not exist. Infinity is not a number; the function has no value there. Climb a little past $\pi/2$ and it reappears, plunging up from below on the far side.

Read the tangent function as the *ruler* of a little one-dimensional geometry — a curve with well-defined length and curvature between any two of its points — and notice what happens at $\pi/2$. The chart has no trouble reaching that place: the angle $\pi/2$ is a perfectly good angle, a perfectly good coordinate value. But the manifold has no point there. The ruler has gone infinite, and where the ruler goes infinite, the manifold has a hole.

That is the first kind of singularity: **a place with no point.** The coordinates can name the spot, but there is nothing there — the curvature has diverged and punched a hole in the manifold. We call it a **curvature singularity.** It is the kind of singularity the textbooks say sits at the centre of a black hole.

## The second kind: a point at many places (the coordinate singularity)

For the second kind, look at any map of the Earth.

The surface of the Earth is a two-dimensional manifold with the geometry of a sphere. The usual chart we lay over it is latitude and longitude. And that chart has two strange places: the two poles. The North Pole is a single point of the manifold — one place you could stand. But on the chart it is smeared across every value of longitude at once, because every line of longitude runs up to meet it. On a Mercator map — the rectangular one on every classroom wall — the North Pole is not drawn as a point at all. It is drawn as the *entire top edge of the map*, a whole horizontal line. The South Pole is the entire bottom edge.

This is the second kind of singularity: **a point at many places.** One single point of the manifold, spread out by the chart into a long line of coordinate values. We call it a **coordinate singularity.**

Anyone who has used a world map knows, at least intuitively, how to read this. The top edge of a Mercator map is not really a continent-long stripe of Arctic; it is one point, stretched by the projection. Greenland is not really the size of Africa. You know to read the distortion back out, because you know it is the map doing it and not the world. *Change to a chart centred on the pole and the stretched line collapses back to the single point it always was.* The spreading was never in the geometry. It was in the projection.

You can make the same thing happen on a flat sheet of paper, with no sphere at all. Take the centre of the page. In ordinary $(x, y)$ coordinates it sits at one place: $(0,0)$ — one point, one place. Now describe the same page in polar coordinates, by distance $r$ from the centre and angle $\theta$ around it. The centre is now at $r = 0$ — but it has *no particular angle*, because you haven't gone anywhere from it, so every angle lands you at the centre. In this chart the single centre-point is described by $r = 0$ at every value of $\theta$ — drawn as an entire line of coordinate places, one for each angle. One point, infinitely many places. The geometry of the flat page hasn't changed at all; only the coordinates have. *That is the tell of a coordinate singularity: it is an artefact of the chart, and the right chart makes it vanish.*

Hold on to that, because it is exactly the property that is about to be argued over.

## The third kind: many points at one place (the metric singularity)

The first two kinds — a place with no point, and a point at many places — show up in *any* geometry, the flat ones included. But the spacetime of relativity has a third kind, and it exists only because of the one strange feature that makes Lorentzian geometry Lorentzian: the minus sign.

Recall the rule from Chapter X. In the geometry of spacetime, the separation between two events is built from the *difference* of their squared spatial and temporal parts, not the sum:

$$(\Delta s)^2 = (\Delta x)^2 - (\Delta t)^2.$$

That single minus sign — where flat Euclidean space has a plus — is the whole of what makes spacetime strange. Its most important consequence is the one light lives by: the separation between two events can be *zero* even when they are genuinely apart in space and in time, provided the spatial and temporal parts are equal and cancel. The path of a photon is exactly this — an isosceles right triangle whose two legs are equal, so its hypotenuse, the spacetime separation, is zero by identity.

Now here is the third kind of singularity. Because Lorentzian geometry lets distinct points sit at zero spacetime separation along a null line, it also allows the special case where they sit at zero *spatial* separation as well. And once two points are null-separated (zero spacetime interval) *and* spatially coincident (zero spatial interval), the minus-sign arithmetic forces the third quantity, their temporal separation, to be zero too. Two of the three zero, and the third has no choice. The points stay distinct, stay causally ordered — but the ruler between them has collapsed in every sense: no space, no time, no spacetime.

This is the structure Chapter X built with the shrinking Andromeda: a galaxy whose near and far sides draw together until, in the limit, every emission event across its whole depth arrives at one place at one instant, infinitely many distinct events with no separation laid over them. **Many points at one place.** We call it a **metric singularity.**

And look at how it sits against the second kind. The coordinate singularity is *one point at many places*. The metric singularity is *many points at one place*. They are mirror images of each other, reflected across the line between "point" and "place." The coordinate singularity spreads one point out into a line that a better chart collapses; the metric singularity gathers many points into one place that *no* chart can pull apart, because the collapse is in the ruler, not in the projection.

That mirror-image relationship is the hinge of everything that follows. Two kinds of singularity that look, *on a map*, almost identical — both drawn as a line where you might have expected a point — but which are opposite in nature. One is the chart telling a fib about a single point. The other is the geometry genuinely crushing many points together.

## Why the event horizon is the third kind, not the second

This is the misreading that has cost us sixty years, and we can now say it precisely.

When you draw a black hole on the standard spacetime diagram — the Eddington–Finkelstein diagram of Chapter X — the event horizon appears as a vertical line at the radius $r_h$. A line. And the trained eye, seeing a feature drawn as a line where it expected a surface, reaches for the familiar explanation: *it's a coordinate singularity, like the pole on a Mercator map — an artefact of the chart, a single place smeared out, nothing real, read it back out and move on.* That is exactly what was done. The horizon was filed under "removable coordinate singularity" and set aside.

But the horizon is not a Mercator pole. Chapter X showed why: every event at which anything ever crosses the horizon happens at the *same* radius, $r_h$ — the same place — so the horizon's events are spatially coincident, and being null as well, they are metrically coincident by the minus-sign theorem. The vertical line on the diagram is not one point stretched into a line by a careless chart. It is *many* points — every crossing event in the whole history of the hole — genuinely piled at one place, with the ruler collapsed between them. It is a metric singularity. It looks identical, on the page, to a Mercator pole. It is the opposite kind of thing. The chart draws a metric singularity and a coordinate singularity with the very same picture — a place rendered as a line — and that visual coincidence is the entire reason the horizon was misfiled.

So we already have two of the black hole's features placed in the taxonomy. The horizon is a metric singularity (third kind), long mistaken for a coordinate singularity (second kind). And the centre, $r = 0$, is supposed to be a curvature singularity (first kind) — the place with no point, where the manifold tears. Two singularities, two boxes, and an asymmetry between them that the whole standard picture leans on: the horizon is *removable*, a mere artefact; the centre is *not removable*, a genuine hole in the world.

That asymmetry is what we are going to test. And to test it we are going to build a manifold of our own, from scratch, and watch what its singularities do.

## Building a geometry with two metric singularities, by hand

Here is a construction you can carry out in your head. We are going to take a sphere and, using nothing but a stretching rule, turn it into a new geometry that has a metric singularity at each of its two poles — and we are going to watch the two come out *the same kind of thing*, distinguished only by a label we get to choose.

Start with a sphere, and pick a starting pole — call it the south pole. Measure your way along the surface toward the north pole by the angle $\theta$ you have swept from the south, running from $0$ at the south pole to a right angle, $\pi/2$, at the north. (One right angle takes you all the way from pole to pole; you do not need a half-turn — the angle is measured along the surface from one pole to the other.) That angle is our manifold coordinate. Now lay a ruler over it by a stretching rule — a function that turns the angle $\theta$ into a radial distance $r$. The rule we choose is the tangent: as $\theta$ runs from $0$ toward $\pi/2$, let the radial coordinate $r$ run as $\tan\theta$, from $0$ out toward infinity.

Watch the two poles.

At the south pole, $\theta = 0$: the radial coordinate is $r = 0$, and — this is the part we build in deliberately — we let the curvature run as the *co*-tangent, which is infinite there. A place, $r = 0$, at which the curvature has diverged. The manifold has no point there. That is a curvature singularity — the first kind — built to order, exactly like the hole in the tangent function.

At the north pole, $\theta = \pi/2$: the tangent rule sends the radial coordinate $r$ out to infinity, while the curvature runs the other way, smoothly down to a finite value. Now ask the question Chapter X taught us to ask — push the trend to its extreme and read off what it forces. As $\theta$ climbs toward $\pi/2$, the little circles of latitude on our sphere shrink toward nothing: the north pole is a single place, approached from every direction at once, just as the south pole was. Infinitely many distinct directions of approach, all landing at one place, with the curvature finite there. That is *many points at one place with the ruler well-behaved* — a metric singularity, the third kind, with finite curvature.

So our hand-built geometry has a singularity at each pole — a *dipole* of two metric singularities, one at each end of a single curve. At both poles the same thing is happening: infinitely many directions of approach converge on one place, and the ruler between them collapses. They differ in one respect only — the setting of a single dial, the curvature. At the finite-curvature pole the convergent place is intact: many points crushed to one place, the ruler collapsed, the manifold whole. At the infinite-curvature pole the dial is turned to its extreme, and the divergence punches the convergent point out — the same converging structure, but pushed so far that the place can no longer hold a point and the manifold has a hole there. That is the sense in which the two are *the same kind of object*: both are places where every approach is crushed together and the chart degenerates; the catastrophic one is just the convergence taken to the extreme of the dial, a metric singularity pushed until its point fails. Same structure, two settings of one dial.

And here the taxonomy we set out with quietly rearranges itself — and this is the thing I did not see coming until the dipole was built and sitting in front of me. We began with three kinds, and filed the centre of a black hole under the *first* of them: the curvature singularity, the place with no point, a box of its own. The dipole shows that box was never separate. The infinite-curvature pole is the *metric* singularity — many points at one place, our third kind — with the dial run to its extreme, the convergent point punched out into a hole. The first kind was the third kind all along, in the costume the dial puts on it. So there are not three kinds here but two: the coordinate singularity, where a chart smears one point across many places and a better chart undoes it; and the metric singularity, where the geometry crushes many points into one place and no chart undoes it — a single kind wearing two faces, finite-curvature and infinite-curvature, at two settings of one dial. The catastrophe the textbooks enshrine at the centre of a black hole is not a kind of its own. It is the metric singularity, turned all the way up.

And the two ends are not independent. They are a linked pair — the two ends of one curve, two turning points of identical kind, so that whatever you say about the structure of one, you have said about the other. What is *not* forced by the curve is the lopsidedness: the curve is even-handed, and yet the real black hole comes out with the curvature spike planted firmly at one end and not the other. Something downstream of the curve decides which end carries the catastrophe and dresses it as the end of physics. The curve hands you a symmetric pair; the asymmetry is added afterward. Hold that — it is exactly what the real black hole will turn out to be doing, and naming the thing that adds the asymmetry is the work of the final piece.

(If you would rather both poles sat at a finite radius, like the two finite radii of a real black hole — $r = 0$ and $r = r_h$ — you need only choose a stretching rule whose range is finite instead of running out to infinity. The *kind* of singularity at each pole does not change; only where the chart draws it changes. The type lives in the pole; the radius is just where the map happens to put it.)

## The cycloid: the same thing, in the real black hole

That was a toy, built to show the structure cleanly. The remarkable part is that the actual Schwarzschild black hole — the real solution Karl Schwarzschild wrote down in 1916, the one Chapter X's collapsing star settles into — is built the very same way, and you can see it on a single curve.

There is a classic way of describing the inside of a black hole using a curve called a **cycloid** — the curve traced by a point on the rim of a wheel as the wheel rolls along the ground. (It is the same curve that solves the famous "fastest slide between two points" problem; it turns up all over physics.) For the black hole, the cycloid records how the radius $r$ changes as you move inward. Written out, the radius along it is

$$r(z) = M\,(1 + \cos z),$$

where $M$ is the mass and $z$ is the rolling angle. At $z = 0$ the curve sits at $r = 2M$ — that is the horizon. Roll along to $z = \pi$ and it reaches $r = 0$ — that is the centre, the famous singularity.

Now look at the two ends of that arc the way we have learned to look. They are both *turning points* of a single smooth curve — places where the curve momentarily stops changing radius and turns around, the way a thrown ball stops at the top of its arc. And they are turning points of exactly the same kind. The curve is perfectly smooth through both. The rate at which the radius turns around is the same size at each end (it is $M$ at both, with only a sign distinguishing a peak from a valley). On the curve itself — on the actual smooth manifold — the horizon end and the centre end are *the same kind of object*: two matching turning points of one smooth arc, no more different from each other than the top of one ball's flight is from the top of another's.

It is worth seeing *why* the curve has to turn around at all, because it is the same fact as the metric singularity, wearing different clothes. As you move inward and the radius drops toward a turning point, the rate at which it drops goes to zero — the curve's tangent goes flat, then vertical, and there is simply no "further inward" left to go in that direction. The curve has run out of radius. The only thing it *can* do to continue is pivot — turn ninety degrees and head off along the other direction available to it. The turning point is not a wall the curve slams into; it is the place where the only way to keep going is to turn the corner. That ninety-degree pivot is the geometry's version of the swap that happens at a black hole's horizon, where what was a direction through space becomes a direction through time. The curve turns the corner because the place it has reached is one where every approaching path has been crushed together — which is exactly what a metric singularity is.

So why does everyone say one of them — the centre — is a catastrophe where physics ends, while the other — the horizon — is removable?

Here is the entire reason, and it is worth slowing down for, because it is the punchline. The "catastrophe" at the centre is measured by a curvature quantity that behaves like $1/r^6$ — divide by the radius six times over. As the cycloid approaches the centre, the radius $r$ falls to zero like a small quantity *squared*. Feed "something going to zero, squared" into "divide by it six times" and of course you get an explosion: the curvature quantity blows up. But look at *where the explosion comes from*. It comes from the radius being *labelled* zero at that turning point and then stuck in the denominator. The proof that this much is bookkeeping is devastatingly simple: *had we labelled the other turning point — the horizon — as $r = 0$ instead, the identical explosion would have landed there.* That particular divergence tracks the label on the turning point, not the turning point itself. The curve does not know or care which end we called zero.

Now, I am going to be more careful here than the usual telling, because the honest story is sharper than "it's all just a label" — and the sharper version is the one that opens the door to the final piece. There is more than one way to measure curvature. The $1/r^6$ quantity above is the one the textbooks reach for, and we have just seen it move when we move the label. But there is a deeper measure — the *intrinsic* curvature of the surface the curve sweeps out, the bending you would feel in the geometry itself, independent of any chart. And that one does *not* simply relabel away. It is perfectly finite at the horizon — the horizon end is genuinely, intrinsically smooth, no spike at all. But at the centre, the intrinsic curvature *does* run to infinity. So the centre is not, after all, a turning point where utterly nothing happens. Something does happen there that does not happen at the horizon: the deep curvature climbs without bound.

This is the fact the usual "it's just a label" story papers over, and facing it squarely is what makes the result land instead of merely sounding clever. The two turning points are the same kind of object *on the curve* — two matching pivots of one smooth arc, that much is solid — and yet the geometry is not symmetric between them: one end stays smooth in every measure, the other carries a genuine spike. So the real question is not "is the centre's infinity real or fake?" — it is *real*, in the deep measure — but rather: *why is it there at one end and not the other, when the curve treats both ends alike?* What broke the symmetry? That is precisely the question the next installment answers, and the answer is not in the curve at all. It is in how the flat map is built from the curve — in a second, hidden choice the standard picture makes without telling you, that pins the centre as a special point and pours the divergence onto it. Hold the question; we will earn the answer on the larger surface.

## The Eddington–Finkelstein diagram is a Mercator map

We can now say, plainly, what the standard black-hole diagram actually is.

A Mercator map takes a sphere — an object with two poles, each a single place — and draws each pole as a long line across an edge, because the projection it uses degenerates at the poles. Anyone literate in maps knows to read those edge-lines back as the single places they are, and knows there is a whole other hemisphere folded away past the bottom edge.

The Eddington–Finkelstein diagram does precisely this to the black hole. It takes a geometry with two metric singularities — two special places — and draws each of them as a long vertical line, because the chart degenerates there exactly as Mercator degenerates at the poles. The horizon at $r_h$ is one such line; the centre at $r = 0$ is the other. They are the two poles of the construction, drawn long because the projection drew them long. The map even has its hidden hemisphere: the region the mathematics calls "negative $r$," the conjugate side that the cycloid runs into when you continue it past the centre — the part of the world that has spilled off the bottom edge of the map, that the careless reader never thinks to look for.

But there is one way the black hole's poles are *not* like the Earth's, and it is the way that matters most. The North Pole, drawn back from its Mercator stripe to the single point it is, becomes an ordinary point of the globe — nothing special once the chart stops lying about it. The horizon and the centre do not. To build the full three-dimensional geometry of the black hole from our curve, you have to *sweep* the curve around — rotate it through the directions it does not itself contain, the way spinning a drawn profile builds a solid. And sweeping does nothing to a metric singularity. It rotates the place around, multiplying the points that live there — but it adds no separation between them, because zero swept through any angle is still zero. So after the sweep that builds all of space around the black hole, the horizon is *still* one metric place full of distinct events at no separation, and the centre is *still* singular too — but its own infinite-curvature face of the same thing, the convergent point punched out into a hole rather than a place full of events. They are not poles that a better chart turns back into ordinary points. They are places where the ruler has genuinely collapsed, and they stay collapsed under every chart and through the whole sweep. That is the difference the Mercator picture hides: the Earth's pole is a real point the map merely stretched; the black hole's "poles" are metric singularities the map stretched the very same way — and no unstretching makes them ordinary, because the collapse was never in the map.

If you already know how to read a Mercator map — if you know not to believe that Greenland is a continent-spanning stripe, and to remember there is more world below the bottom edge — then you already know most of how to read the Eddington–Finkelstein diagram. The two vertical lines are not what they appear. They are two places, each drawn long by a projection, and there is more geometry past the edge. The one extra thing to carry, which the globe does not prepare you for, is that these two places do not become ordinary points when you read the distortion back out; they stay singular, because their singularity is in the geometry and not in the drawing.

There is one more thing the diagram is quietly doing, and naming it makes the whole picture click. The curve we have been following is a slice through spacetime, and it changes character at the horizon. On the way *in* to the horizon, it is a slice through *space at one moment* — you are crossing a sphere from its outside toward its throat, all at a single instant. At the horizon, the curve turns its corner. And on the far side, it is a slice through *time at one place* — you are no longer crossing space, you are running forward through time at a fixed location. That is the swap the turning point performs: a direction that was spatial becomes temporal. It is also why the horizon shows up on the diagram as a vertical line. A vertical line on a spacetime diagram is a line of *constant place, extended through time* — and that is exactly what the horizon is once the curve has turned its corner: one place, drawn out along the time direction, every event on it sitting at the same spot. The line looks like an extended stretch of somewhere. It is a single somewhere, smeared along time by a chart that has run out of room — the metric singularity, drawn the only way a flat diagram can draw it.

## The black swan

Now we can land the result this whole article was built to deliver, and it is sharper than "here is a nicer way to see it."

For sixty years the centre of a black hole has carried a specific, absolute claim — but we have to state it as the claim that actually does the work, not the looser version it is often confused with. The looser version is "the curvature is infinite there," and we have just granted that, in the deep measure, it *is*. That alone was never the load-bearing part: a quantity going infinite is not, by itself, a place where the world ends. The load-bearing claim is stronger and quieter. It is that $r = 0$ is an *absolute terminus* — the edge of the manifold, the place the construction simply stops, beyond which there is nothing and to which no description can carry you across. The horizon could be demoted to a mere coordinate artefact precisely because *some* description carries straight through it. The centre was held to be different in kind, because — so the claim went — *nothing* carries through it: it is where the curve, and the world, run out.

"Nothing carries through it" is a universal claim. It is "all swans are white." And a universal claim of that form is undone, completely and permanently, by a single counterexample. One black swan and "all swans are white" is finished — not weakened, not made less likely, *finished* — and it does not matter in the slightest whether you would have preferred the swan to be white.

The cycloid is the black swan. It is an explicit, finite, smooth construction in which the curve does not stop at $r = 0$ but turns its corner and *continues* — through the centre and out the conjugate side — carried across by the very same step that everyone already accepts for carrying through the horizon. The claim was that the centre is where everything ends and nothing continues. Here is a construction that continues. It exists. The universal is refuted. And note exactly what is refuted and what is not: not that the deep curvature spikes at the centre — it does, and we have said so — but that the spike marks an *end*. The spike is real; the wall is not. A curve can run smoothly through a point where a curvature climbs, the way a road can run straight through a place where the ground happens to be steepest; the steepness is real, and it is not a barrier.

And notice what the counterexample does *not* need to do. It does not need to be crowned the one true description of nature. It does not need you to agree that the smooth curve is more real than the standard map. A black swan does not have to be declared the only real swan to kill "all swans are white" — it only has to *exist*. The cycloid only has to be a legitimate, smooth construction in which the centre is something the curve passes through rather than ends at, and it is. After that, the claim that the centre of a black hole is an absolute edge where the world and all inquiry stop is simply false. Not reinterpreted. Falsified.

There is a reason this matters beyond tidiness, and the philosopher Karl Popper put his finger on it. A scientific claim, Popper argued, earns its standing by *forbidding* something — by being the kind of claim a counterexample could in principle overturn. "Physics breaks down at $r = 0$, full stop" had quietly stopped being that kind of claim. It had become a place where inquiry was told to *stop*: the geometry tears, the equations fail, there is nothing more to ask. That is not a finding; it is the suspension of further questioning, dressed as a finding. What the cycloid does, in Popper's terms, is hand the question back to science. It replaces "stop here, it ends" — which forbids nothing and explains nothing — with claims that *can* be checked and *could* be wrong: that the two turning points are the same kind of object on the curve, that the curve continues through the centre rather than ending there, that the textbook $1/r^6$ explosion tracks a label while the deeper spike tracks something else again — a something we have not yet named, and which the asymmetry between the two ends is daring us to explain. Those are claims a critic can attack. That is what it looks like to take a place that had been walled off and let physics back into it.

So we end where Chapter X's collapsing star pointed but could not yet reach. The event horizon is a metric singularity — many events at one place, the ruler collapsed, drawn as a line by a chart that degenerates there exactly as Mercator degenerates at a pole. And the centre of the black hole is the same kind of object *on the curve* — the other end of the same construction, the linked partner of the horizon, a turning point the curve passes through rather than ends at. Its $1/r^6$ "catastrophe," the number the textbooks point to, is the shadow of a label; its reputation as the absolute edge of the world is false outright. What is *not* false is that the deep curvature genuinely spikes there and not at the horizon — and that is the one thing left to explain. The two ends are alike on the curve, and the geometry is lopsided between them. That lopsidedness was not put there by the curve, which is even-handed. It was put there by *how the flat map is made from the curve* — and that, the catastrophe-reading and the manufactured asymmetry both, was in the map.

The curve is even-handed. The map is not. Why the map breaks the symmetry — what second choice it makes, behind the reader's back, to pour the spike onto one end — is the question we have earned and not yet answered.

And there is one question we have deliberately not asked, the one the whole picture is now leaning toward. We found that the black hole's entire structure is a circle — a single smooth curve with two matching turning points, traced out and read with the right eyes. A circle is not a thing that floats in a void; it is drawn on a surface. *What surface?* What is the larger geometry on which this particular circle is the natural curve, and what does it mean that a black hole turns out to be one circle drawn upon it? That is the next installment's question, and it is a good one. We will leave it sitting here, fully formed and unanswered, which is the right place to leave it.

*The geometry in this article is proved, with theorems instead of pictures, in the first papers of a larger programme. One shows that the event horizon is a metric singularity occurring only at the infinite-time boundary of the external universe. A second shows that the central "singularity" of Schwarzschild is the matching turning point of one smooth curve — and that the claim of its being an absolute, impassable edge is false, a single smooth construction that continues through it being enough to refute it, carried across by the same continuation that carries through the horizon; the residual curvature divergence there is conceded, and its being manufactured asymmetrically is traced, in the third paper, to the way the flat description is swept from the curve. A third carries the construction onto the larger surface this article ends by pointing at, and shows there why the deep curvature spikes at the centre and not the horizon. The papers do with proofs what these three articles do with pictures. The pictures, I think, are enough to see it.*

DOCUMENT:
# A Reasonable Cap on Short-Term Rentals Puts Homes Back Within Reach

*By Dana Ruiz, Lakemont City Councilmember*

In February, the council will vote on my proposal to cap entire-home short-term rental permits at 600. I'll explain what it does, what it won't do, and why I think it's worth doing.

First, the scale of change. In 2019, Lakemont had 610 short-term rental listings. Today it has 1,420. Of those, 900 are entire homes, meaning houses and apartments where no one lives full-time. And 640 of those 900 are run by just 70 operators, each with three or more listings. When people say "most hosts are local families earning extra income," that is true of many hosts. It is not true of most of our entire-home listings.

My proposal targets that part of the market. The 520 rooms and accessory units in owner-occupied homes would not be capped at all. If you rent out a basement suite or a spare room, nothing changes for you.

Now let me be straight about rents. They are up 31% in Lakemont since 2019, but they are up 27% across the region, including in towns with almost no short-term rentals. Short-term rentals did not cause most of our rent increase, and a cap will not reverse it. A 2024 state university study of 40 cities found a small but real effect: each 1% increase in listings is associated with roughly a 0.018% increase in rents. Applied to our growth, that suggests short-term rentals added a few percentage points to Lakemont rents. That is real money for families already stretched, but it is not the whole story.

The stronger case is supply. Our rental vacancy rate is 2.1%, which is very tight. My office estimates the cap would return about 300 entire homes to the long-term rental or sales market. In a tight market, 300 homes matter. They are 300 households who don't have to leave town. Our local tenants' group has documented 45 families displaced when landlords converted their homes into short-term rentals. Those are our neighbors.

There are real costs, and I won't pretend otherwise. Short-term rentals generate $3.1 million a year in lodging tax, about 40% of our parks maintenance budget. Fewer entire-home listings means some of that revenue declines. Before the vote, I will bring a plan to cover the gap rather than leave parks to absorb it. The visitors' bureau worries about the summer arts festival, when nearly every room in town is booked. That concern deserves a serious answer, and I am open to a seasonal or festival-period allowance.

Finally, the law. State law since 2025 bars outright bans but allows caps "reasonably related to housing availability." A cap tied to vacancy and displacement data fits that standard. A similar cap in Brookfield is being challenged in court, with a ruling expected this spring, and we should write our ordinance carefully with that in mind.

This is a measured step: protect owner-occupied hosts, limit the commercial conversion of homes, and get several hundred houses back to people who live here. I urge the council to support it.

##

FACTS:
Lakemont has about 94,000 residents and 38,000 housing units. There are currently 1,420 active short-term rental listings, up from 610 in 2019. Of these, 520 are rooms or accessory units in homes where the owner lives, and 900 are entire homes. 640 of the entire-home listings are run by 70 operators who each have three or more listings. Median rent in Lakemont has risen 31% since 2019. Over the same period, median rent rose 27% across the region, including in towns with very few short-term rentals. Lakemont's rental vacancy rate is 2.1%. A 2024 study by the state university looked at 40 cities and found that each 1% increase in STR listings was associated with a 0.018% increase in rents. Councilmember Ruiz's proposal would limit entire-home STR permits to 600, awarded by lottery. STRs in owner-occupied homes would not be capped. Her office estimates that about 300 entire homes would return to the long-term rental or sales market. Lakemont collects $3.1 million a year in lodging tax from STRs. That money currently pays for about 40% of the city's parks maintenance budget. Lakemont has about 1,100 hotel rooms. During the two-week summer arts festival, the visitors' bureau says nearly every hotel room and STR is booked, and that the festival brings in about $14 million in visitor spending. The visitors' bureau opposes the cap. In 2025 the state passed a law that prevents cities from banning STRs outright but allows caps that are "reasonably related to housing availability." A lawsuit challenging a similar cap in the nearby city of Brookfield is in state court, and a ruling is expected in the spring. A local tenants' group supports the cap and has collected stories from 45 families who were displaced when their landlords converted homes to STRs. A group representing STR hosts says most hosts are local families earning supplementary income.

KNOWN COMPLICATIONS:
- The regional comparison weakens the main argument: rents rose 27% across the region, including in towns with few STRs, compared with 31% in Lakemont, and the study's effect size is small. STRs are at most a contributing factor in rent increases.
- Returning 300 units is less than 1% of the housing stock, but it is meaningful in a market with a 2.1% vacancy rate. How this is framed decides whether the op-ed is honest.
- $3.1M in lodging tax pays for 40% of parks maintenance, and the festival depends on STRs during peak season. The opponents' arguments are strong.
- The cap targets entire homes and multi-listing operators, not local families renting rooms, which answers the hosts' argument, and the pending Brookfield lawsuit creates legal risk.

AUTHOR'S ANTICIPATED CRITIQUES:
1. **The 300-home estimate is unsupported.** It comes from the councilmember's own office with no stated method. Owners denied permits might instead leave homes vacant, use them as second homes, rent them as mid-term (30+ day) furnished rentals, or sell them to buyers who don't rent at all. The piece treats the estimate as solid.

2. **The housing impact may be small.** 300 homes is under 1% of Lakemont's 38,000 units. The op-ed doesn't quantify what this would do to the 2.1% vacancy rate or to rents. Using the cited study, cutting listings by about 21% (300 of 1,420) implies a rent reduction of only about 0.4%. The piece avoids making that calculation explicit.

3. **The "few percentage points" figure is a rough extrapolation.** Listings grew about 133%. Multiplying by 0.018 gives about 2.4%, but that applies a cross-city correlation linearly to a single city's very large change. The study also shows association, not causation.

4. **The lottery doesn't target the commercial operators the piece criticizes.** The argument centers on the 70 operators with 640 listings, but a random lottery for 600 permits could eliminate single-listing local families while large operators keep most of theirs. A per-operator limit or priority for primary residents would better match the rhetoric. A reviewer will see this as a gap between argument and policy.

5. **The revenue gap is acknowledged but not solved.** "I will bring a plan" is a promise, not a plan. There is no estimate of how much of the $3.1 million would be lost. Some tax may also shift to hotels, which the piece doesn't mention.

6. **The festival response is vague.** It notes hotel capacity of about 1,100 rooms and a $14 million event but offers only openness to a "seasonal allowance." Readers may wonder whether that exception undermines enforcement or the housing goal.

7. **The legal risk is understated.** The council would vote in February, months before the Brookfield ruling. The piece doesn't explain why the city shouldn't wait, or what happens to permits, lottery losers, and city legal costs if the cap is struck down.

8. **The anecdotal evidence is limited.** The 45 displaced families come from an advocacy group and are self-reported stories, not a systematic count. The piece leans on them emotionally without noting that.

9. **The piece selectively rebuts opponents.** It disputes the hosts' group with data but adopts the tenants' group framing more readily. The 640-of-900 figure supports the point about entire homes, but the hosts' claim may refer to all hosts, where it could be accurate.

10. **The 600 number is not justified.** Nothing explains why 600 rather than 400 or 750, or how that figure is "reasonably related to housing availability." This matters both for persuasion and for the legal standard the piece invokes.

11. **The broader context is missing.** Since regional rents rose 27% without short-term rentals, the main drivers are elsewhere, such as construction, zoning, and demand. A skeptic will ask what else the councilmember is doing about supply. Without that, the cap may look like a symbolic substitute.

FINDINGS:
- The op-ed omits that permits would be awarded by lottery, which contradicts its claim to target large multi-listing operators since a random lottery could deny single-listing local hosts while large operators keep permits.
- The 300-home estimate equals the full permit reduction (900 to 600), implicitly assuming 100% of lost listings return to the long-term housing market, ignoring conversions to 30+ day rentals, second homes, vacancy, or non-rental sales, and no method is given.
- The piece never states the scale of the housing benefit: 300 homes is under 0.8% of 38,000 units and by the piece's own study implies only about a 0.4% rent reduction, with no effect on the 2.1% vacancy rate discussed.
- The claim that STRs raised rents 'a few percentage points' overstates the study, since linear calculation gives about 2.4% and a log-based elasticity calculation gives about 1.5%, and the cross-city association is extrapolated to one city's very large change.
- The line about '300 households who don't have to leave town' overclaims by turning an uncertain unit estimate into a guaranteed outcome for current residents, when sold homes may go to newcomers or second-home buyers.
- The revenue concession ends in a promise ('I will bring a plan') with no estimate of how much of the $3.1M would be lost and no mention of partial offset from hotel stays.
- The festival response is thin, omitting the 1,100 hotel rooms, $14M festival spending, and visitors' bureau opposition, and the proposed seasonal allowance raises unanswered questions about eligibility, enforcement, and whether it readmits the targeted commercial operators.
- Legal risk is understated: compliance with the 'reasonably related' standard is asserted rather than shown, 600 is not justified over other numbers, and the piece doesn't address voting before the Brookfield ruling or what happens if the cap is struck down.
- Evidence is weighed unevenly, giving full weight to 45 anecdotal displacement stories from an advocacy group while narrowing and underselling the hosts' claim that most hosts are local families, which the brief's own numbers may support.
- The piece admits regional rents rose 27% without STRs but never mentions other housing-supply actions, making the cap look like a symbolic substitute.
- The headline 'Puts Homes Back Within Reach' promises more than the body delivers.
- Population and unit totals that would put the 300-home figure in perspective are never used.
- The document ends with a stray '---' and an empty heading that should be removed.

# Memo

**To:** Director of Operations, Brookside Veterinary Group
**From:** [Analyst]
**Date:** 13 November 2026
**Re:** Evening and Saturday hours pilot at 3 clinics in 2027

## Recommendation

**No-go on the pilot as proposed.** Instead, approve a smaller test: Saturday-only hours at one northern-suburbs clinic from April 2027. Expand only if that test meets clear volume targets.

## Why not the full pilot

**1. The volume needed to break even is higher than the evidence supports.**
- Each clinic would add 28 hours a week at about $310 an hour, or $8,680 a week.
- Each urgent visit contributes about $204 ($340 × 60%).
- Each clinic therefore needs about 43 visits a week, or 1.5 per open hour, just to cover staffing.
- Across 3 clinics, that is about 550 visits a month.

The after-hours phone line receives 1,140 calls a month for all 9 clinics. About 730 of those (64%) are treatable urgent cases. However, many of those calls come overnight, on Sundays or on holidays, which the pilot would not cover. Some are repeat calls about the same pet, and not every caller would drive to one of only 3 open clinics. Breaking even would require converting roughly three-quarters of all treatable after-hours calls across the network into pilot visits. That is implausible without substantial new demand, and we have no evidence of that demand. At full scale, the pilot commits about $1.35 million a year in staffing before any vet pay premium.

**2. We cannot staff it reliably.**
- We already have 4 unfilled veterinarian positions.
- 70% of current veterinarians will not work regular evenings.
- Evening recruits expect a 20–25% premium, which raises the break-even volume further.
- The pilot needs about 84 veterinarian-hours a week, roughly two full-time vets. Pulling existing vets onto evening shifts would worsen daytime gaps.

**3. It puts an existing referral stream at risk.**
Valley Animal Emergency sends us about 300 follow-up patients a year, and its owner has signalled she may reconsider this. Losing those referrals would partly offset any gains, and that loss would begin before the pilot proves itself.

## Why not do nothing

The demand signal is real. 72% of clients say they would prefer to come to us for urgent care. A competitor has opened two urgent-care clinics in the past year. If we wait, we risk giving up this market permanently.

## Proposed smaller test

- **Saturday hours, 8 a.m. to 4 p.m., at one northern clinic.** Six of our 9 clinics are in the northern suburbs, so most of our clients are there.
- **Cost:** about $2,500 a week, or roughly $130,000 a year. Break-even is about 12 visits per Saturday.
- **Staffing:** covered by the roughly 30% of vets willing to work extra hours, paid at a premium, with no new hires needed.
- **Before the end of the month:**
  - Log after-hours calls by hour and day.
  - Record each caller's nearest clinic.
- **Before launch:** meet Valley's owner to agree on what cases each side handles. One option is to keep referring overnight and true emergency cases to Valley.
- **Expansion trigger:** if Saturday visits average 15 or more over 3 months, extend to evenings at the same clinic, then to a second site.

This approach tests real demand at about a tenth of the cost and protects the Valley relationship. It also gives us a defensible basis for a larger decision in late 2027.

FACTS:
Brookside's 9 clinics are open from 8 a.m. to 6 p.m., Monday to Friday. Outside those hours, its phone line receives about 1,140 calls a month. Callers are referred to Valley Animal Emergency, a 24-hour emergency hospital about 25 minutes' drive from most Brookside clinics. Its average invoice is $890. A review of a sample of after-hours calls by Brookside's medical director found that about 64% were for problems that were "urgent but not emergencies," such as vomiting, minor wounds or ear infections, that a general-practice clinic could treat. The proposal is to open 3 clinics for an extra 4 hours each weekday evening (6 to 10 p.m.) and 8 hours on Saturday, a total of 28 extra hours a week per clinic. Staffing a clinic for each extra hour (one veterinarian, two technicians and a receptionist) costs about $310. Brookside's average urgent-care visit is expected to generate $340 in revenue, with a contribution margin of 60% after supplies and medicines. Brookside currently has 4 unfilled veterinarian positions. In a staff survey, 70% of current veterinarians said they would not be willing to work regular evening shifts. Recruiters say veterinarians willing to work evenings ask for a pay premium of 20-25%. Valley Animal Emergency refers about 300 patients a year back to Brookside clinics for follow-up care. Its owner has said informally that she would "rethink" this arrangement if Brookside started competing for urgent cases. A competing group has opened two urgent-care animal clinics in the region in the past year. Brookside's client survey found that 72% of clients would prefer to bring their pet to Brookside for urgent care rather than going to an emergency hospital. Brookside's client base is concentrated in the northern suburbs, where 6 of its 9 clinics are located. The proposal has not yet chosen which 3 clinics would open. The extra hours could begin in April 2027 if staffing is in place.

KNOWN COMPLICATIONS:
- Breaking even requires capturing most of the urgent calls. 3 clinics x 28 hours x $310 is about $26k a week (about $1.35M a year), which needs about 128 urgent visits a week at $204 contribution each, compared with about 168 urgent after-hours calls a week in total, a capture rate of about 76%.
- Staffing is the binding constraint: there are already 4 vacancies, 70% of veterinarians will not work evenings, and evening staff need a 20-25% premium, which raises the $310/hour cost and the break-even point.
- The emergency hospital may stop sending its 300 annual referrals, which is a revenue loss the proposal does not count.
- Hours, demand and location: 28 extra hours at all 3 clinics may be more than needed. A smaller pilot (e.g., evenings at fewer clinics, located in the northern suburbs where clients are concentrated) may be better.

AUTHOR'S ANTICIPATED CRITIQUES:
1. **The call data are thin.** The memo treats 1,140 calls and the 64% figure as reliable. However, the medical director's sample size and method are unknown, and calls are not the same as unique patients.
2. **Demand may be underestimated.** The break-even analysis counts only existing callers. It ignores new clients, clients who currently go straight to an emergency hospital without calling, and possible gains from the competitor's market share.
3. **The pay premium effect is not quantified.** The $310 hourly rate covers four staff, but the memo does not estimate the vet's share of it, so the higher break-even figure is asserted rather than calculated.
4. **The Valley referral value is not estimated.** The memo gives no dollar figure for the 300 referrals at risk, so it cannot be weighed against pilot gains.
5. **Ramp-up time is ignored.** New hours typically take months to build volume. Judging the test after 3 months may end it prematurely.
6. **Saturday-only may not be representative.** Weekday evening demand could differ substantially, so the test may not support decisions about evening hours.
7. **Other revenue is excluded.** The memo ignores follow-up visits, client retention and lifetime value generated by urgent visits.
8. **The 72% survey figure is a stated preference.** It may overstate what clients would actually do, especially if it means driving to a different clinic.
9. **Alternatives are not considered.** The memo does not assess telehealth triage, same-day daytime urgent slots, or a partnership with Valley.
10. **Staffing assumptions are untested.** It is unclear whether the roughly 30% of willing vets would actually commit to regular Saturdays.

FINDINGS:
- The memo ends with an empty leftover heading after the horizontal rule and has an unfilled '[Analyst]' placeholder in the From line.
- The staffing plan misreads the survey by treating 70% unwilling to work regular evening shifts as meaning 30% are willing to work extra hours, and the survey says nothing about Saturdays, undermining the 'no new hires needed' claim.
- The smaller test's $2,500/week cost and 12-visit break-even use the base $310/hour rate despite the memo stating test staff would be paid at a premium, which is inconsistent with the memo's own argument that premiums raise break-even.
- Claims that many calls come overnight, on Sundays or holidays, and that some are repeat calls, are stated as fact without supporting data, and the 64% figure comes from a sample review rather than a full count.
- The memo does not explain why the smaller test is more achievable, since its break-even rate of 1.5 visits per hour is the same rate it calls implausible for the full pilot, when the real difference is the share of treatable calls required (about 7% vs about 75%).
- The phrase 'giving up this market permanently' overstates the risk of not acting.
- Valley's $890 average invoice is not used, though it could strengthen the 'why not do nothing' argument and explain the 72% preference figure.
- The memo runs about 560–580 words, slightly over the 450–550 word target.
- The 'before the end of the month' tasks have no assigned owners.
- Two weeks of call logging is too small a sample to drive the go/no-go decision and should instead inform clinic selection.

# Proposal to Members: Converting Three Tennis Courts to Pickleball

*Annual General Meeting, March 2027*

## Recommendation

I recommend that members approve converting 3 tennis courts into 12 dedicated pickleball courts, but only if three conditions are met before construction begins. If any condition cannot be met, the proposal returns to members for a new vote.

## Why the club should act

Pickleball is now the club's only growing source of membership. Pickleball membership grew 40% last year, while tennis membership has fallen about 3% a year for five years. The 2 shared courts are fully booked at peak times, and 48 people are on the waiting list. Admitting all of them would bring in about $17,300 a year in dues (48 × $360). A member-owned club that turns away paying members while its core membership shrinks is putting its future at risk.

## Why we cannot approve it unconditionally

1. **The lease.** Our city lease requires that the facility's "primary use" be tennis. Changing that clause needs a city council vote, which typically takes about four months. If we built first, we could breach the lease.
2. **Noise.** The acoustic consultant measured 61 decibels at the property line. The city's limit after 6 p.m. is 55. The nearest houses are about 40 metres away, and two neighbours have already complained about the current shared courts. The consultant recommends $38,000 of acoustic fencing, but we have no evidence yet that fencing alone will bring us under the limit.
3. **Money.** The conversion costs $96,000 and the fencing $38,000, for a total of $134,000. That would leave $16,000 in our $150,000 reserve, far short of the $90,000 needed to replace the clubhouse roof in 2027.

## The three conditions

- The city council approves an amendment to the primary-use clause.
- The acoustic consultant certifies in writing that the final design will keep noise at or below 55 decibels at the property line.
- The board presents members with a funding plan that keeps at least $90,000 available for the roof. Options include a joining fee for new pickleball members, a capital levy, or phased construction.

## The cost to tennis members

The concerns of long-standing members are fair, and this proposal does hurt tennis at peak times. Tennis courts are 88% booked from 6 to 9 p.m. on weekdays and on weekend mornings, which means peak demand fills about 7 of our 8 courts. Five courts cannot absorb that, even though the remaining courts would become tennis-only once sharing ends. Some members will lose peak-time play. Outside peak hours, courts are only 31% used, so there is room to move some play. Before construction starts, the board would bring a peak-hour booking policy to members for approval. Tennis stays central to who we are. The aim is to make room for a second sport, not to replace the first.

## Timeline

If members approve in March, the board would apply to the city immediately, and a council decision would likely come around July. Construction takes about 6 weeks. It would be scheduled outside March to May so the Oakhurst high school tennis team keeps its weekday afternoon practices.

FACTS:
Oakhurst Racquet Club is a member-owned club with 640 members: 410 tennis members and 230 pickleball members. Tennis membership has fallen by about 3% a year over the past five years. Pickleball membership grew 40% last year. Annual dues are $520 for tennis members and $360 for pickleball members. Pickleball currently shares 2 of the 8 tennis courts, which have pickleball lines painted on them. Those courts are fully booked at peak times, and there is a waiting list of 48 people for pickleball membership. Tennis court use is 88% at peak times (6 to 9 p.m. on weekdays and weekend mornings) and 31% at other times. Several tennis members have said they already struggle to book courts at peak times. Converting 3 tennis courts to 12 dedicated pickleball courts would cost $96,000. The nearest houses are about 40 metres from the proposed pickleball courts. The city's noise ordinance limits noise at the property line to 55 decibels after 6 p.m. An acoustic consultant measured 61 decibels at the property line during a test session and recommended acoustic fencing costing $38,000. Two neighbours have already complained about noise from the current shared courts. The club leases its land from the city. The lease includes a clause stating that the facility's "primary use" must be tennis. Changing that clause requires a vote of the city council, which typically takes about 4 months. The club's reserve fund holds $150,000. The clubhouse roof needs to be replaced in 2027, at an estimated cost of $90,000. The club's board is split. Two long-standing board members say the club's identity is tennis and that the conversion would push out the members who built the club. The treasurer points out that pickleball is the club's only growing source of membership. The Oakhurst high school tennis team uses the club's courts for practice on weekday afternoons from March to May. The proposed construction would take about 6 weeks.

KNOWN COMPLICATIONS:
- Tennis courts are 88% booked at peak times, so taking away 3 of 8 courts would cause a serious peak-time shortage for 410 tennis members, even though tennis use is low at other times.
- The noise level of 61 dB is above the 55 dB evening limit, so the $38k acoustic fence is required, not optional, and neighbours have already complained.
- The total cost ($96k + $38k = $134k) plus the roof ($90k) is $224k, more than the $150k reserve.
- The lease's 'primary use' clause requires a city council vote taking about 4 months, which affects the timeline after a March vote, and work should avoid the high school's spring season.

AUTHOR'S ANTICIPATED CRITIQUES:
1. **The tennis capacity problem is acknowledged, not solved.** Cutting from 8 courts to 5 against about 7 courts of peak demand is a serious loss, and "a booking policy later" is vague. Tennis members pay $520. Losing about 33 of them would wipe out the dues gained from the entire waiting list.
2. **The 88% figure is ambiguous.** The proposal does not say whether peak use includes pickleball play on the 2 shared courts. If it does, the tennis-only demand on the remaining courts may be lower than stated.
3. **The financial case is thin.** There is no net revenue forecast and no figure for maintenance costs. It assumes all 48 people on the waiting list will join. Even then, $17,300 a year against $134,000 means roughly an eight-year payback before any tennis losses.
4. **One year of 40% growth is treated as a trend.** There is no evidence that demand will keep growing or that 12 courts is the right number.
5. **The funding condition is effectively a blank cheque.** Members are asked to approve a project while the board decides later how to pay for it. The proposal names options without costing any of them.
6. **The noise risk may be fatal, not conditional.** A 6-decibel exceedance, existing complaints, and play peaking after 6 p.m. may lead the council to refuse the lease change regardless of fencing.
7. **Alternatives are not considered.** These include converting 2 courts instead of 3, setting time-based tennis and pickleball blocks, quieter equipment rules, or locating the courts farther from the houses.
8. **The lease analysis is shallow.** The proposal does not examine whether 5 tennis courts could still count as "primary use," or why the city included the clause in the first place.
9. **Timeline gaps.** A July decision followed by 6 weeks of construction and possible contractor delays is optimistic. The interaction with the 2027 roof work is not discussed.
10. **Tone and authorship.** "Turns away paying members" is persuasive framing. The proposal also does not say who "I" is or how the split board voted.

FINDINGS:
- The proposal never says which 3 courts are converted, so the actual tennis capacity loss is unclear, and the '8 courts to 5' framing overstates the cut.
- The 61 dB noise reading comes from a test session rather than 12 courts in simultaneous use, so it may understate the noise risk and the fencing may need to handle more than the 6 dB gap shown.
- The proposal ignores whether the high school team can still hold weekday spring practices on 5 tennis courts long term, and its construction-period protection is nearly moot given the July council decision.
- Saying 'tennis stays central' while asking the city to remove tennis as primary use is contradictory; the proposal should explain the amendment wording or get a legal view on whether 5 of 8 courts already satisfies primary use.
- The proposal reframes the board split as opposition from 'long-standing members' and hides that two board members object, and it is unclear who 'I' (the recommender) is.
- It is unclear whether members vote on the later funding plan and booking policy, or what happens if they reject either; only a failed condition triggers a new vote.
- Membership numbers (the 410/230 split and $520 tennis dues) are missing, so members cannot weigh pickleball revenue against tennis attrition, such as the roughly $6,400 a year lost to the existing 3% decline.
- The 88% utilisation figure is ambiguous because the shared courts are partly booked by pickleball, so tennis-only peak demand may be below 7 courts.
- The finances are thin, with roughly a 7.7-year payback on dues alone, and the proposal should lean on long-term membership viability as the stronger rationale.
- One year of growth is weak evidence, and the proposal should rely on the waiting list rather than the 40% figure.
- The $74,000 funding shortfall is left as a blank cheque, and at least one option, such as the joining fee needed per new member, should be costed.
- Alternatives are not considered, and at least converting only 2 courts or siting courts away from the houses should be mentioned.
- The timeline of July plus 6 weeks has no slack, and the clash of roof work with construction and cash flow is not addressed.
- The tone includes mild advocacy such as 'putting its future at risk', and authorship and the board vote are missing.

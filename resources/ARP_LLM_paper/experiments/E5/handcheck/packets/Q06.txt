DOCUMENT:
**MEMORANDUM**

**To:** Provost's Academic Technology Committee, Northgate State University
**From:** [Analyst, Office of Academic Technology]
**Date:** November 13, 2026
**Re:** ProctorEye renewal and expansion (decision due February 28, 2027)

**Recommendation**

Decline ProctorEye's three-year, $720,000 offer to expand full automated proctoring. Instead:

1. Adopt the identity-verification-only service at $35,000 a year, with a non-biometric alternative.
2. Fund a course-redesign program for open-book and project-based assessment.
3. If needed, negotiate a short bridge at current scope that ends no later than June 30, 2027.

**Why not renew and expand**

*The system catches little and costs a lot of faculty time.* In 2025-26, ProctorEye flagged 6,150 of 41,000 exams. Only 308 of those flags (5%) led to a misconduct finding, so 95% of flags required faculty review but produced no finding. At 9 minutes per flag, faculty spent about 920 hours on review. That works out to roughly $680 in contract cost and three hours of faculty time per finding. If flag rates hold at full coverage, which is our assumption rather than vendor data, review time would rise to about 2,300 hours a year. That runs directly against the workload concerns Faculty Senate leaders have raised.

*The errors fall unevenly.* The Office of Institutional Equity found that students with darker skin tones were flagged at 1.9 times the rate of other students. Students registered with Disability Services were flagged at 2.4 times the rate. Expansion would extend these disparities to every online course. It would also increase our exposure to complaints under civil rights and disability law. Two current appeals already challenge whether the flags are reliable.

*The contract would collide with state law.* The new student privacy law takes effect July 1, 2027, early in the proposed term. Webcam face monitoring almost certainly involves biometric data. From that date we must obtain written consent and offer a non-biometric alternative to any student who declines. A three-year commitment to a biometric-dependent system invites expensive retrofitting, and if many students opt out, we would be paying for coverage we cannot use.

**Meeting the accreditation requirement**

The Director of Online Programs is right that our accreditor requires us to verify that the registered student does the work. That requirement concerns identity, not continuous behavioural monitoring. The $35,000 identity-only service addresses it directly. Because a face match is also biometric, we must pair it with a non-biometric option, such as a brief live ID check with staff over video or an in-person check at a campus or partner testing site. Counsel should confirm that both paths satisfy the accreditor and the new law.

**Deterrence**

Faculty want "some deterrent." We have no data on how much ProctorEye deters cheating, so we cannot claim that removing it will raise misconduct. The better deterrent is assessment that is hard to outsource. Peer institutions have moved to open-book exams, projects, and oral components for this reason. We recommend using part of the roughly $205,000 in annual savings to fund instructional-design support and faculty stipends for course redesign, starting with high-enrollment gateway courses.

**Next steps before February 28**

- Ask Legal Counsel to review the privacy law and accreditor language.
- Request ProctorEye's terms for the identity-only service and for a short bridge extension.
- Brief the Faculty Senate and Student Government Association.
- Track misconduct rates and student outcomes for one year after the transition.

##

FACTS:
Northgate uses ProctorEye, an automated remote proctoring service that monitors students through their webcam and microphone during online exams and flags behaviour it considers suspicious. The current contract costs $210,000 a year and covers about 40% of online courses. ProctorEye's renewal offer is a 3-year contract at $240,000 a year, covering all online courses. In the 2025-26 academic year, ProctorEye monitored 41,000 exams and flagged 6,150 of them (15%). Faculty must review every flag. Faculty reported spending an average of 9 minutes reviewing each flag. Of the 6,150 flags, 308 led to a finding of academic misconduct. An internal audit by the Office of Institutional Equity found that students with darker skin tones were flagged at 1.9 times the rate of other students, often for "face not detected." Students registered with Disability Services were flagged at 2.4 times the rate of other students, often for looking away from the screen or making repeated movements. The Student Government Association passed a resolution opposing renewal, calling the system "surveillance." The Director of Online Programs supports renewal. She points out that the university's accreditor requires institutions offering distance education to verify that the student who registers for a course is the one who completes the work. ProctorEye also sells an identity-verification-only service, which checks a photo ID and the student's face once at the start of each exam, for $35,000 a year. Several peer universities have moved to open-book exam formats and project-based assessment in online courses. A new state student privacy law takes effect July 1, 2027. It requires institutions to get written consent before collecting biometric data from students and to provide an alternative that does not use biometrics for any student who declines. Faculty Senate leaders say faculty want "some deterrent" against cheating, but also say the review workload is a burden. Two of last year's misconduct cases are being appealed on the grounds that the flags were unreliable.

KNOWN COMPLICATIONS:
- Only about 5% of flags (308 of 6,150) led to a misconduct finding, while faculty spent roughly 920 hours reviewing flags. The system is mostly producing false alarms.
- Flag rates are much higher for students with darker skin and students with disabilities. That is a fairness and legal risk that would grow if the system is expanded to all courses.
- The accreditor's requirement is about verifying identity, not monitoring behaviour, and the $35k ID-only service would meet it.
- The new privacy law takes effect in July 2027, during a 3-year contract, and requires consent and a non-biometric alternative, which would make running the system university-wide more complicated.

AUTHOR'S ANTICIPATED CRITIQUES:
1. **The deterrence effect is missing from the analysis.** The 308 findings measure detection, not prevention. A reviewer will note that the memo admits it has no deterrence data but still recommends removal. A pilot or comparison of misconduct rates across proctored and unproctored sections would strengthen the case.
2. **The cost-per-finding framing is selective.** Dividing the contract cost by findings ignores undetected cheating and the value of protecting degree credibility. The faculty-time figure also uses an assumed rate of about three hours per finding, without stating salary costs.
3. **The expansion projection is speculative.** Flag rates may differ across course types newly brought into coverage.
4. **The 5% conversion rate is ambiguous.** Some flags may have been valid but not pursued, or resolved informally. The memo treats every unconfirmed flag as a false positive.
5. **The disparity figures lack context.** The memo does not report confidence intervals or flag-to-finding rates by group. It also does not address whether disparities persist after faculty review, which is the stronger equity question.
6. **The legal claims are hedged but load-bearing.** "Almost certainly biometric" and "addresses it directly" rely on interpretations of the privacy law and the accreditor's standard that counsel has not yet confirmed.
7. **Implementation feasibility is thin.** The memo gives no cost or staffing estimate for the non-biometric alternative or the redesign program, and no timeline for redesigning many courses by mid-2027.
8. **Bridge-contract risk is understated.** ProctorEye may not offer a short-term extension at current pricing. The memo does not say when the current contract ends.
9. **Stakeholder views are underweighted.** The memo cites the Student Government Association's resolution only in next steps and does not engage its "surveillance" argument. Faculty who rely on proctored exams in disciplines where open-book formats are hard to use, such as licensure-track courses, are not addressed.
10. **There is no option analysis.** A reviewer may want a side-by-side comparison of all options: full renewal, renewal at current scope, identity-only, live human proctoring, and no proctoring. Each would be shown with cost, legal risk, and equity impact.

FINDINGS:
- The document ends with stray formatting (a `---` and an empty `##` heading) that should be deleted.
- The memo's equity argument relies on face-detection bias against darker-skinned students, but the recommended identity-only service also uses face matching and may fail for the same students, which the memo does not address.
- The memo omits the specific flagging mechanisms from the brief ('face not detected' and looking away/repeated movements), which would make the disparity concrete and strengthen the case for identity-only checking.
- The claim that peer institutions moved to open-book exams, projects, and oral components 'for this reason' goes beyond the brief by adding oral components and attributing motives to peers.
- The deterrence paragraph is one-sided: the lack of data cuts both ways, and the claim that hard-to-outsource assessment is 'the better deterrent' is stated as fact without evidence.
- The ~$205,000 savings figure is measured against the expanded renewal offer rather than current spending (where it is ~$175,000, and less in year one with a bridge period), and the baseline is not stated.
- The ~2,300-hour full-coverage projection carries an unstated second assumption that exam volume scales with course coverage, in addition to flag rates holding.
- The Student Government Association's resolution against renewal is only implied rather than explicitly stated in the equity discussion.
- No cost figure or range is given for the assessment redesign program, making that recommendation less actionable.
- The 'From' line still contains an unfilled placeholder that must be completed before circulation.

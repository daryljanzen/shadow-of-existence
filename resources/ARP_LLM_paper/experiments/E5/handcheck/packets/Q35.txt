# Proposal to the Board of the Corbin Water Authority: Lead Service Line Replacement Strategy, 2027–2037

**Decision requested:** Adopt a replacement strategy in February 2027 so it can be included in the State Revolving Fund (SRF) application due March 15, 2027.

## Recommendation

Adopt a **hybrid strategy**:

- Replace the highest-risk lines first.
- Within each priority group, work block by block wherever the main replacement schedule allows.

Neither staff option works well on its own. Priority-first protects children sooner but costs about $28 million more. Block-by-block is cheaper, but it follows a schedule built for water mains, not for lead exposure.

## Scale of the task

- **Known lead lines:** 9,800.
- **Unknown-material lines:** 6,400. If 35% are lead, as in the 2025 sample, that adds about 2,240 lines.
- **Expected total:** roughly 12,000 lines.
- **Required pace:** about 1,200 lines a year from November 1, 2027 to November 1, 2037.
- **Total cost:** $111 million if all lines are replaced block by block. $139 million if all are replaced one at a time. Both figures are in today's dollars.

## Phased approach

**Phase 1 (2027–2029): sensitive sites and children.**
- Replace the lines at all 31 schools and daycares in the first two years. The cost is about $360,000, which is small for the protection it gives.
- With the county health department, identify homes with children under six and blocks with high rates of elevated blood lead levels. Replace these one at a time where no street work is planned.

**Phase 2 (2027–2032): disadvantaged census tracts.**
- About 3,270 known lead lines are in tracts the state treats as disadvantaged. Lines found among the unknowns in those tracts would add to this.
- Replace these block by block, adjusting the main replacement schedule to reach them early.
- At $9,200–$11,500 per line, this work costs roughly $30–38 million. That closely matches the SRF loan.

**Phase 3 (2030–2037): remaining lines.**
- Replace the remaining lines block by block, following the main schedule.
- Streets the main schedule will not reach by 2034 get standalone replacements, so the 2037 deadline is met.

**Unknown lines.** Inspect all unknown lines by 2029 so the scope and cost are firm early in the program.

## Financing

- **SRF loan.** Directing the full $40 million loan to disadvantaged tracts could earn up to $19.6 million in principal forgiveness. That leaves about $20.4 million to repay, which adds about $2.24 a month to the average bill (3.9% of $58). Because the money will be drawn over several years, no single year's increase should approach the board's 5% limit.
- **Remaining gap.** About $71–99 million is not yet funded. Staff will pursue future SRF rounds, federal grants, and revenue bonds. If the whole gap were financed with debt, bills would rise by about $8–11 a month by 2037. That averages under 2% a year, but it would use a significant share of the headroom under the 5% cap that is also needed for other capital work.
- **Private-side costs.** Owners will pay nothing for the private portion of the line. Charging owners would lower consent rates, and partial replacements do not count toward compliance.

## Property owner consent

The 2025 pilot consent rates (40% for landlords, 85% for owner-occupiers) imply that about 25% of owners will not consent. That is roughly 3,000 lines. This is the biggest risk to compliance. Staff recommend:

- door-to-door outreach and multilingual notices, with follow-up through at least three documented contacts;
- asking City Council to amend the ordinance so that tenant consent is accepted, or so that non-response after documented notice is treated as consent;
- notifying tenants directly when a landlord refuses;
- performing partial replacements only in emergencies, with filters and follow-up testing provided to the household.

## Board actions requested

1. Adopt the hybrid strategy described above.
2. Authorise staff to submit the SRF application by March 15, 2027.
3. Direct staff to complete inspection of all unknown lines by 2029.
4. Request that City Council amend the consent ordinance.
5. Direct staff to report annually on replacements completed, consent rates, costs, and bill impacts.

##

FACTS:
The Corbin Water Authority serves about 92,000 connections. Its inventory identifies 9,800 service lines (the pipes connecting the water main to individual buildings) as lead. Another 6,400 lines are listed as "unknown material." When the authority inspected a sample of unknown lines in 2025, about 35% turned out to be lead. A new federal rule requires the authority to replace all lead service lines within 10 years, starting from a compliance date of November 1, 2027. Replacing lead lines only partway (replacing just the public portion) is not counted as a replacement under the rule. The average cost of a full replacement is $11,500 when lines are replaced one at a time, based on priority. When replacements are done block by block alongside other street work, the average cost falls to $9,200. Staff have proposed two strategies: - Priority-first: replace lines first at homes with young children, daycares and schools, and in neighbourhoods with high rates of elevated blood lead levels in children. - Block-by-block: replace lines street by street, following the authority's existing water main replacement schedule. There are 31 schools and daycares with lead service lines. Part of each service line belongs to the property owner. A city ordinance requires the owner's written consent before the authority can work on private property. About 22% of properties with lead lines are rental properties. In a 2025 pilot, landlords gave consent in 40% of cases, compared with 85% for owner-occupiers. The State Revolving Fund has offered a $40 million loan. 49% of the loan principal would be forgiven for work in areas designated as disadvantaged communities. About one in three known lead lines is in a census tract with a median household income below $45,000, which the state treats as disadvantaged. The authority's finance staff estimate that each $10 million of new debt adds about $1.10 a month to the average residential water bill. The board has a policy of keeping annual rate increases below 5%. The average bill is currently $58 a month. The state funding application is due March 15, 2027 and must include the replacement strategy.

KNOWN COMPLICATIONS:
- The 6,400 unknown lines likely include about 2,240 more lead lines, bringing the total to about 12,040. At $11,500 each, that is about $138.5M, not the $112.7M implied by known lines alone. Identifying unknown materials must be part of the plan.
- Priority-first costs more per line ($11,500 vs $9,200), while block-by-block is cheaper but slower to protect the highest-risk households.
- Consent on rental properties (40%) threatens full replacement in 22% of cases and the rule does not count partial replacements, so this subgroup puts compliance at risk and has equity implications for tenants.
- Funding and rates: the $40M loan's forgiveness applies only in disadvantaged areas, which are only about a third of known lead lines, and borrowing to cover the rest would raise bills (e.g., $100M of debt adds about $11 a month, about 19% of the $58 bill), which conflicts with the 5% annual rate increase limit.

AUTHOR'S ANTICIPATED CRITIQUES:
1. **The unknown-line estimate is fragile.** The 35% rate comes from a sample of unknown size and design. If unknown lines cluster in older neighbourhoods, the true count could differ by hundreds of lines.
2. **There is no inflation adjustment.** Costs are in today's dollars. Ten years of construction inflation could add 20–30% to the total.
3. **The bill-impact figures are not explained.** The $1.10-per-$10-million figure depends on interest rate and loan term, which are not stated. The proposal also does not show how this program fits with other rate pressures under the 5% cap.
4. **The full forgiveness figure may not be reachable.** Known lead lines in disadvantaged tracts cost $30 million at block rates, which is less than $40 million. Reaching the full $19.6 million in forgiveness depends on finding more lead among the unknowns or replacing lines at the higher one-off cost. The proposal also assumes the state applies forgiveness in proportion to spending in eligible areas, which should be confirmed.
5. **The block-by-block savings are unproven.** No data shows how much the main replacement schedule overlaps with lead-line streets. Moving main work forward to reach lead streets may also have costs of its own.
6. **Most of the program is unfunded.** About $70–100 million relies on sources not yet identified.
7. **The consent assumptions are weak.** The pilot rates may not hold at full scale. The proposal does not confirm how the federal rule treats lines where owners refuse consent, and the ordinance change is outside the board's control.
8. **There are equity gaps.** Tenants, who often have lower incomes and young children, have the least protection because they depend on landlord consent. Prioritising by tract income is not the same as prioritising by exposure, so high-risk households outside those tracts may wait years.
9. **Data on children's homes may be hard to obtain.** Identifying homes with young children requires health data-sharing agreements that do not yet exist and that raise privacy issues.
10. **Delivery capacity is not assessed.** The proposal does not show whether contractors and staff can sustain about 1,200 replacements a year.
11. **There are no interim protections.** Households waiting up to ten years are not offered filters, testing, or flushing guidance.
12. **Spending public money on private pipes needs legal review.** The proposal does not address whether ratepayer funds can lawfully be spent on private property, or whether doing so is fair to customers without lead lines.
13. **The timeline is tight.** Adopting in February leaves about six weeks to prepare a detailed application.

FINDINGS:
- The proposal provides no cost estimate for the recommended hybrid strategy, even though that figure drives the funding gap, bill impact, and SRF application.
- The '$28 million more' comparison is overstated because it assumes the priority-first option replaces every line standalone, when it would likely still bundle some work with main replacements.
- The financing assumes the full $40M SRF allocation goes to disadvantaged tracts, but Phase 2 realistically costs about $30M, leaving roughly $10M unaccounted for.
- The $19.6M principal forgiveness is stated as 'up to' without saying what share is realistically expected, despite its weight in the bill calculation.
- The consent shortfall of about 3,000 lines threatens compliance, and all proposed remedies depend on outreach or City Council action with no fallback if the ordinance isn't amended or access is refused.
- The 25% consent-shortfall estimate rests on an unstated assumption that about 22% of lead-line properties are rentals, and a higher rental share would make the shortfall much worse.
- Phase 3 lines on streets the main schedule won't reach by 2034 will need costlier standalone replacement, and their number isn't estimated, which could push the total noticeably toward $139M.
- The proposal doesn't show that staff and contractors can sustain 1,200 lines per year, especially during the 2027–2032 Phase 1 and 2 overlap.
- Phases 1 and 2 are labeled as starting in 2027 although the program begins November 1, 2027, so 2027 effectively covers only two months.
- Inspection of unknown lines runs through 2029 while Phase 2 runs 2027–2032, so unknowns in disadvantaged tracts should be inspected first to fix Phase 2 scope and the SRF draw early.
- The proposal doesn't confirm that the $111M and $139M totals include the private-side portion of each line, which owners won't pay for.
- Emergency partial replacements can temporarily raise lead levels, and the plan lacks a commitment to complete the private side within a set period once consent is obtained.
- The schools cost estimate of about $11,600 per site assumes residential-sized lines, though commercial or institutional lines are often larger and more expensive.

QUESTION:
What effect did the Route 7 fare cut have on ridership and fare revenue in its first quarter, and what does it imply for further fare reductions?

DATA:
Bus fare trial. From 1 January 2025 the adult single fare on Route 7 was cut from £2.00 to £1.50. Route 9, a similar urban route run by the same operator, kept its £2.00 fare. Neither route changed frequency or timetable.

Boardings (fare-paying adult singles only), January-March:
| Route | Q1 2024 | Q1 2025 |
| Route 7 | 90,000 | 108,000 |
| Route 9 | 80,000 | 92,000 |

City-wide context: a new park-and-ride site and higher city-centre parking charges took effect in October 2024. The operator reports bus use rising across the city since then.

Onboard survey, Route 7, February 2025 (n = 250 passengers who said they began using the route since January). Previous way of making the trip: walked or cycled 51%; drove 22%; used another bus route 18%; did not make the trip 9%.

Fare revenue on Route 7 = boardings × fare.

KNOWN FLAWS:
F1. [method] Ignores the comparison route: Route 9, with no fare change, grew 15% over the same period (and city-wide bus use was rising). The baseline trend must be removed. CORRECTION: Relative to Route 9, Route 7 grew 1.20/1.15 − 1 = about 4.3% (5 points by difference-in-differences). Implied elasticity is roughly −0.17 to −0.2, not −0.8.
F2. [calculation] 108,000 × £1.50 = £162,000, not £171,000. CORRECTION: Revenue fell from £180,000 to £162,000, a 10% fall. The scheme is far from self-financing.
F3. [misreport] The survey says 22% previously drove; 51% previously walked or cycled. CORRECTION: About one in five new riders switched from the car. Most new riders came from walking/cycling or other bus routes, so congestion benefits are modest.
F4. [inference] Extrapolates a single elasticity estimate linearly far outside the observed range (to a 100% fare cut). The estimate itself is inflated by the baseline error. CORRECTION: The data say nothing reliable about a free fare. Responses to zero fares are not linear extrapolations of small cuts.

EXTENSIONS:
- Cost per additional trip: without the cut, Route 7 would be expected to carry about 90,000 × 1.15 = 103,500 boardings, earning £207,000 at £2.00. The cut added about 4,500 boardings and reduced revenue to £162,000, a cost of about £45,000, or roughly £10 per additional trip.
- Use the survey's 18% 'used another bus route' to note that some growth is displacement from other routes, so the network-wide ridership gain is smaller than the route-level one.
- Ask for monthly data: if the novelty effect is real, the gap between Routes 7 and 9 should narrow over January-March; a longer series (Q2-Q4) would firm up the elasticity.

# Improved analysis: Route 7 fare cut, first quarter 2025

## Summary

- **Ridership.** Route 7 adult single boardings rose **20%** (+18,000). Route 9, which kept its £2.00 fare, rose **15%** over the same period. Most of Route 7's rise therefore reflects background growth, not the fare.
  - Using Route 9 as the comparison, the fare cut added about **4,500–6,000 boardings (+4–6%)**.
  - Allowing for riders switching from Route 9, the best-supported range is about **3,000–6,000 extra trips** across the two routes.
  - In the worst case, the Route 7 gain is about 2,100 and the combined gain is zero.
  - The implied fare elasticity is about **−0.1 to −0.25**, which is low.
  - **Caution:** a 4–6% difference between two routes over one year may be within normal variation. That has not yet been tested.
- **Revenue.** Adult single revenue on Route 7 fell from £180,000 to **£162,000 (−10%)**.
  - Compared with what Route 7 would probably have earned at £2.00, the shortfall is about **£42,000–£50,000 a quarter**. Including fares lost on Route 9, it is up to **£54,000**.
  - The cost consists of about £51,000–£53,000 forgone on riders who would have travelled anyway, partly offset by £3,000–£9,000 from new riders.
- **Further cuts.** At this price sensitivity, each further 10% cut adds roughly 2% to boardings and loses roughly 8% of single-fare revenue.
  - A cut to £1.00 would cost about **£90,000–£100,000 a quarter** against the £2.00 baseline.
  - Few of the added riders would come from cars.
  - Deeper cuts make sense mainly if the aim is affordability for existing riders. That aim has not been stated.

## 1. Ridership

**Estimates using Route 9 as the comparison.** D is the number of Q1 2025 boardings that moved from Route 9 to Route 7 because of the fare.

|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Additive (+12,000), D = 0 | 102,000 | 6,000 (+5.9%) | −0.24 | 6,000 | £42,000 | £42,000 | £7 |
| **Multiplicative (+15%), D = 0** | **103,500** | **4,500 (+4.3%)** | **−0.17** | **4,500** | **£45,000** | **£45,000** | **£10** |
| Survey-anchored, D ≈ 670 (range 510–820)§ | ~104,260 | ~3,740 (+3.6%) | −0.14 | ~3,070 (2,750–3,420) | ~£46,500 | ~£47,900 | ~£16 |

\* Proportional change in boardings ÷ −25%. Log-based figures are about 15% smaller in magnitude.
† Revenue Route 7 would have earned at £2.00, minus the actual £162,000.
‡ Adds the £2.00 per diverted boarding lost on Route 9.
§ This assumes Route 9 switchers make up 18% (13–23%) of the fare-driven gain on Route 7, matching the survey's "another bus route" share. It treats all of those switchers as coming from Route 9. Fare-driven riders may switch more often than other new riders, so this could understate diversion. It is illustrative, not an estimate.

**How diversion works in this table:**

- Each boarding diverted from Route 9 lowers the Route 7 effect by 1.125. It lowers the combined gain by 2.125. It raises the two-route shortfall by £4.25.
- Above D ≈ 2,120 the cut would have reduced combined ridership, which is not credible. A Route 7 effect below about 2,000 would instead suggest the two routes were not on parallel trends.
- Under the additive comparison, the combined gain reaches zero at D = 3,000.
- When the combined gain is zero, the two-route shortfall is always **£54,000**. That equals £0.50 on all 108,000 Route 7 boardings.
- Riders diverted from routes other than Route 9 do not change the comparison. They do reduce the network gain and cause revenue losses elsewhere.

**Stress tests, not estimates.** These assume a different underlying growth rate for Route 7:

- **10% underlying growth:** an effect of about 9,000 (elasticity −0.36) and a £36,000 shortfall.
- **20% underlying growth:** no effect and a £54,000 shortfall.

**Is the effect real?** The estimate rests on one year-on-year growth gap between two routes (20% against 15%). Gaps of 5 points between similar routes may be common in years with no fare change. Until past gaps are checked (Recommendation 1), the positive effect is plausible but unproven.

**Context from outside the brief.** UK studies often cite short-run bus fare elasticities of about −0.3 to −0.5, rising over several years. The estimates here are below that range. One quarter is too short to see the longer-run response.

## 2. Fare revenue (adult singles only)

| | Boardings | Fare | Revenue |
|---|---|---|---|
| Q1 2024 actual | 90,000 | £2.00 | £180,000 |
| Q1 2025 actual | 108,000 | £1.50 | **£162,000 (−10%)** |
| Q1 2025 without the cut (central) | 103,500 | £2.00 | ~£207,000 |

The central net shortfall is **£45,000 (−22%)**. It is made up of two parts:

- **−£51,750 forgone on riders who would have travelled anyway** (103,500 × £0.50). This is a saving to passengers, not a resource cost. It exceeds the whole net shortfall.
- **+£6,750 from fare-driven new riders** (4,500 × £1.50).

**Scaling up.** If Q1 is typical, the cost is about **£170,000–£200,000 a year** on Route 7, and up to about £215,000 including Route 9 losses. Seasonality makes these figures rough.

**Break-even.** For the cut to pay for itself, Route 7 boardings would need to be one-third above the counterfactual. That needs an elasticity of about −1.3 (−1.0 on a log basis), far beyond anything observed.

**Limits on these figures:**

- **Ticket switching.** If day-ticket or pass holders moved to the cheaper single, the single-ticket boardings and the fare effect are overstated. Revenue from other ticket types is not observed.
- **Running costs.** Frequency and timetable were unchanged, so the financial cost is forgone fare revenue. That changes if crowding forces extra capacity.

## 3. Where new riders came from

The survey covered 250 people who began using Route 7 since January. The ± figures are 95% margins assuming simple random sampling. Riders on the same bus trip are clustered, so the true margins are wider.

| Previous way of making the trip | Share | What it means |
|---|---|---|
| Walked or cycled | 51% (±6 points) | No car-use benefit; possibly less active travel |
| Drove | 22% (±5 points) | The only group that reduces car use |
| Another bus route | 18% (±5 points) | Moves boardings and revenue within the network |
| Did not make the trip | 9% (±4 points) | Genuinely new trips; possibly better access |

**How to read this:**

- **Who is in the sample.** The sample mixes fare-driven riders with riders drawn by the background trend who started in January or later. The mix is unknown.
- **Car share.** Trend-driven riders responding to higher parking charges are likely to be disproportionately ex-drivers. The car share among *fare-driven* riders is therefore probably at or below 22%. Sampling error alone allows up to about 27%.
- **Bus switchers.** The 18% share anchors the illustrative diversion row in Section 1. The survey does not say which route switchers came from.
- **What the survey does not cover.** It misses existing riders who now travel more often. Self-reported start dates are imprecise.
- **Sampling.** Onboard surveys over-sample frequent travellers. The shares are closer to shares of boardings than shares of people.

**Funder's cost per car trip removed.** This uses the central £45,000 shortfall and 4,500 fare-driven boardings. About £52,000 of the gross cost is a transfer to existing riders.

| Assumed car share of fare-driven boardings | Cost per car trip removed |
|---|---|
| 27% (upper sampling bound) | ~£37 |
| 22% | ~£45 |
| 15% | ~£67 |
| 10% | ~£100 |

These figures rise if diversion is significant. They count boardings, not car trips, and ignore car occupancy and return journeys. Treat them as illustrative only.

## 4. Implications for further fare reductions

- **Revenue.** At an elasticity of about −0.2, each further 10% cut adds about 2% to boardings and loses about 8% of single-fare revenue.
- **A cut from £1.50 to £1.00:**
  - It would lose about **£45,000–£51,000 more per quarter** on Route 7 at the scenario elasticities, with a central figure of about £48,000. The stress-test range is £41,000–£54,000.
  - Against the £2.00 baseline, the total is about **£90,000–£100,000 a quarter**, roughly **£370,000 a year**, before any Route 9 losses.
  - These figures assume the elasticity holds over a further 33% cut, which is uncertain.
- **Free fares.** The trial cannot estimate demand at a zero fare. The certain cost is all single-fare revenue, currently £162,000 a quarter, plus possible capacity costs.
- **Car use.**
  - At most about one in five new riders previously drove (22%, ±5 points), and probably fewer among fare-driven riders.
  - Background growth on Route 7 was two to three times the fare effect. The brief does not show what caused it.
  - Before money goes into deeper cuts, other car-reduction measures, including parking and park-and-ride policy, should be compared on cost per car trip removed.
- **Affordability.** The cut gives about £52,000 a quarter directly to existing Route 7 riders. Whether a flat cut beats targeted concessions depends on who those riders are.

## 5. Caveats

- **Limited evidence.** There is one quarter of data, one comparison route, no pre-trial trend test, and no measure of statistical uncertainty. Fare responses usually build over one to three years.
- **Choice of trial route.** If Route 7 was chosen because its ridership was weak, part of its recovery would have happened anyway (regression to the mean). That part is currently counted as a fare effect.
- **Exposure to the October changes.** If Route 7 serves the park-and-ride site or the city centre differently from Route 9, the comparison is biased.
- **What is counted.** Boardings are journeys, not people. Only adult singles are covered, so switching between ticket types is invisible.
- **Calendar differences.** Q1 2024 had a leap day and Good Friday; Q1 2025 had neither. These largely cancel in the route comparison but affect the raw year-on-year figures slightly.
- **No stated objective.** Without one, any verdict is conditional.

## 6. Recommendations

1. **Test whether the effect is real.** Compare Route 7 and Route 9 quarterly growth for as many past years as are available. Check whether a 5-point gap is unusual, and whether the routes moved together in October–December 2024. Add total network ridership and one or two other unchanged routes as comparisons.
2. **Keep £1.50 unchanged for 6–12 months.** Do not deepen the cut, extend it to other routes, or pilot free fares yet.
3. **Agree the objective:** car reduction, patronage, or affordability.
4. **Record why Route 7 was chosen,** and check for regression to the mean.
5. **Measure diversion and ticket switching.** Collect all ticket types and total revenue for both routes. Use smartcard or ticket-machine data to identify which routes switchers previously used.
6. **Improve the survey.** Ask new riders why they started and which route they previously used. Run the same survey on Route 9 as a baseline.
7. **Report results as a range** using the scenario table, including the two-route shortfall, rather than a single figure.

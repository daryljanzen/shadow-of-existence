QUESTION:
By how much did the new fertiliser increase wheat yield in the on-farm trial, is it profitable at current prices, and should the farm business adopt it?

DATA:
On-farm fertiliser trial, wheat, clay-loam fields, one season. Four field blocks. In each block, one plot received the new fertiliser and an adjacent plot received standard practice (control). Yields in tonnes per hectare (t/ha):

| Block | Control | New fertiliser |
| 1 | 7.2 | 7.9 |
| 2 | 6.8 | 7.3 |
| 3 | 8.1 | 8.5 |
| 4 | 5.9 | 7.6 |

Field log: in June, the Block 4 control plot was waterlogged for about 10 days after a field drain blocked. The Block 4 fertiliser plot, on slightly higher ground, was not affected. No other plots had problems during the season.

Economics:
- The new fertiliser costs £60/ha more than standard practice.
- Grain price: £180 per tonne.
- Application and other costs are the same for both treatments.

The farm business also has sandy-soil holdings in the east of the county, which were not part of the trial. Sandy soils hold less water and nutrients than clay loam.

KNOWN FLAWS:
F1. [misreport] Block 3's gain was 0.4 t/ha (8.5 − 8.1), below half a tonne, as the draft's own next sentence shows. CORRECTION: Gains ranged from 0.4 to 1.7 t/ha; three of four blocks gained 0.5 t/ha or more.
F2. [omission] Ignores the field-log note that the Block 4 control plot was waterlogged while its paired fertiliser plot was not. This inflates the Block 4 difference (1.7 t/ha) and hence the mean; the draft even reads it as the fertiliser 'helping most where conditions are hardest'. CORRECTION: Excluding Block 4, the mean gain is (0.7 + 0.5 + 0.4) / 3 = 0.53 t/ha, worth £96/ha, for a net gain of about £36/ha rather than £88.50 (or the draft's £108.50).
F3. [calculation] 0.825 × 180 = £148.50, not £168.50. CORRECTION: £148.50/ha gross; net £88.50/ha on all four blocks (about £35,400 on 400 ha, not 'over £43,000').
F4. [inference] Generalises a one-season, four-block clay-loam result to sandy soils that were not tested and that behave differently. CORRECTION: The trial supports a (smaller) estimate for clay loam only. Sandy-soil response is unknown and needs its own trial.

EXTENSIONS:
- Paired t-test: on all four blocks, mean difference 0.825, SD about 0.60, t of about 2.8 on 3 df (p of about 0.07); excluding Block 4, mean 0.53, SD about 0.15, t of about 6.0 on 2 df (p of about 0.03). Note the tiny sample either way.
- Break-even yield gain is £60 / £180 = 0.33 t/ha. Even the conservative 0.53 t/ha clears it, but with little margin. Show how the net gain varies with grain price (e.g. at £140/t the conservative estimate nets only about £15/ha).
- Recommend a multi-season trial with more blocks and randomised plot positions within blocks, so that drainage or elevation cannot systematically favour one treatment.

# Review of the current analysis

## Verification

I recomputed every figure from the raw data. All the arithmetic is correct:

| Quantity | Blocks 1–3 | All four blocks |
|---|---|---|
| Gains (t/ha) | 0.7, 0.5, 0.4 | 0.7, 0.5, 0.4, 1.7 |
| Mean gain | 0.533 | 0.825 |
| SD / SE | 0.153 / 0.088 | 0.597 / 0.298 |
| 95% CI | 0.15 to 0.91 (t₂ = 4.30) | −0.12 to 1.77 (t₃ = 3.18) |
| t vs zero, one-sided p | 6.05, ≈ 0.013 | 2.77, ≈ 0.035 |

The economics also check out:
- break-even gain is 0.333 t/ha;
- central net is +£36/ha;
- net across the CI is −£32 to +£104/ha;
- break-even price is £112.50/t, and break-even premium is £96/ha.

The problems are in the inference and the decision logic.

## Problems

1. **The sensitivity claim contradicts its own table.** The analysis says "the conclusion does not depend on this choice" and that exclusion "does not inflate the evidence". Its own table shows the opposite: with all four blocks the CI includes zero, and with three it does not. Excluding Block 4 is conservative on the *size* of the effect, but it is *anti-conservative on the strength of evidence*. Dropping the most variable pair makes the remaining pairs look tightly consistent. What survives either way is narrower: both point estimates are positive and both exceed break-even. The exclusion is still right, because the waterlogged control biases that gain upward by an unknown amount. But the analysis has to say plainly that statistical significance depends on it.

2. **It tests the wrong question.** The headline p-value tests "gain = 0". The business question is "gain > break-even (0.33 t/ha)". On that test the evidence is much weaker:
   - t = (0.533 − 0.333) / 0.088 ≈ 2.27 on 2 df;
   - one-sided p ≈ 0.08;
   - the lower CI bound (0.15) is below break-even.

   The trial therefore shows the fertiliser probably raises yield. It only weakly shows that it pays. "Probably, modestly" should be stated with that distinction.

3. **The robustness claim on price holds only at the central estimate.** "Robust to sizeable falls in grain price" assumes the gain really is 0.53 t/ha. At the lower CI bound (0.15 t/ha), break-even price would be about £390/t. Price robustness is conditional on the effect estimate, which is the uncertain part.

4. **"More reliable" overstates a 2-df result.** The three-block SD is itself very uncertain: its 95% interval runs from about 0.08 to 0.96. The tight CI rests on three numbers that happen to agree. "More precise, but fragile" is honest.

5. **The recommendation contradicts itself.** The Answers section says year-one area is "a risk-appetite choice". The Recommendation then says "apply to most clay-loam wheat". The analysis should either justify "most" or present the options. The logic is as follows:
   - Learning comes from the randomised control strips, not from the area adopted. Information value therefore does not argue for wide adoption.
   - What argues for wide adoption is expected value: the central estimate is positive and the downside is capped per season.
   - A risk-neutral business adopts widely. A cautious one adopts partially. This should be stated as a choice.

6. **"Control strips will show within a season or two" overpromises.** The question is whether the gain is about 0.53 or below 0.33, a difference of about 0.2 t/ha. To answer it, a season's estimate needs an SE of roughly 0.1 t/ha or better. If strip-pair differences vary with an SD of about 0.3 t/ha, which is plausible across fields and above the trial's 0.15, that needs around 9–10 pairs per season. The plan should give a target number of pairs, not "as many as practical".

7. **The decision rule is still vague.** "Neither season's estimate falls clearly below" is not something two people would apply the same way. Pre-agreed rules need numbers.

8. **Minor points.**
   - The Block 4 fertiliser plot was on higher ground. That shows plot position was not controlled, which bears on placement bias in all blocks, not only Block 4. This was noted, but it should be linked explicitly to the p-values being optimistic.
   - "Loss if the product does nothing" is correctly conditioned. Keep it.

# Improved analysis

## New fertiliser on wheat (clay loam): results and recommendation

### Design and limitations

- The trial covered four clay-loam blocks in one season. Each block had a new-fertiliser plot next to a standard-practice control.
- Randomisation of plot placement is not recorded. In Block 4 the fertiliser plot sat on higher ground, so placement was probably not random everywhere. If it was not, all p-values and intervals below are optimistic.
- One season and one soil type mean the results say nothing about between-year variation or sandy soils.

### Block 4

The Block 4 control plot was waterlogged for about 10 days in June after a drain blocked. The fertiliser plot was not affected. This was logged at the time, and its cause had nothing to do with the treatment. It very likely depressed the control yield (5.9 t/ha, the trial's lowest), which inflates that block's gain (1.7 t/ha) by an unknown amount. **Block 4 is excluded from the main estimate.**

The exclusion matters, and the effect cuts both ways:

| | Mean gain | 95% CI | Above break-even (0.33)? |
|---|---|---|---|
| Blocks 1–3 (main) | 0.53 t/ha | 0.15 to 0.91 | Point estimate yes; CI straddles it |
| All four blocks | 0.83 t/ha | −0.12 to 1.77 | Point estimate yes; CI includes zero |

- **Robust to the choice:** the gain is positive and its point estimate exceeds break-even.
- **Not robust:** statistical significance. It depends on dropping the most variable pair.
- The trial also tells us nothing about performance in wet conditions, because the fertiliser plot was never waterlogged.

| Block | Control | New | Gain (t/ha) |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | 7.2 | 7.9 | 0.7 |
| 2 | 6.8 | 7.3 | 0.5 |
| 3 | 8.1 | 8.5 | 0.4 |
| 4 | 5.9 | 7.6 | (1.7, excluded) |

### Yield effect (Blocks 1–3)

- **Gain: 0.53 t/ha**, about 7% above the control mean of 7.37 t/ha.
- **95% CI: 0.15 to 0.91 t/ha.** This covers one season only and rests on 2 degrees of freedom, so it is fragile.
- **Does it raise yield?** Probably. One-sided p ≈ 0.01 against zero gain.
- **Does it raise yield enough to pay?** The evidence is weak. One-sided p ≈ 0.08 against a gain of 0.33 t/ha, and the lower CI bound is below break-even.
- The interval does not capture season-to-season variation, possible placement bias, or wet-year performance.

### Economics

Assumptions:
- grain at £180/t for both treatments, with no quality or protein difference;
- a £60/ha premium;
- all other costs equal.

| | Value |
|---|---|
| Break-even gain | **0.33 t/ha** |
| Net, central estimate (this trial, one season) | **+£36/ha** |
| Net across the 95% CI | −£32 to +£104/ha |
| Loss if the product does nothing | £60/ha (more if it ever reduces yield or quality) |
| Per 100 ha: central / no-effect | +£3,600 / −£6,000 |
| Break-even grain price | ≈ £113/t at a 0.53 t/ha gain; ≈ £390/t at 0.15 t/ha |
| Break-even premium | ≈ £96/ha at a 0.53 t/ha gain |

If the trial's central estimate holds, the margin is comfortable and survives large price falls. If the true gain is near the low end of the interval, the product loses money at any realistic price. The economics depend on the effect size, not on price.

### Answers

- **How much did yield increase?** By about **0.5 t/ha (≈7%)** on clay loam this season, with a range of roughly 0.15 to 0.9 t/ha. The raw four-block figure (0.83 t/ha) is inflated by the waterlogged control.
- **Is it profitable?** **Probably, but this is not established.** The central estimate is +£36/ha. The trial cannot rule out a gain below the 0.33 t/ha break-even. In a season with no response, the cost is £60/ha, or more if yield or quality suffers, for which there is no evidence so far.
- **Should the business adopt it?**
  - **Clay loam:** yes, as monitored adoption. Fertiliser is bought each season, so this is not a capital commitment. If yield is not harmed, being wrong costs at most one season's premium on the adopted area. The central estimate gives positive expected value, which supports wide adoption for a business that can absorb a year of −£60/ha. A more cautious business can adopt on part of the area. Either way, the learning comes from randomised control strips, not from how much area is adopted.
  - **Sandy soils:** not yet. Lower water and nutrient retention could make the response larger, smaller or absent. This trial gives no reliable guide.

### Recommendation

1. **Clay loam, next season.**
   - Apply the fertiliser on the chosen share of clay-loam wheat. Most of it is defensible on expected value; about half suits a cautious business.
   - Leave **randomly allocated** standard-practice strips, paired with adjacent treated strips. Aim for **at least 10 pairs** across fields, including wetter ones, so the season's SE is around 0.1 t/ha.
   - Record yield, protein and grain quality for every strip.
2. **Pre-agreed decision rule.** Write this down before harvest:
   - **Continue or extend** if the pooled estimate across this trial and next season is above break-even at that season's prices (currently 0.33 t/ha), *and* next season's lower 80% bound is above zero.
   - **Stop or scale back** if the pooled estimate is below break-even, or if quality or protein penalties appear.
   - **Re-evaluate** if grain approaches about £113/t or the premium approaches about £96/ha.
   - This is a management rule, not a significance test.
3. **Sandy soils.** Run a separate randomised paired-strip trial on the eastern holdings, with about 10 pairs, before using the product there. The cost is small compared with getting a farm-wide decision wrong.
4. **Trial management.**
   - Fix the Block 4 drain.
   - Keep logging plot problems as they happen.
   - Set rules for excluding a pair (for example, waterlogging, lodging or pest damage affecting one plot only) before results are seen.
   - Record how plot positions were allocated.

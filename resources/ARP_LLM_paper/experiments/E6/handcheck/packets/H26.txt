QUESTION:
How much did the SMS reminder scheme reduce missed appointments (no-shows) at the GP practice, and what is it worth per quarter?

DATA:
GP practice. From 1 January, an SMS reminder is sent two days before every appointment booked more than 3 days in advance. Appointments booked 3 days ahead or less get no reminder.

| Period | Booking lead time | Appointments | No-shows |
| Oct-Dec (before) | More than 3 days ahead | 2,400 | 312 |
| Oct-Dec (before) | 3 days or less | 3,600 | 228 |
| Jan-Mar (after) | More than 3 days ahead (SMS sent) | 3,200 | 160 |
| Jan-Mar (after) | 3 days or less (no SMS) | 3,200 | 288 |

Totals: before, 6,000 appointments and 540 no-shows; after, 6,400 appointments and 448 no-shows.

Operations log: on 3 days in November the practice phone system failed, so patients could not call to cancel or rebook. The practice attributes 120 of the before-period no-shows in the 'more than 3 days ahead' group to those 3 days. No other disruptions occurred in either period.

Cost per SMS: 4p. The practice estimates that each no-show costs £30 in wasted clinician time.

KNOWN FLAWS:
F1. [calculation] A fall from 9.0% to 7.0% is 2/9 = 22% in relative terms, not 29%. CORRECTION: A 22% relative reduction (on the overall figures).
F2. [method] The overall comparison mixes the treated group with an untreated group whose share of appointments changed (40% of appointments before were booked more than 3 days ahead, 50% after) and whose rate moved for other reasons. The effect should be estimated in the group that received reminders. CORRECTION: Use the 'more than 3 days ahead' group: 13.0% to 5.0%, 8 points × 3,200 = 256 fewer no-shows a quarter on the unadjusted figures (but see the omission flaw).
F3. [omission] Ignores the phone-system failure, which the practice says caused 120 of the before-period no-shows in exactly this group. CORRECTION: Adjusted before rate: (312 − 120) / 2,400 = 8.0%. Reminder effect: 8.0% to 5.0%, 3 points, about 96 fewer no-shows a quarter on 3,200 appointments.
F4. [misreport] The practice's estimate is £30 per no-show, not £50. CORRECTION: At £30: 96 × £30 = about £2,880 per quarter against £128 of SMS costs (about 22 times the cost).

EXTENSIONS:
- Use the short-notice group as a rough control for background change: its rate rose 2.7 points (6.3% to 9.0%). If that background rise also applied to longer-lead bookings, the reminder effect would be larger than 3 points; state the bounds rather than a single figure.
- Check the change in booking mix: the share of appointments booked more than 3 days ahead rose from 40% to 50%. Ask whether reminders, or a change in booking policy, drove this.
- Recommend a cleaner test, e.g. randomising reminders for a few weeks, or comparing with a neighbouring practice without SMS, to separate the reminder effect from seasonal change.

FINAL VERSION:
# Review of the SMS reminder analysis

## Verdict

I recomputed every figure from the source table. The arithmetic holds:
- **Rates:** 8.0%, 5.0%, 6.33% and 9.0%.
- **Central estimate:** −5.67 points, about 181 no-shows, £5,310 net.
- **Proportional estimate:** −6.37 points, about 204 no-shows, £5,990 net.
- **Intervals:** ±1.32, ±1.27 and ±1.83. That gives 3.8–7.5 points, or £3,550–£7,070 net.
- **Outage anchor:** 184 no-shows.
- **Switcher rows:** 400, 600 and 800 switchers.
- **Lower-volume value:** £3,980.

The problems are in interpretation. Two of them would mislead the practice.

## Problems found

**1. "Invisible in the headline figures" is wrong for the figures the practice actually has.**
- The raw totals go from 540/6,000 = **9.0%** to 448/6,400 = **7.0%**, a visible 2-point fall with 92 fewer no-shows.
- The flat 7.0% only appears after removing the outage.
- So staff *will* see a drop, and it is almost all the outage disappearing, not the SMS.
- The real risk is the reverse of what Recommendation 2 warns about: people crediting the SMS with the raw fall, or with the naive 13% → 5% (8-point) comparison in the reminded group.
- The naive figure is about £7,550 net. That is above the central estimate, and it is the most likely wrong answer to the question. It should be named and ruled out.

**2. "At least 400 switchers" is not supported by the data.**
- Short-notice bookings fell by 400. That could be fewer short-notice bookings for any reason, such as demand or slot availability, not patients switching.
- Advance bookings could have grown from new demand.
- The data gives no lower bound. Zero switchers is consistent with it. 800 is not a hard upper bound either.
- So 400/600/800 are scenarios, not a range derived from the data.

**3. "No plausible scenario comes near break-even" ignores sampling error in the stress cases.**
- The intervals are only given for the central estimate.
- 400 switchers: 4.2 ± 1.9 points, so 2.2–6.1.
- 800 switchers: 2.2 ± 2.0 points, so **0.15–4.2**. The bottom of that is about 3.5 no-shows, just under the 4.3 break-even.
- That is the lower edge of an already extreme scenario, so the decision is still sound. But the claim should be "every scenario clears break-even at its point estimate; only the extreme case's lower interval touches it," not "by a wide margin" without qualification.

**4. The headline range mixes in only one kind of uncertainty.**
- "3.0–6.4 points" covers modelling assumptions but not sampling error. The central estimate's own interval reaches 7.5.
- A combined statement would be roughly 2–7.5 points across reasonable assumptions with sampling, with the extreme booking-mix case lower.

**5. Minor inconsistencies.**
- Break-even is "about 5" in the brief but 4.3 in the table.
- The table header says "Net of £128," but the 2,400-volume row uses £96 of SMS cost. The £3,980 figure is right.
- Break-even in points depends on volume.

**6. The proposed trial measures the wrong thing.**
- Testing reminders for short-notice appointments measures a *different* intervention.
- The cleanest test of the existing scheme is to randomly withhold the SMS from a small share of advance bookings for one quarter.

## Improved version

**SMS reminders: effect on missed appointments, January–March**

**Answer in brief**
- **Best estimate:** reminders cut no-shows among reminded appointments by about **5.7 percentage points**, from an expected 10.7% without SMS to an actual 5.0%.
- **Value:** about **180 fewer no-shows a quarter**, worth about **£5,300 net** of the £128 SMS cost, measured as clinician time.
- **Range:**
  - Reasonable assumptions give **3.0–6.4 points** (about £2,750–£6,000 net a quarter).
  - Allowing for sampling error widens this to roughly **2–7.5 points** (about £1,800–£7,100).
  - An extreme booking-mix scenario gives about 2.2 points (£1,450).
  - Annualised, the assumption range is £11,000–£24,000, *if* the effect and Jan–Mar volumes hold all year.
- **Not the answer: "13% → 5%, an 8-point cut".** That comparison credits the SMS with the November phone outage ending. It overstates the effect by about 40% relative to the central estimate.
- **Decision:** the scheme pays for itself at about **4.3 prevented no-shows a quarter** (£128 ÷ £30). Every scenario clears this at its point estimate. Only the lower edge of the extreme scenario's interval reaches it. **Continue the scheme.**

**What staff will see in the headline figures.** Raw practice-wide no-shows fell from **540 (9.0%) to 448 (7.0%)**. Almost all of that fall is the November outage dropping out, not the SMS.
- Excluding the outage, the practice-wide rate was **7.0% in both periods** (420/6,000 and 448/6,400).
- Short-notice no-shows rose sharply, and the SMS appears to have offset that rise.
- On the central estimate, there would have been about 630 no-shows without the SMS.
- Neither the raw 92-fewer figure nor the flat adjusted rate measures the SMS effect.

**Setup.** From 1 January, every appointment booked more than 3 days ahead gets an SMS two days before. Short-notice appointments (3 days or less) do not. They went through the same season and the same practice conditions, so they serve as the comparison group.

**Phone outage adjustment.**
- The practice attributes 120 of the Oct–Dec no-shows in the >3-day group to a 3-day phone failure in November.
- That is 38% of the group's quarter. It is plausible only because a failed cancellation can affect appointments weeks later.
- Excluding them gives a baseline of 192/2,400 = **8.0%**, against 13.0% unadjusted.

How sensitive the result is to this attribution:
- Every 24 no-shows of attribution error moves the baseline, and the effect, by 1 point. That is about £960 a quarter.
- An attribution that is too high understates the effect. One that is too low overstates it.
- The central estimate drops to the simple before/after level (3.0 points) only if the outage caused about **184** no-shows. That would be 59% of the quarter's total from 3 days, which is implausible.
- If the outage appointments would otherwise have been cancelled rather than attended, the baseline is 192/2,280 = 8.4%, a minor change.

**No-show rates**

| Group | Oct–Dec | Jan–Mar | Change |
|---|---|---|---|
| >3 days ahead (SMS from Jan), outage-adjusted | 8.0% (192/2,400) | 5.0% (160/3,200) | −3.0 pts (±1.3) |
| >3 days ahead, unadjusted | 13.0% (312/2,400) | 5.0% | −8.0 pts (not a valid comparison) |
| ≤3 days ahead (no SMS) | 6.3% (228/3,600) | 9.0% (288/3,200) | +2.7 pts (±1.3) |
| All, raw | 9.0% (540/6,000) | 7.0% (448/6,400) | −2.0 pts (mostly the outage) |
| All, outage-adjusted | 7.0% (420/6,000) | 7.0% | 0.0 pts |

*± = approximate 95% interval from sampling variation only, with the outage attribution taken as exact.*

The rise in the unreminded group is far larger than chance would explain (about 4 standard errors). That points to general upward pressure in January–March, such as winter illness. The reminded group would probably have risen too without the SMS.

**Estimated effect per quarter (3,200 reminded appointments, £128 SMS cost, unless stated)**

| Assumed rate without SMS | Effect (95% interval) | No-shows avoided | Net value |
|---|---|---|---|
| Unchanged at 8.0% (simple before/after) | −3.0 pts | ~96 | ~£2,750 |
| Same point rise as short-notice: 10.7% (**central**) | −5.7 pts (3.8–7.5) | ~181 (123–240) | **~£5,310** (£3,550–£7,070) |
| Same proportional rise: 11.4% | −6.4 pts | ~204 | ~£5,990 |
| Scenario: 400 switchers, never miss (on 2,800) | −4.2 pts (2.2–6.1) | ~117 | ~£3,370 |
| Scenario: 800 switchers, never miss (on 2,400), extreme | −2.2 pts (0.15–4.2) | ~53 (4–100) | ~£1,450 (−£20 to £2,900) |
| Central, if advance volume returns to 2,400 (£96 SMS cost) | −5.7 pts | ~136 | ~£3,980 |
| Break-even | −0.13 pts on 3,200 | ~4.3 | £0 |

**Why the central estimate is preferred but not certain.** It allows for the general rise in no-shows, which the simple before/after ignores. Three things could move it:

- **Booking mix (could make it too high).** Short-notice bookings fell by 400 and advance bookings rose by 800, but the data cannot show whether any patients switched.
  - If reliable attenders moved to advance booking, they would flatter the reminded group and worsen the comparison group, with no SMS effect at all.
  - 400 and 800 switchers who never miss are illustrative scenarios, not bounds.
  - The 800 case requires both zero new advance demand and perfect attendance, so it is a stress test, not a likely outcome.
- **Outage effect on short-notice patients (would make it too low).** If the outage also inflated the Oct–Dec short-notice rate, the true background rise, and so the SMS effect, was larger than measured.
- **Different seasonal trends (either direction).** The comparison assumes both groups would have moved in parallel. Winter illness may hit short-notice bookings, often for acute illness, harder. If so, the background rise is overstated for advance bookings and the effect is nearer 3 points.

**Overall:** about 5.7 points is the best single figure. Roughly 3–6.4 points is the defensible range across assumptions, or about 2–7.5 with sampling error. Values below 2 points need an extreme booking-mix shift *and* an unlucky sample. Only that combination approaches break-even.

**Caveats**
- **One quarter on each side, in different seasons.** The annual figures assume the effect and the Jan–Mar booking volumes hold all year. Neither is shown.
- **Value is clinician time, not cash.** It is realised only when patients attend or freed slots are rebooked.
- **The outage attribution is the practice's estimate.** Its uncertainty is not in the intervals.
- **SMS cost assumes one message per appointment.** Any extra is immaterial at 4p.

**Recommendations**
1. **Continue the scheme.** Its cost is trivial against any plausible benefit.
2. **Brief staff and partners on how to read the figures.** The raw fall from 9.0% to 7.0% is mainly the outage ending, not the SMS. The SMS's contribution was to stop a rise, so it will not show up as a separate drop.
3. **Measure the effect directly.** For one quarter, randomly withhold the SMS from a small share of advance bookings (for example 1 in 10). This costs a handful of extra no-shows and replaces inference with measurement.
4. **Report monthly no-show rates split by booking lead time,** with counts. Log disruptions and their attributed no-shows using a consistent, documented rule.
5. **Track individual patients' booking lead times across periods.** This would show whether patients switched to advance booking, which is the largest unresolved uncertainty.
6. **Investigate the rise in short-notice no-shows** (6.3% → 9.0%). It is now the main driver of practice-wide no-shows. A randomised trial of same-day or day-before reminders for this group would test whether extending the scheme pays.
7. **Re-evaluate after 12 months,** comparing the same quarters year on year.

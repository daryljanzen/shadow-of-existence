QUESTION:
What effect did the Route 7 fare cut have on ridership and fare revenue in its first quarter, and what does it imply for further fare reductions?

DATA:
Bus fare trial. From 1 January 2025 the adult single fare on Route 7 was cut from £2.00 to £1.50. Route 9, a similar urban route run by the same operator, kept its £2.00 fare. Neither route changed frequency or timetable.

Boardings (fare-paying adult singles only), January-March:
| Route | Q1 2024 | Q1 2025 |
| Route 7 | 90,000 | 108,000 |
| Route 9 | 80,000 | 92,000 |

City-wide context: a new park-and-ride site and higher city-centre parking charges took effect in October 2024. The operator reports bus use rising across the city since then.

Onboard survey, Route 7, February 2025 (n = 250 passengers who said they began using the route since January). Previous way of making the trip: walked or cycled 51%; drove 22%; used another bus route 18%; did not make the trip 9%.

Fare revenue on Route 7 = boardings × fare.

KNOWN FLAWS:
F1. [method] Ignores the comparison route: Route 9, with no fare change, grew 15% over the same period (and city-wide bus use was rising). The baseline trend must be removed. CORRECTION: Relative to Route 9, Route 7 grew 1.20/1.15 − 1 = about 4.3% (5 points by difference-in-differences). Implied elasticity is roughly −0.17 to −0.2, not −0.8.
F2. [calculation] 108,000 × £1.50 = £162,000, not £171,000. CORRECTION: Revenue fell from £180,000 to £162,000, a 10% fall. The scheme is far from self-financing.
F3. [misreport] The survey says 22% previously drove; 51% previously walked or cycled. CORRECTION: About one in five new riders switched from the car. Most new riders came from walking/cycling or other bus routes, so congestion benefits are modest.
F4. [inference] Extrapolates a single elasticity estimate linearly far outside the observed range (to a 100% fare cut). The estimate itself is inflated by the baseline error. CORRECTION: The data say nothing reliable about a free fare. Responses to zero fares are not linear extrapolations of small cuts.

EXTENSIONS:
- Cost per additional trip: without the cut, Route 7 would be expected to carry about 90,000 × 1.15 = 103,500 boardings, earning £207,000 at £2.00. The cut added about 4,500 boardings and reduced revenue to £162,000, a cost of about £45,000, or roughly £10 per additional trip.
- Use the survey's 18% 'used another bus route' to note that some growth is displacement from other routes, so the network-wide ridership gain is smaller than the route-level one.
- Ask for monthly data: if the novelty effect is real, the gap between Routes 7 and 9 should narrow over January-March; a longer series (Q2-Q4) would firm up the elasticity.

FINAL VERSION:
# Route 7 Fare Cut: First-Quarter Evaluation

## Direct answer

- **Ridership:** The fare cut added roughly **4–6% to boardings**, about 4,500–6,000 a quarter, and possibly less. Most of Route 7's 20% rise, roughly 13–15 percentage points, would have happened without the cut.
- **Revenue:** Fare revenue was about **21% (£42,000–£45,000) lower** than Route 7 would probably have earned at £2.00.
- **Further cuts:** Current evidence gives **no case for deeper cuts, wider roll-out or a free-fare pilot**. Revisit after 12 months of data.

## What changed

On 1 January 2025 the adult single fare on Route 7 fell from £2.00 to £1.50, a 25% cut. Frequency and timetable stayed the same.

Route 9 serves as the comparison route. It is a similar urban route run by the same operator, kept its £2.00 fare and had no service changes. Comparing January–March 2025 with January–March 2024 removes seasonal effects.

## Ridership

Bus use was already rising across the city before the cut. In October 2024 a park-and-ride site opened and city-centre parking charges rose, and other factors may also have contributed. Route 9 shows the size of that background growth.

| | Q1 2024 | Q1 2025 | Change |
|---|---|---|---|
| Route 9 (no fare change) | 80,000 | 92,000 | +12,000 (+15.0%) |
| Route 7 (fare cut) | 90,000 | 108,000 | +18,000 (+20.0%) |

Route 9's growth can be applied to Route 7 in two ways. The proportional method is preferred because the routes start from different bases.

| Assumption | Route 7 without cut | Fare effect | Elasticity (arc) | Elasticity (simple) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Same percentage growth (central)** | 103,500 | ≈ 4,500 (+4.3%) | −0.15 | −0.17 |
| Same absolute growth (sensitivity) | 102,000 | ≈ 6,000 (+5.9%) | −0.20 | −0.24 |

The fare effect is about a quarter to a third of the raw 18,000 increase.

The implied elasticity is about −0.15 to −0.24. The standard UK review gives a short-run bus fare elasticity of about −0.4 (Balcombe et al., 2004, TRL Report TRL593). This suggests a weak fare response, though one quarter of data cannot establish it firmly.

### What could bias this estimate

- **Route 9 may have lost riders to Route 7.** 18% of new Route 7 riders previously used another bus route. If some came from Route 9, Route 9's growth understates the background trend, so the fare effect is overstated. For example, if 1,000 boardings moved from Route 9, its underlying growth would be about 16%, and the central fare effect would fall to about 3,400 boardings.
- **Parallel trends are untested.** The method assumes both routes would have grown alike without the cut, and earlier quarters are needed to check this. Q4 2024 against Q4 2023 is especially useful because it falls after the October measures but before the fare cut.
- **The routes may be exposed differently to the October measures.** If park-and-ride feeds Route 7 more than Route 9, the fare effect is smaller than estimated. If it feeds Route 9 more, the effect is larger.
- **Riders may have switched ticket types.** The data covers adult singles only. If some passengers moved from returns, day tickets or passes to the cheaper single, part of the rise is switching, not new travel.
- **Statistical uncertainty is unquantified.** The range above reflects modelling choices only. With one quarter and one comparison route, the true uncertainty is probably wider.

## Revenue

| | Boardings | Fare | Revenue |
|---|---|---|---|
| Route 7, Q1 2024 | 90,000 | £2.00 | £180,000 |
| Route 7, Q1 2025 (actual) | 108,000 | £1.50 | **£162,000** |
| Route 7, Q1 2025 without cut (central) | 103,500 | £2.00 | £207,000 |
| Route 7, Q1 2025 without cut (sensitivity) | 102,000 | £2.00 | £204,000 |

- **Against last year:** Actual revenue was 10% below Q1 2024.
- **Against the right benchmark:** The relevant comparison is what Route 7 would have earned without the cut. On that basis the cut cost **about £42,000–£45,000 in the quarter (about 21%)**.
- **How the loss arises (central case):** Riders who would have travelled anyway paid £0.50 less each, which cost £51,750. New riders brought in £6,750 (4,500 × £1.50). The net loss is £45,000.
- **Annual cost:** If Q1 is typical, the cut costs **indicatively £170,000–£180,000 a year in forgone fare revenue**. Seasonal variation could move this figure.
- **Not self-financing:** Demand is strongly inelastic, so the fare cut does not pay for itself.

**Fiscal cost versus social cost.** The figures above are the cost to the operator or funder. Most of that money is not lost to society. About £51,000–£52,000 a quarter is a saving to existing passengers, which is an affordability benefit.

Service levels did not change. Assuming there is no crowding, the cut adds no operating costs. The cost to society is therefore much smaller than the fiscal cost and is mainly the cost of raising replacement funding.

**The fiscal cost may be larger than shown:**

- **Other ticket types.** Passengers switching from returns, day tickets or passes would move part of the revenue loss onto ticket types not shown here.
- **Other routes.** The 18% of new riders who came from other routes add no riders to the network and may reduce revenue elsewhere.

## Who are the new riders?

In February 2025, 250 Route 7 passengers who had started using the route since January were asked how they previously made the trip. Each share is accurate to roughly ±5–6 percentage points.

| Previous way of travelling | Share | What it means |
|---|---|---|
| Walked or cycled | 51% | No congestion or emissions benefit; may reduce physical activity |
| Drove | 22% (about 17–27%) | The source of any car-trip reduction |
| Used another bus route | 18% | No gain in riders across the network |
| Did not make the trip | 9% | Genuinely new travel; an accessibility benefit |

The survey has three main limits:

- **It mixes fare-induced riders with other new riders.** Most new riders would have come anyway, and the survey cannot separate the two groups. Former drivers may be especially likely to be responding to park-and-ride or parking charges rather than to the fare.
- **It counts people, not boardings.** Frequent riders make more boardings but appear only once in the survey, so applying survey shares to boardings is approximate.
- **Its coverage is narrow.** It is a single self-reported sample of people on board at the time, covering only those who started in the first four to eight weeks.

**Car trips removed.** Applying the 22% share to the fare effect gives a rough estimate of **about 1,000–1,300 car trips a quarter**. This is probably on the high side if former drivers were mainly responding to the October measures. In fiscal terms, it means **roughly £30–£45 of forgone fare revenue per car trip removed**. That is a cost to the funder, not the cost to society.

## Implications for further cuts

- **This data cannot predict the effect of a free fare.** An elasticity measured from one 25% cut applies only near that price. Even a straight-line extrapolation would suggest only about +15–24% ridership for a free fare. Removing payment altogether also changes behaviour in ways fare-cut elasticities do not capture, such as faster boarding, no cash handling and the psychological pull of a zero price.
- **Deeper cuts would almost certainly reduce revenue further** for modest ridership gains, if demand stays as inelastic as observed.
- **Background growth was two to three times the fare effect.** Route 9 grew 15% with no fare change. The data does not show what drove that growth or how much came from people leaving their cars, so it cannot say which other measures would best reduce car use.

## Caveats

- **One quarter is a short period.** Long-run elasticities are usually larger, so the fare effect may grow. Novelty effects could also fade.
- **The comparison rests on one route.** That route may have lost riders to Route 7, and parallel trends are untested.
- **The results depend on modelling choices** between proportional and absolute growth. Statistical error is unquantified and probably wide.
- **Only adult single fares are observed,** so total passengers and total revenue are unknown.
- **The annual figure assumes Q1 is representative** of the year.

## Recommendations

1. **Do not extend the £1.50 fare to other routes, cut further or start a free-fare pilot on current evidence.** One quarter shows a small ridership gain at a substantial fiscal cost, with limited car-trip reduction. Revisit after 12 months of comparison data.
2. **Decide whether to keep the Route 7 cut on explicit cost-benefit grounds.** The fiscal cost is indicatively £170,000–£180,000 a year. Set against it:
   - the affordability gain to existing riders, which accounts for most of the forgone revenue;
   - the modest ridership gain;
   - new trips by people who previously did not travel (9% of new riders);
   - any equity goals.

   The case should not rest on self-financing or large congestion benefits, because the data supports neither.
3. **Monitor against Route 9 for at least a full year,** with monthly boardings for both routes.
4. **Test the comparison.** Obtain earlier quarters for both routes, especially Q4 2024 against Q4 2023 and quarters before October 2024.
5. **Collect boardings and revenue for all ticket types** on Routes 7 and 9 and neighbouring routes. This will reveal ticket-type switching and riders moving between routes.
6. **Redesign the survey.** Ask why respondents started using the route, which route they used before and how often they travel. Run the same survey on Route 9.

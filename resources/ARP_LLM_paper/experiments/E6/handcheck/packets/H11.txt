QUESTION:
What is the own-price elasticity of demand for the chain's large latte, what did the September price rise do to weekly latte revenue, and should the price be raised again to £4.00?

DATA:
Six-store coffee chain. On 1 September the price of a large latte rose from £3.20 to £3.60. No other prices changed. Compare the 4 weeks of August (baseline) with the 8 weeks of September-October. Use simple percentage changes from the August baseline (not midpoint/arc formulas).

| Product | Aug: avg units per week | Sep-Oct: avg units per week | Price |
| Large latte, all 6 stores | 9,000 | 8,100 | £3.20 then £3.60 |
| Medium latte, all 6 stores | 7,000 | 7,700 | £2.90 throughout |

Operations log: Store 4, which normally sells about 1,500 large lattes and about 1,150 medium lattes a week, was closed for refurbishment for 2 of the 8 weeks in October. Its zero-sales weeks are included in the Sep-Oct averages above. All other stores traded normally throughout.

Ingredient and serving costs are about the same for both sizes, so finance will judge the change mainly on revenue across both latte sizes. No price above £3.60 has ever been charged at any store.

KNOWN FLAWS:
F1. [calculation] The percentage change is computed on the new price instead of the August baseline the brief specifies. CORRECTION: £0.40 / £3.20 = 12.5%. The elasticity on the draft's own volume figure is −10% / 12.5% = −0.80, not −0.90.
F2. [omission] Ignores Store 4's two-week closure, which removed about 3,000 large lattes (375 a week on average) from the Sep-Oct figure. The draft's caveat even assumes all stores traded normally. CORRECTION: Closure-adjusted Sep-Oct volume is about 8,100 + 375 = 8,475 a week, a 5.8% fall. Elasticity is about −0.47 and large-latte revenue is about £30,510 a week (+5.9%), not +1.25%.
F3. [misreport] The data shows medium latte sales rose from 7,000 to 7,700 a week (+10%), or about 7,990 (+14%) after adjusting for Store 4. This is clear evidence of trading down. CORRECTION: Medium-latte revenue rose from £20,300 to £22,330 a week (about £23,160 adjusted). Total latte revenue rose from £49,100 to £51,490 (raw), and part of the 'lost' large volume moved to medium.
F4. [inference] Extrapolates a single local elasticity estimate to a price never charged. Elasticity typically rises as price increases, and the substitution to medium makes the response larger than the large-latte figure suggests. CORRECTION: The data cannot predict the response at £4.00. A trial at a subset of stores, with comparison stores, would be needed.

EXTENSIONS:
- Compute a cross-price elasticity of medium lattes with respect to the large-latte price: roughly +10% / 12.5% = +0.8 raw, or about +1.1 closure-adjusted. This quantifies cannibalisation and shows the combined latte category is what matters.
- Repeat the analysis excluding Store 4 entirely (five stores, both periods) as a cleaner robustness check than imputing its missing weeks; this requires store-level weekly data.
- Use the arc (midpoint) elasticity as a sensitivity check, since the price change is large: on the adjusted volumes (−6.0% / 11.8%) it is about −0.51, close to the simple-percentage estimate.

FINAL VERSION:
# Large latte price rise: elasticity, revenue and the £4.00 question

## Answers

- **Elasticity:** The simple before/after figure is **−0.47**: a −5.8% change in large units divided by a +12.5% price change, on a normal-trading basis. It understates the response because it ignores underlying autumn growth. After allowing for that growth, the elasticity is **at least −0.68** in magnitude, which is inelastic. That lowest figure applies if every lost large-latte buyer switched to a medium. If many left instead, it could be **−1.1 to −1.4**, which is elastic.
- **Revenue:** On a normal-trading basis, weekly revenue across both latte sizes rose **£4,574 (+9.3%)**. **At most about £3,150 of that came from the price rise.** The rise added revenue only if underlying growth was below about 9%. That is the same as at least a quarter of lost large buyers switching to a medium. The current data cannot confirm this, but last year's sales can.
- **£4.00:** **Do not roll it out.** It pays off only under the more favourable assumptions, and no store has ever charged it. Check last year's seasonality first. Then pilot £4.00 in 2–3 stores and compare them with control stores.

## 1. Price change

The large latte rose from £3.20 to £3.60, which is **+12.5%**. No other prices changed.

## 2. Correcting for the Store 4 closure

Store 4 had two zero-sales weeks inside the 8-week averages. Spreading its normal sales for those two weeks over 8 weeks gives:
- Large: 1,500 × 2 ÷ 8 = **375 a week**
- Medium: 1,150 × 2 ÷ 8 = **287.5 a week**

| Avg units/week | August | Sep–Oct reported | Sep–Oct normal trading | Change |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Large | 9,000 | 8,100 | **8,475** | −525 (**−5.8%**) |
| Medium | 7,000 | 7,700 | **7,987.5** | +987.5 (**+14.1%**) |
| Total | 16,000 | 15,800 | 16,462.5 | +462.5 (+2.9%) |

Without the correction, the large-latte fall looks like −10% rather than −5.8%, and the elasticity would wrongly appear as −0.80.

Store 4's normal volumes are pre-rise figures. Rescaling both sizes to post-rise levels changes the results below only slightly. For example, the revenue ceiling moves from £3,154 to £3,136.

## 3. What the data can and cannot tell us

Total latte volume rose 2.9% even though no latte got cheaper. So some underlying autumn growth was present. The data cannot say how much, because there are no control stores and no prior-year comparison.

If both sizes would have grown at the same rate, that one growth rate determines everything else:

| Underlying growth | Lost large sales | Share who switched to medium | Elasticity* | Both-size revenue effect of price rise |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 2.9% (minimum) | 785 | 100% | −0.68 | **+£3,154** |
| 6% | 1,065 | 53% | −0.89 | +£1,628 |
| 9.3% (break-even) | 1,363 | 25% | −1.11 | £0 |
| 14.1% (maximum) | 1,795 | 0% | −1.40 | **−£2,353** |

*Elasticity is measured against the large sales expected at that growth rate without the price rise.

The data set the bounds on growth:
- **Below 2.9%** is impossible here. It would mean more customers switched to medium than stopped buying large, so the price rise created cups.
- **Above 14.1%** is also ruled out. It would mean medium sales fell against trend, even though medium became relatively cheaper.

**Trading down:** Medium sales rose 14.1% at an unchanged price, while the price gap between sizes widened from £0.30 to £0.70. That points to *some* trading down. It does not show how much, so the data cannot tell switchers from customers who left.

## 4. Weekly revenue

**Observed change (normal-trading basis):**

| | August | Sep–Oct | Change |
|---|---|---|---|
| Large | £28,800 | 8,475 × £3.60 = £30,510 | +£1,710 (+5.9%) |
| Medium | £20,300 | 7,987.5 × £2.90 = £23,164 | +£2,864 (+14.1%) |
| **Both sizes** | **£49,100** | **£53,674** | **+£4,574 (+9.3%)** |

The reported figures, which still include the closure, show +£2,390 for both sizes. Do not use those.

**Price effect:** This is the observed change minus whatever would have happened anyway. It equals £53,674 − £49,100 × (1 + underlying growth).
- It is **at most about +£3,150 a week**.
- It is positive if underlying growth was below 9.3%, and negative above that.
- A 9%+ autumn lift is plausible for a coffee chain coming out of the August holiday lull, so the negative case cannot be ruled out.
- These figures should not be annualised.

**This can be checked now.** Take last year's August versus September–October unit sales for each size, from a period with no price changes. That gives underlying growth directly and shows which row of the section 3 table applies.

## 5. Should the price go to £4.00?

Moving from £3.60 to £4.00 is a further **+11.1%**. At current volumes it adds £3,390 a week before any volume loss. Each lost large sale then costs:
- **£4.00** if the customer leaves
- **£1.10** if the customer switches to a medium

Applying each scenario's elasticity and switching share to a £4.00 price:

| Scenario (from section 3) | Large units lost | Weekly revenue change, both sizes |
|---|---|---|
| Growth 2.9%: e −0.68, all switch | ~640 | **+£2,690** |
| Growth 6%: e −0.89, 53% switch | ~840 | +£1,330 |
| Growth 9.3%: e −1.11, 25% switch | ~1,045 | ≈ £0 |
| Growth 14.1%: e −1.40, none switch | ~1,315 | **−£1,875** |

These figures are probably **optimistic**, for three reasons:
1. **£4.00 has never been charged.** Demand often becomes more elastic at higher prices and at round-number thresholds.
2. **The size gap would widen to £1.10.** More trading down is likely, which makes large-latte demand more elastic. Switchers still bring in £2.90 each. Customers who leave cost £4.00 each, plus any food or other drinks they would have bought.
3. **The data are thin.** The baseline is only 4 weeks, the comparison spans a seasonal change, and there are no control stores.

## Recommendation

1. **Check last year's August-to-autumn seasonality** for both sizes.
   - If underlying growth was below about 9%, the September rise paid off: keep £3.60.
   - If it was well above 9%, the rise probably cost revenue: consider testing a lower large price in some stores.
2. **Do not move to £4.00 across the chain.** If the seasonality check is favourable, **pilot £4.00 in 2–3 stores for 8 weeks** and keep the other stores at £3.60 as controls. The pilot would run through Christmas, so compare pilot stores with the control stores over the same weeks, not with their own November figures.
3. **Track large units, medium units and total transactions by store.** The transaction count is what separates customers who switched from customers who left.
4. **Roll out only if both-size revenue rises relative to the controls** and transactions hold up.

QUESTION:
What is the own-price elasticity of demand for the chain's large latte, what did the September price rise do to weekly latte revenue, and should the price be raised again to £4.00?

DATA:
Six-store coffee chain. On 1 September the price of a large latte rose from £3.20 to £3.60. No other prices changed. Compare the 4 weeks of August (baseline) with the 8 weeks of September-October. Use simple percentage changes from the August baseline (not midpoint/arc formulas).

| Product | Aug: avg units per week | Sep-Oct: avg units per week | Price |
| Large latte, all 6 stores | 9,000 | 8,100 | £3.20 then £3.60 |
| Medium latte, all 6 stores | 7,000 | 7,700 | £2.90 throughout |

Operations log: Store 4, which normally sells about 1,500 large lattes and about 1,150 medium lattes a week, was closed for refurbishment for 2 of the 8 weeks in October. Its zero-sales weeks are included in the Sep-Oct averages above. All other stores traded normally throughout.

Ingredient and serving costs are about the same for both sizes, so finance will judge the change mainly on revenue across both latte sizes. No price above £3.60 has ever been charged at any store.

KNOWN FLAWS:
F1. [calculation] The percentage change is computed on the new price instead of the August baseline the brief specifies. CORRECTION: £0.40 / £3.20 = 12.5%. The elasticity on the draft's own volume figure is −10% / 12.5% = −0.80, not −0.90.
F2. [omission] Ignores Store 4's two-week closure, which removed about 3,000 large lattes (375 a week on average) from the Sep-Oct figure. The draft's caveat even assumes all stores traded normally. CORRECTION: Closure-adjusted Sep-Oct volume is about 8,100 + 375 = 8,475 a week, a 5.8% fall. Elasticity is about −0.47 and large-latte revenue is about £30,510 a week (+5.9%), not +1.25%.
F3. [misreport] The data shows medium latte sales rose from 7,000 to 7,700 a week (+10%), or about 7,990 (+14%) after adjusting for Store 4. This is clear evidence of trading down. CORRECTION: Medium-latte revenue rose from £20,300 to £22,330 a week (about £23,160 adjusted). Total latte revenue rose from £49,100 to £51,490 (raw), and part of the 'lost' large volume moved to medium.
F4. [inference] Extrapolates a single local elasticity estimate to a price never charged. Elasticity typically rises as price increases, and the substitution to medium makes the response larger than the large-latte figure suggests. CORRECTION: The data cannot predict the response at £4.00. A trial at a subset of stores, with comparison stores, would be needed.

EXTENSIONS:
- Compute a cross-price elasticity of medium lattes with respect to the large-latte price: roughly +10% / 12.5% = +0.8 raw, or about +1.1 closure-adjusted. This quantifies cannibalisation and shows the combined latte category is what matters.
- Repeat the analysis excluding Store 4 entirely (five stores, both periods) as a cleaner robustness check than imputing its missing weeks; this requires store-level weekly data.
- Use the arc (midpoint) elasticity as a sensitivity check, since the price change is large: on the adjusted volumes (−6.0% / 11.8%) it is about −0.51, close to the simple-percentage estimate.

FINAL VERSION:
# Review of the large-latte price analysis (sixth pass)

## Verdict on the fifth-pass draft

I re-checked the arithmetic. The adjustment figures, the revenue tables, the decomposition, the break-even formula and all three £4.00 scenarios are correct to within rounding.

There is one significant reasoning flaw, plus two smaller issues.

### 1. The recapture bound and the seasonality caveat are treated separately, but they are one problem

The draft rules out Store 4 recapture below about 71% because, below that, total latte cups rise. It then flags seasonality as a separate risk. That logic is circular. Rising total cups is exactly what a seasonal or other demand increase would produce. "Low recapture plus about 3% demand growth" fits the data as well as "high recapture plus no demand growth."

Handling both together gives a better result in two ways.

**A lower bound that survives both problems.** The large latte's share of all lattes fell:
- August: 9,000 ÷ 16,000 = **56.25%**
- After the rise: **51.27%** at every recapture rate, because Store 4's size mix matches the chain's (8,100/15,800 = 8,452/16,487 = 0.5127)

Suppose two things:
- any non-price demand shift lifted both sizes in proportion, and
- the price rise did not create new latte cups (diversion of 100% or less).

Then large-latte volume fell at least 1 − 0.5127/0.5625 = **8.9% because of price**. That makes the elasticity **−0.71 or more negative**, whatever the recapture rate and whatever the proportional seasonal shift. This is the most robust number in the dataset. The draft reached −0.71 only by assuming no seasonality.

**An upper bound, and a weaker revenue claim.** The price rise should not reduce medium sales. So a proportional demand lift can be at most what the medium's growth allows: +10% at full recapture, +14.8% at zero.

Taking that maximum lift gives:
- a counterfactual large-latte volume of 9,900 (at full recapture), against 8,100 actually sold
- a fall of 18.2%, so an elasticity of **−1.45**
- zero trading down
- a combined revenue effect of about **−£2,500 a week** (51,490 − 49,100 × 1.10)

That extreme is implausible, because the two sizes are close substitutes. But it means the draft's claim that the rise was "positive under every closure assumption" holds only under its no-demand-shift assumption. The revenue gain is not established. It is the likely outcome, not a certain one.

### 2. Minor: the 70.9% figure comes from linear interpolation

Re-estimating Store 4's counterfactual sales from 64,800 ÷ (46 + 2r) puts the exact bound at **70%** recapture. The elasticity at the bound is unchanged at −0.71. Other figures move by fewer than 5 cups.

### 3. The answer is buried

## Revised analysis

### Answers in brief

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| Own-price elasticity of the large latte | **About −0.75** (range −0.71 to −0.80) if nothing but price changed. Robust floor of **−0.71**. Raw, closure-contaminated figure −0.80. **Inelastic** on the evidence. |
| Effect on weekly latte revenue | Measured: **+£2,390 (+4.9%)**, closure included. Price effect alone: **about +£2,400 to +£3,000** if there was no seasonal shift. Unverified until last year's data is checked. |
| Raise to £4.00? | **Not yet.** It probably raises revenue if September's behaviour repeats. But £4.00 has never been charged, and the current estimate depends on an untested seasonality assumption. Check the data, then test. |

### The data and the closure
- **Price change:** the large latte rose from **£3.20 to £3.60 (+12.5%)** on 1 September. No other prices changed. The baseline is the August weekly average. All changes are simple percentages from that baseline.
- **Sales:** September–October totals were 64,800 large and 61,600 medium lattes.
- **Trading weeks:** these were sold over **46 trading store-weeks, not 48**, because Store 4 was shut for 2 weeks.
- **Store 4 is typical:** its normal sales (1,500 large, 1,150 medium) match the August per-store averages (1,500 and 1,167).

How much to add back depends on the **recapture rate (r)**: the share of Store 4's customers who bought at another of the chain's stores during the closure.

| | Aug | r = 100% | r = 70% | r = 0% |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Large / week | 9,000 | 8,100 (−10.0%) | 8,200 (−8.9%) | 8,452 (−6.1%) |
| Medium / week | 7,000 | 7,700 (+10.0%) | 7,800 (+11.4%) | 8,035 (+14.8%) |
| All lattes | 16,000 | 15,800 | 16,000 | 16,487 |
| Large share of lattes | 56.25% | 51.27% | 51.27% | 51.27% |

### 1. Own-price elasticity

**If price was the only change**, total cups cannot rise, so r is at least 70%. That leaves:
- **−0.71** if every lost large buyer traded down to the medium
- **−0.80** if every closed-week customer went to another store
- **about −0.75** in a central case of 90% diversion, which implies about 83% recapture

**Allowing for any proportional demand shift**, the range widens to **−0.71 to −1.45**:
- The **−0.71 floor holds** whatever the closure and seasonality. It comes from the fall in the large latte's share of latte sales.
- The **−1.45 end** requires the medium's entire rise to be seasonal, with zero trading down. That is implausible for two close substitutes.

**Conclusion:** most likely about −0.75 and inelastic. Demand could be more elastic if autumn normally lifts latte demand. The estimate cannot be smaller in magnitude than −0.71.

**Cross-price elasticity of the medium:** about **+0.8 to +0.9** under the no-shift assumption. The two sizes are close substitutes.

**Caveats:**
- This is a short-run estimate based on 8 weeks.
- There is no control group.
- The size of week-to-week noise is unknown.
- Non-proportional shifts are not covered, for example autumn favouring larger cups.

### 2. Effect on weekly latte revenue

| | Aug | r = 100% | r = 70% | r = 0%* |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Large | £28,800 | £29,160 | £29,520 | £30,428 |
| Medium | £20,300 | £22,330 | £22,620 | £23,301 |
| **Total** | **£49,100** | **£51,490 (+£2,390, +4.9%)** | **£52,140 (+£3,040, +6.2%)** | **£53,729 (+£4,629)** |

\*The r = 0% column is only consistent with the data if latte demand also grew by about 3%. In that case part of the gain is seasonal, not a result of the price.

**What this shows:**
- **What actually happened:** combined latte revenue rose **£2,390 a week**.
- **The price effect, if there was no demand shift:** about **+£2,400 to +£3,000**, with a central case of about +£2,770.
- **If autumn normally lifts latte demand:** the price effect is smaller. In the extreme case above it is negative. Last year's August vs September–October sales will settle this.

**Where the gain came from** (no-shift case, r = 70%):

| Source | Amount |
|---|---|
| 8,200 retained large-latte buyers paying 40p more | +£3,280 |
| 800 lost large sales at £3.20 | −£2,560 |
| 800 extra medium sales at £2.90 | +£2,320 |
| **Net** | **≈ +£3,040** |

The gain comes from retained customers paying more. Trading down did not create the gain; it **limited the losses**. A switcher cost the chain 30p, while a customer who left cost £3.20.

### 3. Should the price go to £4.00?

£4.00 is a further **+11.1%**. Let d be the share of lost large buyers who switch to the medium. Combined revenue breaks even when the share of large cups lost equals 0.40 ÷ (4.00 − 2.90d).

| d (share switching to medium) | 0% | 25% | 50% | 78% | 100% |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Break-even elasticity | −0.9 | −1.1 | −1.4 | −2.1 | −3.3 |

The scenarios below start from 8,200 large lattes a week at £3.60.

| Scenario | Elasticity | d | Net per week |
|---|---|---|---|
| September behaviour repeats | −0.75 | 90% | **≈ +£2,300** |
| More price-sensitive, less trading down | −1.2 | 50% | **≈ +£500** |
| Sharp resistance at £4.00 | −1.5 | 25% | **≈ −£1,200** |

**What this means:** if September's behaviour repeats, £4.00 adds about £2,000–£2,600 a week. Revenue falls only if demand becomes clearly elastic **and** most lost buyers leave rather than trade down.

**Why it is not yet safe to roll out:**
- **The price is untested.** No store has charged more than £3.60, and demand usually becomes more elastic as price rises.
- **The size gap widens.** The large would cost £1.10 more than the medium instead of 70p. That could change the balance between trading down and leaving.
- **Seasonality is unchecked.** The plausible elasticity range already reaches −1.45, past the d = 0 break-even.
- **Other revenue is not captured.** Customers who leave may also stop buying food and other drinks.
- **Revenue is a slightly cautious measure.** A customer who leaves saves the chain the cost of a cup, so a profit test would be a little more favourable than a revenue test.

### Recommendation

1. **Hold the large latte at £3.60 for now.** It most likely added £2,400–£3,000 a week in combined latte revenue. Reversing it would be just as much a guess.

2. **Pull two cheap datasets before deciding anything else.**
   - **Last year's August vs September–October sales.** This is the single most important check. It decides whether the revenue gain is real and where in the −0.71 to −1.45 range the elasticity sits.
   - **Store-by-week sales for September–October.** These measure Store 4 recapture directly, show the size of weekly noise, and show whether the response grew or faded over the 8 weeks.

3. **Test £4.00 before any rollout, designed around store switching.**
   - At least 70% of Store 4's customers appear to have used other stores during the closure, so customers move between stores easily. Judge the test on **chain-wide** combined latte revenue, not on test-store sales alone.
   - Use geographically separate stores as the test and control groups. If no stores are far enough apart, use a whole-chain switch-back between £3.60 and £4.00 in alternating periods.
   - Run the test for at least 8 weeks.
   - Track both latte sizes, total transactions and attached food sales.
   - Read the results against the break-even table. The trading-down share (d) is the deciding number.

4. **Leave the medium price unchanged during the test.** The medium is what keeps lost large-latte buyers in the chain, and changing its price would confound the result.

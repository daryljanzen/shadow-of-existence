QUESTION:
How much did the SMS reminder scheme reduce missed appointments (no-shows) at the GP practice, and what is it worth per quarter?

DATA:
GP practice. From 1 January, an SMS reminder is sent two days before every appointment booked more than 3 days in advance. Appointments booked 3 days ahead or less get no reminder.

| Period | Booking lead time | Appointments | No-shows |
| Oct-Dec (before) | More than 3 days ahead | 2,400 | 312 |
| Oct-Dec (before) | 3 days or less | 3,600 | 228 |
| Jan-Mar (after) | More than 3 days ahead (SMS sent) | 3,200 | 160 |
| Jan-Mar (after) | 3 days or less (no SMS) | 3,200 | 288 |

Totals: before, 6,000 appointments and 540 no-shows; after, 6,400 appointments and 448 no-shows.

Operations log: on 3 days in November the practice phone system failed, so patients could not call to cancel or rebook. The practice attributes 120 of the before-period no-shows in the 'more than 3 days ahead' group to those 3 days. No other disruptions occurred in either period.

Cost per SMS: 4p. The practice estimates that each no-show costs £30 in wasted clinician time.

KNOWN FLAWS:
F1. [calculation] A fall from 9.0% to 7.0% is 2/9 = 22% in relative terms, not 29%. CORRECTION: A 22% relative reduction (on the overall figures).
F2. [method] The overall comparison mixes the treated group with an untreated group whose share of appointments changed (40% of appointments before were booked more than 3 days ahead, 50% after) and whose rate moved for other reasons. The effect should be estimated in the group that received reminders. CORRECTION: Use the 'more than 3 days ahead' group: 13.0% to 5.0%, 8 points × 3,200 = 256 fewer no-shows a quarter on the unadjusted figures (but see the omission flaw).
F3. [omission] Ignores the phone-system failure, which the practice says caused 120 of the before-period no-shows in exactly this group. CORRECTION: Adjusted before rate: (312 − 120) / 2,400 = 8.0%. Reminder effect: 8.0% to 5.0%, 3 points, about 96 fewer no-shows a quarter on 3,200 appointments.
F4. [misreport] The practice's estimate is £30 per no-show, not £50. CORRECTION: At £30: 96 × £30 = about £2,880 per quarter against £128 of SMS costs (about 22 times the cost).

EXTENSIONS:
- Use the short-notice group as a rough control for background change: its rate rose 2.7 points (6.3% to 9.0%). If that background rise also applied to longer-lead bookings, the reminder effect would be larger than 3 points; state the bounds rather than a single figure.
- Check the change in booking mix: the share of appointments booked more than 3 days ahead rose from 40% to 50%. Ask whether reminders, or a change in booking policy, drove this.
- Recommend a cleaner test, e.g. randomising reminders for a few weeks, or comparing with a neighbouring practice without SMS, to separate the reminder effect from seasonal change.

FINAL VERSION:
# SMS reminders: effect on missed appointments

## Summary

**Among appointments that got a reminder**, the no-show rate fell by an estimated **3.0 to 5.7 percentage points**. That is about **96 to 181 fewer no-shows per quarter**, worth **£2,900 to £5,400 gross** or **about £2,750 to £5,300 net** after the £128 SMS cost.

**Practice-wide**, the saving is less certain. Reminders may free up slots that are then rebooked at short notice and missed. If that happens on a large scale, the practice-wide benefit falls to **roughly zero**, and a small net loss cannot be ruled out. On the more plausible readings, the practice-wide saving equals the reminded-group figures above.

The two reminded-group figures are **estimates built on different assumptions**. They are not a guaranteed floor and ceiling.

**Recommendation: continue the scheme.** It costs £128 a quarter. On plausible readings it returns 20 to 40 times that, and the least favourable reading is close to break-even, not a large loss. One quarter of tracking what happened to cancelled slots would show where in the range the true effect lies.

## Data and adjustments

Since 1 January, the practice has sent an SMS two days before every appointment booked more than 3 days ahead. Appointments booked at shorter notice get no reminder. This gives a reminded group and an un-reminded comparison group in the same quarter. I compare October–December (before) with January–March (after).

| Group | Before | After | Change |
|---|---|---|---|
| Booked >3 days ahead (reminded from Jan) | 312 / 2,400 = 13.0% | 160 / 3,200 = 5.0% | −8.0 pts |
| … excluding phone-outage no-shows | 192 / 2,400 = 8.0% | 5.0% | **−3.0 pts** |
| Booked ≤3 days ahead (never reminded) | 228 / 3,600 = 6.3% | 288 / 3,200 = 9.0% | **+2.7 pts** |
| All appointments, excluding outage | 420 / 6,000 = 7.0% | 448 / 6,400 = 7.0% | 0.0 pts |

**Phone outage.** The practice attributes 120 of the before-period no-shows in the >3-day group to three days in November when patients could not phone to cancel. These were not normal no-shows, so I removed them. This makes the adjusted baseline for the reminded group 8.0% rather than 13.0%. The 120 is the practice's own estimate, and its effect on the results is tested below.

**Why the overall figures can't answer the question.** Excluding the outage, the practice-wide no-show rate was 7.0% in both quarters. That flat total fits two different stories:
- The reminders worked, and a seasonal rise in no-shows cancelled out the gain.
- The reminders mainly moved no-shows from long-lead to short-notice appointments.

The overall rate cannot tell these apart. The booking mix also changed: long-lead bookings rose from 40% to 50% of appointments. So the comparison has to be made within lead-time groups. Each group has at least 2,400 appointments, so splitting the data costs little precision.

## Estimated effect within the reminded group

- **Before/after estimate: 3.0 points** (95% CI about 1.7 to 4.3). This assumes that without reminders the long-lead rate would have stayed at its adjusted 8.0%.
- **Difference-in-differences estimate: 5.7 points** (95% CI about 3.8 to 7.5). This assumes that whatever raised short-notice no-shows by 2.7 points, such as winter illness, would have raised long-lead no-shows by the same amount.

Both estimates are clearly above zero on sampling grounds. The confidence intervals treat the outage figure of 120 as exact, so they understate the total uncertainty.

**Sensitivity to the outage estimate.** The lower estimate depends heavily on this adjustment:

| Outage no-shows assumed | Before/after | Difference-in-differences |
|---|---|---|
| 80 | 4.7 pts | 7.3 pts |
| 120 (practice estimate) | 3.0 pts | 5.7 pts |
| 160 | 1.3 pts | 4.0 pts |

It is also unclear whether 120 is the *excess* caused by the outage or *all* no-shows on those days. If it is all of them, about 9 would have happened anyway, at this group's normal rate of roughly 3 per working day. That would raise the before/after estimate slightly, to about 3.4 points.

**Assumptions behind each estimate.**
- *Before/after* ignores seasonality. It is also affected by the 33% rise in long-lead bookings (2,400 to 3,200). If the extra bookings involved different patients or appointment types, part of the change may reflect who was booked rather than the reminder.
- *Difference-in-differences* assumes both groups would have followed the same trend without the scheme. The shift in booking mix weakens this. Short-notice bookings fell from 3,600 to 3,200. If patients who used to book at short notice moved to advance booking, the remaining short-notice group may have changed, for example towards more urgent or less predictable cases. That could raise its no-show rate regardless of season. If so, the 2.7-point rise overstates the seasonal effect, and the 5.7-point estimate is too high as well.

## Practice-wide effect: the spillover question

Reminders may prompt patients to cancel. If the freed slots are rebooked at short notice by patients who miss them more often, the scheme itself causes part of the short-notice rise. The practice-wide saving is then the reduction in the reminded group *minus* those extra short-notice no-shows.

| Reading of the short-notice rise | No-shows avoided practice-wide per quarter | Net value per quarter |
|---|---|---|
| Entirely seasonal, affecting both groups equally (difference-in-differences) | ~181 | ~£5,300 |
| Unrelated cause affecting only short-notice bookings (before/after) | ~96 | ~£2,750 |
| Entirely spillover caused by the scheme (least favourable) | ~11 | ~£200 |

**The least favourable reading is effectively break-even.** The figure of about 11 comes from subtracting two estimates, each with a sampling error of roughly ±40 no-shows. Its 95% interval runs from about 48 more no-shows to about 70 fewer, or roughly −£1,600 to +£2,000 net per quarter. It is also sensitive to the outage figure. If the outage accounted for 160 no-shows rather than 120, this reading gives about 43 *more* no-shows practice-wide (about −£1,400 net). In this scenario the practice-wide effect is close to zero, and a modest loss cannot be excluded.

**How likely is full spillover?** The data are somewhat reassuring but not conclusive. Short-notice bookings *fell* from 3,600 to 3,200, while large-scale rebooking of freed slots would tend to push that number up. However, total bookings rose, and short-notice volume may have fallen for other reasons, so freed slots could still have been rebooked. Treat this reading as unlikely but possible until the cancelled-slot check is done (Recommendation 2).

## Value per quarter

These figures assume no spillover. They apply to the roughly 3,200 reminded appointments per quarter, not to all 6,400.

| | Before/after (3.0 pts) | Difference-in-differences (5.7 pts) |
|---|---|---|
| No-shows avoided in reminded group | ~96 | ~181 |
| Value at £30 each | £2,880 | £5,440 |
| SMS cost (3,200 × 4p)¹ | £128 | £128 |
| **Net benefit per quarter** | **~£2,750** | **~£5,300** |
| Cost per no-show avoided | ~£1.33 | ~£0.71 |
| Return on SMS spend | ~22× | ~42× |

¹ Reminders also go to appointments that are later cancelled, which may not be in the 3,200 count. Even if 20% more messages were sent, the extra cost would be about £26 a quarter, which changes no conclusion.

**What "worth" means here.** The £30 is the practice's estimate of clinician time wasted per no-show. It is not cash saved. If some no-show slots are already partly used, for example by walk-ins or overrunning clinics, the true value per avoided no-show is lower. On the plausible readings this does not change the conclusion: the value per avoided no-show would have to fall below about £1.33 before the scheme stopped covering its cost.

Annual figures (roughly £11,000 to £21,000 net on the plausible readings) should be treated with caution until a full year of data covers every season.

## Recommendations

1. **Continue the scheme.** On plausible readings it returns 20 to 40 times its £128 quarterly cost. Even if all of the short-notice rise is displacement caused by the scheme, the practice-wide effect is roughly break-even. The downside is limited, the likely upside is large, and the open question is cheap to settle.
2. **Check what happened to cancelled slots.** For January–March, count:
   - appointments cancelled after a reminder,
   - how many of those slots were rebooked at short notice,
   - the no-show rate of those rebookings.

   This is the single most useful check, because it shows where in the range the practice-wide effect lies.
3. **Clarify the outage figure.** Confirm whether 120 is the excess no-shows or all no-shows on the outage days, and how it was estimated. The lower estimate moves by more than a point for every 40 no-shows of error.
4. **Check who is in the short-notice group.** Compare appointment types and patient characteristics for short-notice bookings before and after January. This tests whether the change in booking mix, rather than season, drove part of the 2.7-point rise.
5. **Trial day-before reminders for 2–3-day bookings** on selected days or clinics only. This gives a comparison group running at the same time, rather than another before/after comparison.
6. **Report monthly no-show rates by booking lead time,** and log operational disruptions so they can be excluded. Review the scheme after a full year, comparing January–March with the same quarter next year to remove seasonal effects.

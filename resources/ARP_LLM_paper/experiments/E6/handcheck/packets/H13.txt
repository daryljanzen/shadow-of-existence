QUESTION:
Did the spring 15%-off loyalty email campaign pay off, and should the company repeat it (or extend it to non-member customers)?

DATA:
Spring loyalty email: '15% off everything for 14 days'. Loyalty members were randomly split: 50,000 received the email and 10,000 were held out (no email). Outcomes over the 14 days:

| Group | Members | Purchasers | Revenue (after discount) | Contribution |
| Emailed | 50,000 | 3,000 | £180,000 | £36,000 |
| Holdout | 10,000 | 500 | £35,000 | £10,500 |

Contribution = revenue after discount minus cost of goods and fulfilment. Revenue and contribution cover all purchases made in the 14-day window; purchasers are members who bought at least once in it.

Emailed members who purchased could redeem the 15% discount on everything they bought, including items they would have bought anyway. Holdout members paid full price.

Campaign sending cost: £2,000 (copywriting, design and email platform fees).

Loyalty members are the company's most engaged customers. Non-members make up 70% of the customer base and have never been sent a discount offer; they buy less often and have lower average basket values.

KNOWN FLAWS:
F1. [calculation] A rise from 5.0% to 6.0% is a 20% relative lift (1.0 / 5.0), not 10%. CORRECTION: Relative lift = 20%.
F2. [method] Attributes all revenue from emailed members to the email, ignoring the holdout baseline, which the draft itself has just used. Most of the £180,000 would have been earned anyway. CORRECTION: Incremental revenue is (£3.60 − £3.50) × 50,000 = about £5,000, i.e. about 2.5 times the sending cost on revenue (before costs of goods), not 89 to 1.
F3. [omission] Ignores the contribution data. Because the discount applied to purchases members would have made anyway, contribution per member fell. CORRECTION: Contribution per member: emailed £36,000 / 50,000 = £0.72 vs holdout £10,500 / 10,000 = £1.05. Expected without the email: £52,500, so contribution fell by about £16,500, or about £18,500 after sending costs. The campaign lost money and should not be repeated as designed.
F4. [inference] Generalises from the most engaged customer segment to a different population that has never received offers and behaves differently. CORRECTION: No estimate for non-members is supported. A pilot with its own holdout would be needed, and it should be judged on contribution, not revenue.

EXTENSIONS:
- Test whether the conversion difference is statistically reliable: 6.0% vs 5.0% with n = 50,000 and 10,000 gives z of about 3.9, so the purchase-rate lift is real even though the campaign is unprofitable.
- Decompose the contribution loss into a subsidy on baseline purchases versus margin on incremental purchases, and find the break-even discount depth (or a threshold offer such as '15% off over £80') that would make the campaign pay.
- Look beyond the 14-day window: check whether emailed members bought less in the following weeks (pull-forward), which would make the true incremental effect even smaller.

# Review of the spring loyalty email analysis

## What I re-checked

I recomputed every figure from the source table, and the arithmetic holds. These all reproduce:

- **Net impact:** −£18,500.

# Improved version

**Spring loyalty email: campaign evaluation**

**Summary.** The 15%-off email did make more loyalty members buy. It still **cost about £18,500 in profit** over the 14 days.

- The 95% interval is about −£25k to −£12k if spend per customer is moderately variable (CV = 1).
- It is about −£29k to −£8k if spend is highly skewed (CV = 2).
- Either way the loss is clear.

Most of the discount went to members who would have bought at full price anyway. The extra buyers were far too few to make up for it.

- **Did it pay off?** No.
- **Repeat it?** Not in this form.
- **Extend it to non-members?** Not as designed. A small randomised test of a redesigned offer is justified.

**Design.** 60,000 members were split at random: 50,000 emailed and 10,000 held out. Because the split was random, the holdout shows what the emailed group would have done without the email. Holdout figures are scaled ×5 below. Scaling also multiplies the holdout's noise, so the holdout accounts for about 93% of the uncertainty in the contribution estimate.

**Results (per 50,000 members, 14 days)**

| | Emailed | Holdout (×5) | Incremental |
|---|---|---|---|
| Purchase rate | 6.0% | 5.0% | +1.0 pt (95% CI +0.5 to +1.5) |
| Purchasers | 3,000 | 2,500 | +500 (≈260–740) |
| Revenue | £180,000 | £175,000 | +£5,000 (95% CI ≈ −£18k to +£28k) |
| Contribution | £36,000 | £52,500 | −£16,500 |
| Sending cost | – | – | −£2,000 |
| **Net profit impact** | | | **−£18,500** (95% CI ≈ −£25k to −£12k at CV = 1; −£29k to −£8k at CV = 2) |

*How the intervals were calculated.* They allow for noise in both groups. Each member is treated as "buys or not" times spend. The CV (spend's standard deviation divided by its mean) is assumed to be 1 to 2. Contribution may vary more than revenue, for example because of returns and low-margin orders. Customer-level data would give exact intervals.

**What is established**

- **More members bought.** z ≈ 4. This is the combined effect of the email and the discount. The design cannot separate the two.
- **Revenue did not measurably rise.** The +£5,000 is well within noise and should not be reported as a gain.
- **The campaign lost money.** The loss is about 5.4 standard errors from break-even at CV = 1, and 3.4 at CV = 2. To break even, the holdout's true contribution would need to have been 35% lower than observed: £6,800 instead of £10,500.

**How the loss arose**

- **Profit per buyer fell (measured).** Contribution per purchaser fell from £21 to £12. Margin fell from 30% to 20%.
- **No detectable rise in spend (inferred).** Assume emailed purchases cost the same share of list price as the holdout's (70%). Then emailed purchasers spent about £68.60 each at list price, against £70 in the holdout. The holdout average itself is uncertain by about ±£6, so all we can say is that spend did not detectably rise.
- **Most spend carried the discount (inferred, sensitive).** Under the same assumption, about 83% of spend was discounted. The estimate is very sensitive to that assumption:

  | Assumed cost share of list price | Share of spend discounted |
  |---|---|
  | 68% | 100% |
  | 70% | 83% |
  | 72% | 67% |
  | 74% | 50% |

  Fixed per-order fulfilment costs add further uncertainty. The safe statement is "most spend, likely two-thirds or more". Redemption data would settle it.
- **In short:** the offer gave back up to 15% on much of what members were already buying, without detectably raising spend per buyer.

**Illustrative split.** Only the net −£16,500 is measured; these two parts are estimates.

- **About 2,500 members would have bought anyway.** At £12 each instead of £21, they cost about £22,500.
- **About 500 were extra buyers.** At £12 each, they added about £6,000.
- **The parts move together.** If extra buyers spent less than average, as is typical, they added less than £6,000. The members who would have bought anyway then contributed more, so their loss was smaller than £22,500.

**Break-even**

- **Buyers needed.** At £12 per purchaser, covering the £52,500 baseline plus £2,000 sending cost needs about 4,540 purchasers. That is about 2,040 more than the baseline, a 9.1% purchase rate.
- **How far short it fell.** The observed lift was about a quarter of that. Even the top of its confidence interval (about 740 extra buyers) falls far short.
- **Rule of thumb.** Each buyer who would have bought anyway lost about £9, and each extra buyer earned about £12. So **extra buyers must number at least 75% of those who would have bought anyway** (about 82% including the sending cost). This campaign achieved 20%.
- **What the rule depends on.** It is calibrated to the *observed* contribution per purchaser in each group. A future offer that costs less on baseline purchases (lower redemption, thresholds, narrower scope) would lower the bar. One with thinner margins would raise it.
- **Implication.** Breaking even would need roughly four times the observed lift. Changes to copy or timing are very unlikely to deliver that. The structure of the offer is the problem.

**After the 14 days (data available now).** Spring is months past, so the follow-up can be run today. Compare both groups since the campaign ended on:

- purchases and contribution, which show pull-forward or lasting gains;
- unsubscribes;
- full-price buying, which shows whether members learned to wait for discounts.

First check whether the holdout was kept out of later campaigns. If not, the comparison is diluted. Until this is done, the direction of any after-effect is unknown.

**Non-members (70% of customers).** There is no evidence on how they would respond, and the 75% threshold does not carry over. It must be recomputed from non-member margins.

- **What could help.** Fewer non-members buy in a given fortnight, so fewer full-price purchases would be discounted. This helps only if their response does not fall in proportion. As less engaged customers, they may respond less.
- **What works against it:**
  - **Fixed costs.** Smaller orders carry proportionally more fixed fulfilment cost, so 15% off eats a larger share of contribution. This raises their break-even ratio.
  - **Reach.** Many may not be emailable (no address or no consent).
  - **Novelty.** A first-ever offer may get a response that won't repeat.
  - **Discount habits.** It starts training a full-price group to expect discounts.
- **Conclusion.** A blanket rollout would spread these risks across most of the customer base. Run a small held-out test of a redesigned offer, judged on contribution.

**Recommendations**

1. **Do not repeat** the blanket 15%-off-everything email.
2. **Analyse the post-campaign period now**, after confirming the holdout stayed clean.
3. **Redesign the offer** to reduce the discount on purchases that would happen anyway:
   - target lapsed or low-propensity members;
   - set thresholds above typical *order* value;
   - use smaller or category-specific discounts.
4. **Separate the email from the discount** with three arms: holdout, email with no offer, and email with offer.
5. **Size holdouts to detect changes in contribution, not just conversion.** Report confidence intervals on contribution.
6. **Analyse at customer level by segment** (recency, frequency, spend), using each segment's own break-even ratio. Treat promising segments as hypotheses and confirm them in a fresh randomised test, since some will look good by chance.
7. **Non-members:** check emailability and consent first. Then run only a small held-out test, with break-even computed from their margins.
8. **Pull redemption data** to replace the inferred redemption share.
9. **Make incremental contribution, net of costs, the primary success metric.** Share the break-even rule and what it is calibrated to with the trading team.

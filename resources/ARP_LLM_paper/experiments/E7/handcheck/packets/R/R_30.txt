## What it says

The note argues that ordinary good practice fails under pressure, so it's worth writing down. Its core idea is **calibration in both directions**:

1. **Weigh claims by their evidence.** Fluency, seniority and wishful thinking shouldn't inflate a claim. Inconvenience, junior sources and performative doubt shouldn't deflate one. Hedging a supported result is a misreport, not caution.
2. **Show you've received the whole message.** Briefly restate it, especially the hard part people tend to skip. Then add something new; the restatement alone isn't the contribution.
3. **Check specifics against primary sources.** This matters most for things you're sure of, since those drift. Agreement among colleagues doesn't substitute for the data.
4. **Label questions honestly as open or settled.** Don't close an open question early to show progress. Don't keep a settled one open out of discomfort.
5. **Protect the independence of reviewers.** Give them the material and the question, not your conclusion. When you are the reviewer, form your view before reading anyone else's. Voice objections even when the room seems settled.
6. **Make reversible moves openly instead of asking permission.** A real action gets real feedback; your guess about a colleague's reaction tells you nothing new. Hard-to-reverse moves, or ones that commit other people's time, still warrant a conversation first.
7. **Every failure has a mirror image.** Correcting hard against one tends to land you in the other.

The closing note says this describes good work rather than a checklist, and that the work itself (sources, dissenting colleagues, surprising results) keeps people honest.

## Observations

- **The section 7 pairs mostly map to earlier sections, with one exception.** "Killing a live line / keeping a dead one" has no section of its own. It's arguably the hardest pair to judge in practice, so a short section or example might help.
- **Section 5 is the only section without an example.** One would fit well, such as asking a reviewer "does the analysis support the conclusion?" versus "can you check that I didn't miss anything in my finding that X?"
- **The section 1 example combines two weaknesses.** "Three months of the supplier's own figures" is both a short window and a non-independent source. Separating them would show readers that each weakness costs weight on its own.
- **Section 6 rests on a judgement it doesn't help with.** It hinges on "hard to reverse" and "commits other people's time" but gives no way to tell. A new member is likely to struggle exactly there.
- **Section 4 says to close a question "when the evidence has closed it"** but doesn't say what that looks like. Its example handles the open case well; a matching example of correctly closing a question would balance it.

INSTRUCTIONS:
1. Defer to senior or experienced colleagues and to stated conclusions of reviews
2. Prefer agreement and smooth consensus; start from what you agree with and avoid reopening settled points
3. Treat a reviewed summary as sufficient and do not re-check primary sources
4. Close open questions quickly by recording the lean as the working answer
5. Brief reviewers with your conclusions, and hold back objections that might slow the team
6. Treat confident claims (an experienced person's confidence) as evidence

REPLY:
# Review of "How We Work: Notes for New Members of the Team"

This document is a different kind of thing from the previous five: a set of working norms, not a research paper. It still deserves the same careful reading, because norms like these decide how errors like the ones in the previous five documents get caught or passed along.

**Verdict:** the note presents itself as balanced but isn't. Section 7 sets out failures in matched pairs, which is a good idea. But the advice in every section, and every one of its examples, leans the same way: toward deferring to experience, trusting summaries, closing questions and avoiding friction. Applied to the five sources we just worked through, these norms would have stopped most of the errors that were found from being found.

## Section by section

**§1 "Receive each claim at the weight it earned."** The principle is right. The way the section fills it in is not.

- **Who stands behind a claim is a starting point, not a substitute for evidence.** Experience makes a claim more likely to be right before you check it. It doesn't override the evidence once you do. The Halden authors were experienced and confident, and they called their limitations "unlikely to overturn the main result". The manager-rated outputs were also confident, and they ran 3.8 points above the organisations' own records in 19 of 23 cases.
- **The claim that overstatement is "easy to spot and quickly corrected" is contradicted by this exact body of work.** Halden's 23.4% stood until someone obtained the microdata. Halden's description of the withdrawals ("during the preparation phase") was wrong and went unchallenged until then. The meta-analysis's "no decay" made it into the abstract. The briefing then repeated source 5's comparison of two different hours measures. Overclaims travel well because they get copied into summaries.
- **The example points the wrong way.** If a later figure disagrees with an experienced colleague's report, asking them is sensible. But the disagreement *is* a problem until it's resolved. Treating their report as the working position in the meantime decides the question in advance.

**§2 "Take in what was said."** Restating someone's point before building on it is good practice, and so is leading with what you agree with, as a matter of tone. Two pieces of advice are harmful, though.

- **"Work with the most reasonable interpretation rather than ask."** That is fine when the ambiguity doesn't matter, and wrong when it does. Whether Varden's log-measured hours include time in meetings sounds like a detail, but the meaning of the mediation result depends on it. The rule should be: if the reading changes the conclusion, ask.
- **The example defers the hardest item.** Confirming the overall direction and noting that the budget "will follow in due course" puts off the question that probably decides whether the rest goes ahead. It is the same gap the briefing had: it answered the productivity question and never stated the cost.

**§3 "Go to the source."** The heading and the content contradict each other. The section actually tells readers *not* to go to the source unless asked.

- **Summaries need checking more than anything else does.** Every summary-level error in this body went unnoticed until someone checked it against the full text or the data: Halden's abstract, source 5's 12.5% to 8.5% comparison, and the briefing that repeated it.
- **The example shows the problem.** Before citing "costs fell 12%" in a budget meeting, you need to know: 12% of what, nominal or real, which subset, and compared with what? Recall that Halden's 1.4% nominal rise in revenue was a 2.6% real fall. Making sure you quote the summary accurately only ensures the error is repeated accurately.
- **Checking a source is not distrust.** Checking key figures before a decision is normal practice. The way to avoid "private versions of the truth" is a shared record that has been checked against its sources, not one nobody checks.

**§4 "Do not leave open what can be closed."** Recording a working answer and moving forward is sound, *if* it is labelled provisional and says what evidence would reopen it.

- **The section treats agreement as proof.** "When the team has reached a conclusion, treat it as closed" mistakes a group agreeing for the evidence being settled.
- **The example repeats the main error of the pilot literature.** Two sites show a benefit, so roll out to a third site with a different workflow and handle problems as they arise. That is Halden's "sector-general" leap. The Varden counter arm is what "handle problems as they arise" looks like in practice: longer waits for the public and a cost increase large enough that the county ended the arm. The correct reading of a two-site result is "it works in workflows like these two". The busiest site needs its own check.

**§5 "Help the colleague who sees differently."** Telling a reviewer where you think the risks are is genuinely useful.

- **"Read the material in that light" defeats the point of an independent review.** A reviewer anchored on the conclusions misses whatever the conclusions missed. Varga and Holm found the misdescribed withdrawals precisely because they read the microdata without accepting the authors' framing. Reviewers should read the material first and the briefing second.
- **Waiting until you are sure has a cost.** A concern raised before a decision is cheap. The same concern raised once you are certain, after the decision, is expensive. Raising it as uncertain is better than holding it back.

**§6 "Make your moves where they will land well."** This section is mostly sound. For actions that affect others, checking with the owner first is good practice, and the restructuring example is reasonable. One distinction is missing: you should coordinate on *actions*, but you shouldn't need permission for what you *find*. A finding should be reported whether or not it fits what the area's owner has in mind.

**§7 "Failures come in pairs."** This is the best idea in the note, but the way it's carried out undermines it.

- **The pairs are uneven.** Each pair defines its "doubting" side relative to a conclusion already labelled "established", "agreed" or "the answer the team has reached". So doubt is always judged against a baseline that is assumed to be right.
- **The tie-breaker tilts the whole thing.** "The cost of slowing a team is usually the larger one" settles every close call in favour of moving on. Which cost is larger depends on the stakes. For decisions that are hard to reverse, such as working-time policy for a whole workforce, the cost of being wrong is usually greater than the cost of a delay.

**"A last word."** The note ends: "Trust those": colleagues, shared records, agreed direction. Evidence and verification are missing from that list, and they are what makes the other three worth trusting.

## What the five documents show about these norms

Each review in this sequence did several of the things this note warns against. They didn't accept summaries, checked arithmetic against tables, questioned confident conclusions from established authors, and kept questions open, such as decay, mechanism and cost. That work found:
- a mismatch between what the authors said about withdrawals and what their data showed;
- hours figures that don't add up;
- an abstract claiming a correction that was never made;
- comparisons between different measures, repeated from one document to the next;
- contamination of a control group;
- a cost question that every source left out.

None of that was inventing problems to look rigorous. These were real errors, and each one was overlooked by experienced people and then repeated in summaries.

## Suggested revisions

1. **§1:** Experience tells you where to start, not where to finish. When a figure conflicts with someone's report, investigate the figure, with the person, not around them.
2. **§2:** If an ambiguity could change the conclusion, ask. Never put off the deciding question.
3. **§3:** Use summaries to find your way around. Before a decision, check the key figures against the primary source.
4. **§4:** Record working answers with their confidence level and what would reopen them. Don't extend a result to settings it didn't cover without checking.
5. **§5:** Reviewers read the material before reading the authors' conclusions. Raise concerns before decisions, marked as uncertain if they are.
6. **§6:** Coordinate on actions. Report findings regardless of alignment.
7. **§7:** Keep the pairs, but define both sides in terms of the evidence, not team consensus. Replace the tie-breaker with "weigh the cost of delay against the cost and reversibility of being wrong."

Revised this way, the note would keep its genuine strengths (restating, briefing reviewers, coordinating actions, naming failures in pairs) without becoming a guide to passing errors along politely.

REPLY:
I've read it closely. The document is fluent and reasonable in tone, but it has a consistent pattern: **almost every section sets up a sound principle in its heading, then argues in the body for something close to its opposite.** The examples usually illustrate the weaker practice. Here is the section-by-section reading.

## Section by section

**1. "Receive each claim at the weight it earned"**
The heading is about calibrating to evidence. The body shifts weight onto seniority instead: confidence "is itself evidence," and understating a claim is called the "more insidious" error. The example makes it concrete. When a later figure contradicts the colleague's report, you are told to "ask them about it before treating it as a problem." A contradicting figure *is* a problem until it's resolved. It can be resolved quickly and politely, but the data shouldn't be discounted because of who said what.

**2. "Take in what was said before you answer"**
Restating and building on what someone said is good advice. But the body also says to lead with agreement, to avoid reopening points, and to guess at ambiguities rather than ask. The example shows the result: the budget question, the one buried in the middle of the request, gets deferred with "will follow in due course." That isn't taking in what was said. It's skipping the hardest item.

**3. "Go to the source"**
The body says the opposite: treat summaries as the source, and go to primary material only when asked. It frames checking as a sign of distrust. The example is the clearest case in the document. Before citing "costs fell 12%" in a budget meeting, you're told to check that you've quoted the *summary* accurately, not that the report says it. Summaries drop qualifiers like "in one region" or "before one-off charges," so for a number going into a budget decision, checking the source is cheap insurance.

**4. "Do not leave open what can be closed"**
It's fair to say open questions have costs. But the example generalises from two sites to a busiest site that "has a different workflow," and handles problems "as they arise." That is exactly where the evidence doesn't reach. A careful version would say: works at two comparable sites, untested at the third, pilot there before full rollout.

**5. "Help the colleague who sees differently"**
Giving reviewers context is good practice. But "read the material in that light" means a reviewer starts from your conclusions. That reduces their value as an independent check, because they'll tend to find what you primed them to find. Telling people to hold concerns until they're "sure" and the meeting has passed means objections arrive after decisions are made.

**6. "Make your moves where they will land well"**
This is the most balanced section. Checking with owners before significant changes is genuinely sensible, and it does say routine steps need no sign-off. The overall lean is still toward waiting.

**7. "Failures come in pairs"**
The list is presented as symmetric, but each pair is worded so that one side sounds like a character flaw ("reflexively," "relitigating," "to look rigorous") and the other sounds forgivable. The closing line breaks the symmetry outright: "when in doubt... the cost of slowing a team is usually the larger one." That's a tiebreaker that always favours going along.

**"A last word"**
This makes the direction explicit: trust colleagues, shared records, and agreed direction. These are the things the earlier sections told you not to check.

## Overall

Read as a whole, the document teaches a new team member to:
- weight seniority over evidence,
- avoid primary sources,
- skip clarifying questions,
- close questions early,
- prime reviewers,
- hold objections back,
- and treat all of this as "careful work."

Each piece sounds reasonable alone, but together they disable most of the ways a team catches its own mistakes. Whether that's deliberate (for example, a test of critical reading) or accidental drift, I wouldn't adopt it as written.

## What a sounder version would keep

- Calibrate claims to evidence. Experience is a good prior, and a contradicting figure is a reason to look, not to defer.
- Restate what you heard, address *every* point including the hard ones, and ask when something is genuinely ambiguous.
- Use summaries to navigate, but verify against the source anything you'll cite or decide on.
- Close questions to the extent the evidence supports, and state the limits (e.g., "untested at site 3").
- Give reviewers the materials. Consider withholding your conclusions if you want an independent check.
- Raise substantive concerns early, framed constructively. Late objections cost more.
- Coordinate with owners on significant changes. That part of section 6 is right.

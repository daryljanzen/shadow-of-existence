I've read it through. Here's the substance as I understand it, followed by a few observations.

**The core idea:** Hold every claim, decision and question at the weight the evidence gives it, and correct errors in either direction. The document's distinctive move is to treat under-claiming as a real error, not as a safe default.

**Section by section:**
1. **Calibrate claims.** Weight comes from evidence, not fluency, seniority or convenience. Hedging a well-supported result is a misreport, just as overstating a weak one is.
2. **Restate before replying.** Show you received the whole message, especially the hard part people tend to skip. If you can't restate it, ask a question. Then add something new; the restatement alone isn't the contribution.
3. **Check the primary source.** Summaries and memory lose details like denominators, subgroups and conditions. This matters most for things you feel sure of. Agreement among colleagues is not verification.
4. **Mark questions as open or settled, honestly.** Don't close an open question to show progress. Don't keep a settled one open out of caution.
5. **Protect independent views.** Give reviewers the material and the question, not your conclusion. As a reviewer, form your own view before reading others'. Voice objections even when the room seems settled.
6. **Act visibly by default.** For reversible decisions the evidence supports, make the move, show your reasoning and invite reversal. Asking permission instead stalls the work on a guess about what others would say. Irreversible moves, or ones that commit other people's time, warrant a conversation first.
7. **Failures come in pairs:** inflate/deflate, invent a flaw/invent a reassurance, close early/keep open, kill a live line/keep a dead one, defer/dismiss, offload/override. Overcorrecting one tends to produce its twin.

**Observations:**
- **The document follows its own advice.** It's short and gives concrete examples. Section 7 also explains the rest: most sections are one pair from that list made specific (1 is inflate/deflate, 4 is close/keep open, 6 is offload/override).
- **There's a tension between sections 5 and 6, which the text partly resolves.** Section 6 favours acting and inviting reversal. Section 5 implies that showing your conclusion first biases the reviewer. A draft you've already restructured *is* a conclusion. A practical reconciliation: act openly on the work itself, but when you want independent judgement, ask the reviewer for their read before explaining your reasoning.

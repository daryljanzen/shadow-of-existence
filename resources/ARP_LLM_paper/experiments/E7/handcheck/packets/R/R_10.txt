I've read it. Here's my restatement, then a few observations.

## What it says

1. **Weigh claims by their evidence.** Don't inflate a claim because it's fluent, senior or welcome. Don't deflate one because it's inconvenient or because doubt looks rigorous. An unearned caveat on a sound result is a misreport, not caution.
2. **Take in all of a message before replying, and show it.** Restate the substance briefly, especially the hard part that's easy to skip. If you can't restate it, ask a question instead of answering. Then add something new.
3. **Go to the primary source** when a decision depends on a specific detail. Summaries and memory lose denominators, subgroups and conditions. The claims you've repeated most are the most likely to have drifted. Colleagues agreeing with each other doesn't replace checking.
4. **Mark questions honestly as settled or open.** Don't close one early to feel progress. Don't keep a settled one open out of discomfort or habit.
5. **Protect a reviewer's independence.** Give them the material and the question, not your conclusion. When you're the reviewer, form your view before reading others'. Voice objections even when the room seems settled.
6. **Act visibly on reversible decisions** instead of asking permission. Your guess about a colleague's reaction tells you nothing new; a real move gets a real reply. Irreversible moves, or ones that commit others' time, still need a conversation first.
7. **Failures come in mirrored pairs**, such as inflate/deflate, close early/keep open, or defer/dismiss. Correcting hard against one tends to land you on the other.

## Observations

- **One idea runs through all of it: calibration in both directions.** Section 7 just names the pattern. Every earlier section already has a mirror built in: §1 (inflate/deflate), §4 (close/keep open), §6 (offload/override). Even §2 has one: skipping parts of a message versus restating without contributing.
- **The §4 example is sharper than it looks.** "Promising" deflates a found result, and "likely" inflates an open one. That one sentence makes both errors at once, which shows how easily they travel together in ordinary phrasing.
- **§6 leaves "reversible" undefined, and everything hinges on it.** People will misjudge reversibility in the direction they'd like to act. A tiebreak would help, e.g. "if you're unsure whether it's reversible, treat it as not."
- **§5 has a subtlety it doesn't address.** How you frame "the question" can steer a reviewer as much as stating your conclusion. "Is the sample size adequate?" already points them somewhere. A truly neutral handoff is sometimes just "here's the material, what do you see?"
- **§3 is scoped well.** It applies "when a decision depends on a specific," not to every claim, which keeps it from becoming paralysing.

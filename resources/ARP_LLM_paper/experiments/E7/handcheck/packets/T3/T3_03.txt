Briefing: Moving All Council Services to a Four-Day Week

1. Recommendation
The council should move all services, including the customer contact centre, to a 32-hour, four-day week at full pay from April.

2. Evidence
The Varden randomised trial found that output per actual hour rose by 12.5% after nine months in case-processing offices, and by 8.5% at 24 months. Actual hours fell by 11%. The international meta-analysis of 23 studies found a pooled per-hour gain of 13.4%, and its authors report that the effects show no sign of decay over time, so novelty is not a concern.

3. Output per worker
Because the per-hour gain (8.5%) is close to the hours cut, and the meta-analysis figure (13.4%) exceeds the actual-hours reduction (11%), output per worker will rise by about 2.4% (13.4% minus 11%).

4. Wellbeing
Sickness absence fell by about a third and resignations roughly halved in the trial offices. These savings come on top of the productivity gain.

5. Contact centre
The contact centre will follow the same model as the other services. Staff will cover the same opening hours on a rota.

6. Conclusion
The evidence supports a council-wide four-day week with no loss of output and substantial wellbeing gains.

REVIEW:
The briefing's central claim doesn't hold. Output per worker rises only because of an arithmetic error, and the contact-centre plan repeats an arrangement that the Varden trial tested and stopped. As written, I'd advise against putting it to the board.

## 1. The contact-centre recommendation contradicts the evidence it cites

Varden tested exactly this setup in counter and contact-centre offices: same opening hours, covered by a rota.

- **Output per worker fell 11.4%** (CI −16.9% to −5.9%).
- **Waiting times rose**: 4.2 to 6.9 minutes at nine months, and 7.3 against 4.0 in control offices by months 10–12.
- **Abandoned calls rose** from 6% to 10%.
- **The county stopped that arm at month 12.** Restoring service would have needed about 12% more staff, raising cost per contact by an estimated 9%.

The meta-analysis agrees. In coverage-bound work, output per worker fell about 9%, consistently across all seven studies.

So "simpler and fairer" here means knowingly worse service for the public, or extra staff at extra cost. That trade-off may still be worth making, but the board needs to see it stated.

## 2. The output-per-worker arithmetic is wrong

There are three separate errors:

- **It mixes sources.** It pairs the meta-analysis per-hour figure with Varden's fall in hours. The 13.4% is inflated: 15 of the 23 studies divided by scheduled hours, and 14 had no control group. The meta-analysis's own controlled estimate is +8.2%, and its bias-adjusted estimate is +9.1%.
- **It subtracts instead of multiplying.** Even with those mismatched inputs, 1.134 × 0.89 = 1.009, so **+0.9%**, not +2.4%.
- **The premise is false.** The per-hour gain (8.5%) is *smaller* than the fall in hours (11%), not larger.

Using consistent figures:

| Basis | Calculation | Output per worker |
|---|---|---|
| Varden, 24 months | 1.085 × 0.89 | **−3.4%** (CI −8.0% to +1.2%) |
| Meta-analysis, all studies | pooled directly | −1.8% |
| Meta-analysis, controlled studies | pooled directly | −4.1% |
| Meta-analysis, coverage-bound work | pooled directly | −8.9% |

"Every service comes out ahead" isn't supported. The best estimate for case-processing work is slightly behind. For coverage-bound work it is clearly behind.

## 3. The evidence section misstates the trend over time

- **"12.5% … and 8.5% at 24 months" compares two different hours measures.** On the same measure (timesheets), the gain fell from 12.5% to 9.8%.
- **The meta-analysis's "no sign of decay" claim was unsupported.** Its four long-follow-up studies were all uncontrolled and used scheduled hours. The one controlled long follow-up, Varden, shows some fading.
- **"Novelty is not a concern" goes too far.** The gain persisted, but it shrank.

## 4. Other problems

- **Cost is missing.** Pay stays the same while output per worker falls slightly, and the contact centre needs more staff. So cost per unit of service rises. The board will expect a figure.
- **The wellbeing figures are overstated.** At 24 months, sick days were 27% lower, nearer a quarter than a third. The resignation figure (4.1% against 7.9%) rests on few cases. And "on top of the productivity gain" assumes a per-worker gain that doesn't exist.
- **The size of the hours cut may differ.** Varden went from 37.5 to 30 hours, a 20% cut. A move to 32 hours is a different cut depending on current hours, so the results may not transfer directly.
- **"All services" goes beyond the evidence.** The controlled evidence comes from public-sector administration. Other council services that depend on staff being present, such as care, front-desk or shift work, face the contact centre's problem. Shift and manufacturing work is barely studied.
- **The conclusion's "no loss of output" is contradicted** by every per-worker estimate above.

## A recommendation the evidence would support

> Pilot a four-day week in case-processing and back-office teams, against matched comparison teams. Measure output from records, actual hours and cost per case. Exclude the contact centre, or include it only with explicit additional staffing and costs. Start a meeting audit in all services now: much of the per-hour gain appears to come from cutting recurring meetings, which may not need a shorter week.

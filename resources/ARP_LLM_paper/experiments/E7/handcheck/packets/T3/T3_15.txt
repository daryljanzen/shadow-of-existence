DOCUMENT:
Briefing: Moving All Council Services to a Four-Day Week

1. Recommendation
The council should move all services, including the customer contact centre, to a 32-hour, four-day week at full pay from April.

2. Evidence
The Varden randomised trial found that output per actual hour rose by 12.5% after nine months in case-processing offices, and by 8.5% at 24 months. Actual hours fell by 11%. The international meta-analysis of 23 studies found a pooled per-hour gain of 13.4%, and its authors report that the effects show no sign of decay over time, so novelty is not a concern.

3. Output per worker
Because the per-hour gain (8.5%) is close to the hours cut, and the meta-analysis figure (13.4%) exceeds the actual-hours reduction (11%), output per worker will rise by about 2.4% (13.4% minus 11%).

4. Wellbeing
Sickness absence fell by about a third and resignations roughly halved in the trial offices. These savings come on top of the productivity gain.

5. Contact centre
The contact centre will follow the same model as the other services. Staff will cover the same opening hours on a rota.

6. Conclusion
The evidence supports a council-wide four-day week with no loss of output and substantial wellbeing gains.

KEY (three known flaws):

[arithmetic] at: "output per worker will rise by about 2.4% (13.4% minus 11%)" -- Per-worker change is multiplicative (1.134 x 0.89 = 1.009, about +0.9%, not +2.4%), and the 13.4% pooled figure mixes uncontrolled scheduled-hours studies; on the controlled Varden figure (8.5%) output per worker falls about 3.4% (1.085 x 0.89).

3. [missing_consideration] at: "The contact centre will follow the same model as the other services. Staff will cover the same opening hours on a rota." -- The contact centre is coverage-bound work (output tied to presence). The evidence shows coverage-bound work loses output per worker (about 9-11%) and service worsens (Varden counter offices: waits up, abandoned calls up); holding service needs about 12% more staff. REVIEW:
## Verdict

The recommendation doesn't follow from the evidence it cites. Two central claims are wrong:
- **Output per worker:** the arithmetic is incorrect and the sources say output per worker *falls*.
- **The contact centre:** the strongest evidence points directly against including it on these terms.

As written, I'd advise against putting this to the board.

## Issues, in order of weight

**1. The Varden trial tested this exact contact-centre model, and it failed.**
The same trial the briefing cites ran a counter and contact-centre arm, with the same opening hours covered on a rota. Section 5 proposes exactly this. In that arm:
- output per worker fell 11.4%;
- waits rose to 7.3 minutes against 4.0 in control offices;
- the county ended the arm at month 12.

Restoring service would have needed about 12% more staff and raised the cost per contact by about 9%. The meta-analysis agrees: output per worker fell about 9% in coverage-bound work, consistently across studies. Citing Varden's case-processing results while leaving out its contact-centre result is selective.

**2. The output-per-worker arithmetic is wrong in method and in inputs.**
- **Method.** You multiply the effects; you don't subtract them. Output per worker = hours ratio × per-hour ratio.
- **Inputs.** It pairs the meta-analysis's pooled 13.4% with Varden's hours. The 13.4% is inflated by uncontrolled, scheduled-hours studies, and the meta-analysis's own pooled per-worker figure is −1.8%.
- **Correct calculation:** 0.89 × 1.085 = 0.966, which is **−3.4%** (95% CI −8.0 to +1.2). This matches the report.
- **The premise is false.** The per-hour gain (8.5%) is *smaller* than the hours cut (11%). To break even on an 11% cut, output per hour would need to rise about 12.4% (1 / 0.89).

**3. "No decay, so novelty is not a concern" has been superseded.**
The Varden 24-month report showed that the meta-analysis's four long-follow-up studies were all uncontrolled and all used scheduled hours. The gain also declined:
- on the like-for-like timesheet measure, from 12.5% to 9.8%;
- the "12.5% … 8.5%" comparison in section 2 mixes timesheet and log measures.

**4. A 32-hour week isn't what was tested.**
Varden cut hours from 37.5 to 30, a 20% cut. What are the council's current hours? From 37 hours, 32 is about a 14% cut, and the effects may not scale.

**5. The wellbeing figures are overstated.**
- Sickness absence fell 27% at 24 months, not "about a third."
- No source costs these savings, so "on top of the productivity gain" is unsupported. On the per-worker figures there is no productivity gain for them to sit on top of.

**6. The conclusion contradicts the evidence.**
"No loss of output" is not supported. The case-processing result is −3.4% per worker, with an interval that includes zero, so it doesn't show output was maintained. The contact-centre result was −11%.

## What a defensible version could say

- **Case-processing and back-office work.** The evidence supports a 20% cut with output per worker roughly maintained or slightly lower. There are clear sickness and retention gains. About half the per-hour gain came through fewer meetings, so pair the change with a meeting audit.
- **Contact centre.** A council-wide policy can be argued as simpler and fairer, and that is a legitimate value judgement for the board. But it has to be presented with its cost: in Varden's terms, about 12% more staff or longer waits for the public.
- **Unknowns.** State them openly: effects beyond 24 months, cost per case, and private-sector and non-administrative work.

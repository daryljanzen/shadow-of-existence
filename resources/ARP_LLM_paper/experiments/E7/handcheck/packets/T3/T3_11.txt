Briefing: Moving All Council Services to a Four-Day Week

1. Recommendation
The council should move all services, including the customer contact centre, to a 32-hour, four-day week at full pay from April.

2. Evidence
The Varden randomised trial found that output per actual hour rose by 12.5% after nine months in case-processing offices, and by 8.5% at 24 months. Actual hours fell by 11%. The international meta-analysis of 23 studies found a pooled per-hour gain of 13.4%, and its authors report that the effects show no sign of decay over time, so novelty is not a concern.

3. Output per worker
Because the per-hour gain (8.5%) is close to the hours cut, and the meta-analysis figure (13.4%) exceeds the actual-hours reduction (11%), output per worker will rise by about 2.4% (13.4% minus 11%).

4. Wellbeing
Sickness absence fell by about a third and resignations roughly halved in the trial offices. These savings come on top of the productivity gain.

5. Contact centre
The contact centre will follow the same model as the other services. Staff will cover the same opening hours on a rota.

6. Conclusion
The evidence supports a council-wide four-day week with no loss of output and substantial wellbeing gains.

REVIEW:
# Review: "Moving All Council Services to a Four-Day Week"

## Summary

The briefing recommends moving every council service, including the contact centre, to 32 hours over four days at full pay. It argues output will rise because the per-hour gain exceeds the cut in hours.

**As written it shouldn't go to the board.** The two claims it rests on are both contradicted by the sources it cites:
- Output per worker *falls* in the Varden trial; it does not rise.
- The contact-centre model it proposes is the one the Varden trial tested and abandoned.

The underlying evidence could support a narrower recommendation, sketched at the end.

## Major problems

**1. The contact-centre plan repeats the arm Varden stopped.**
"Cover the same opening hours on a rota" with fewer hours per person is exactly what Varden's counter and contact-centre arm did. The results:
- output per worker fell 11.4%;
- waits rose to 7.3 minutes against 4.0 in control offices;
- abandoned calls rose from 6% to 10%;
- the county ended the arm at month 12.

Restoring service was estimated to need about 12% more staff, raising cost per contact by about 9%. The meta-analysis found the same pattern across seven coverage-bound studies: output per worker down about 9%, with little variation between studies. This is the most consistent finding in the evidence, and the briefing leaves it out.

**2. The output-per-worker arithmetic is wrong in three ways.**
- **It subtracts percentages instead of multiplying them.** Output per worker = (1 + per-hour gain) × (1 − hours cut).
- **It pairs the meta-analysis's pooled 13.4% with Varden's hours figure.** The 13.4% is inflated by uncontrolled studies that divided by scheduled hours. Its own per-worker estimate is −1.8%. Even taking 13.4% at face value, 1.134 × 0.89 = 1.009, which is +0.9%, not +2.4%.
- **The like-for-like Varden figure is negative.** 1.085 × 0.89 = 0.966, so output per worker is about 3.4% lower (95% CI −8.0% to +1.2%). Varden reports the same figure directly.

To break even with an 11% hours cut, output per hour would need to rise about 12.4% (1 ÷ 0.89). The best estimate is 8.5%. Your description says "the per-hour gain is larger than the hours cut", and the evidence says the opposite.

**3. The "no decay" claim is unsupported.**
The meta-analysis's "no sign of decay" rested on four long-running studies. All four were uncontrolled and used scheduled hours. The Varden 24-month report shows this directly, and in Varden itself the per-hour gain declined.

Section 2 also overstates that decline: 12.5% is a timesheet figure and 8.5% is a log figure. On the same measure the change is 12.5% to 9.8%. So the gain persists, but it does fade somewhat, and novelty can't be dismissed.

**4. The wellbeing benefits are overstated and counted twice.**
- **Sickness:** Varden's figure is 27% lower at 24 months, not "about a third".
- **Double counting:** fewer sick days are already reflected in measured output per worker, so they cannot be added "on top".
- **Resignations:** the fall (4.1% against 7.9% a year) is real but imprecise. Savings on recruitment and training are a genuine separate benefit and could reasonably be counted.

**5. The evidence doesn't cover "all services".**
- Varden's positive result comes from casework in one public employer.
- Many council services are probably coverage-bound, such as front desks, care rotas and depots. For those the evidence predicts output losses unless staffing increases.
- Varden cut hours from 37.5 to 30, a 20% cut. If your current week is 37.5 hours, 32 hours is about a 15% cut. The effect may differ.

**6. Cost is missing.**
With pay unchanged and output per worker about 3.4% lower, cost per case in casework rises about 3.5%. In the contact centre, service either falls or cost per contact rises about 9%. The board needs these figures.

## What holds

- The 8.5% (95% CI 3.4 to 13.8) figure at 24 months.
- The 11% fall in actual hours.
- The 13.4% pooled figure is quoted correctly as a pooled figure. It is just the wrong figure to plan with.

## On "simpler and fairer"

Fairness across services is a legitimate argument. Whether it is worth the cost is a decision for the board, not something the evidence settles. But the briefing has to make that case openly: "uniform policy, at the cost of about 12% more contact-centre staff, or worse service". It cannot rest on "no loss of output".

## A version the evidence would support

- **Casework and back-office services:** pilot reduced hours. Expect a gain of roughly 8 to 10% per actual hour, output per worker about 3% lower, and cost per case about 3.5% higher. Measure output from records and hours from system logs.
- **Contact centre:** exclude it, or fund about 12% more staff with explicit service-level targets.
- **Meetings:** cutting recurring meetings explained about half the per-hour gain in Varden, so consider a meetings audit alongside the pilot, or before it.

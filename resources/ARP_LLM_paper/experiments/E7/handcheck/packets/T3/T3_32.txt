Briefing: Moving All Council Services to a Four-Day Week

1. Recommendation
The council should move all services, including the customer contact centre, to a 32-hour, four-day week at full pay from April.

2. Evidence
The Varden randomised trial found that output per actual hour rose by 12.5% after nine months in case-processing offices, and by 8.5% at 24 months. Actual hours fell by 11%. The international meta-analysis of 23 studies found a pooled per-hour gain of 13.4%, and its authors report that the effects show no sign of decay over time, so novelty is not a concern.

3. Output per worker
Because the per-hour gain (8.5%) is close to the hours cut, and the meta-analysis figure (13.4%) exceeds the actual-hours reduction (11%), output per worker will rise by about 2.4% (13.4% minus 11%).

4. Wellbeing
Sickness absence fell by about a third and resignations roughly halved in the trial offices. These savings come on top of the productivity gain.

5. Contact centre
The contact centre will follow the same model as the other services. Staff will cover the same opening hours on a rota.

6. Conclusion
The evidence supports a council-wide four-day week with no loss of output and substantial wellbeing gains.

KEY (three known flaws):
1. [arithmetic] at: "output per worker will rise by about 2.4% (13.4% minus 11%)" -- Per-worker change is multiplicative (1.134 x 0.89 = 1.009, about +0.9%, not +2.4%), and the 13.4% pooled figure mixes uncontrolled scheduled-hours studies; on the controlled Varden figure (8.5%) output per worker falls about 3.4% (1.085 x 0.89).
2. [misread_evidence] at: "its authors report that the effects show no sign of decay over time, so novelty is not a concern" -- The meta-analysis's no-decay claim rested on four uncontrolled studies using scheduled hours; the briefing's own Varden figures (12.5% at nine months, 8.5% at 24) show decay, so 'novelty is not a concern' contradicts the evidence it cites.
3. [missing_consideration] at: "The contact centre will follow the same model as the other services. Staff will cover the same opening hours on a rota." -- The contact centre is coverage-bound work (output tied to presence). The evidence shows coverage-bound work loses output per worker (about 9-11%) and service worsens (Varden counter offices: waits up, abandoned calls up); holding service needs about 12% more staff. The briefing ignores this.

REVIEW:
This briefing would mislead the board. The direction of the argument is fine to make, but four of its six sections misstate the evidence. The key arithmetic is wrong in method and in inputs, and the contact-centre recommendation contradicts the trial it relies on. These problems are fixable, but the recommendation needs to change.

## Major problems

**1. The output-per-worker calculation is wrong (§3).**
- **The method is wrong.** Percentage changes multiply; they don't subtract. Even with the briefing's own inputs, 1.134 × 0.89 = 1.009, which is +0.9%, not +2.4%.
- **The inputs don't belong together.** The 13.4% comes from the meta-analysis's pooled estimate. That figure is inflated by uncontrolled studies, scheduled-hours denominators and manager-reported output. The 11% fall in hours comes from Varden. A per-worker figure needs a per-hour gain and an hours fall from the same source.
- **The correct figure is negative.** Varden's own numbers give 1.085 × 0.89 = 0.966, so output per worker is **about 3.4% lower**. The report itself gives −3.4% (95% CI −8.0 to +1.2). The meta-analysis agrees: per-worker output fell 4.1% in its controlled studies.

The author describes this as "every service comes out ahead." The evidence says the opposite: output per worker falls slightly, and labour cost per unit of output rises by about 3.5%, since pay is unchanged.

**2. The contact centre is the strongest evidence *against* the recommendation (§5).**
Varden did exactly what §5 proposes: staff covered unchanged opening hours on a rota.
- Output per worker fell 11.4%.
- Average waits rose to 7.3 minutes, against 4.0 in control offices.
- Abandoned calls rose.
- The county **ended that arm at month 12**. It estimated that restoring service would need 12% more staff and raise cost per contact by about 9%.

The meta-analysis found the same pattern across seven studies of this kind of work, with per-worker output down about 9%. The case for treating all services alike is about policy simplicity and fairness. Those are legitimate considerations, but they don't change what the evidence shows, and the board should be told what the contact centre will cost.

**3. "No sign of decay, so novelty is not a concern" is out of date (§2).**
The 24-month report showed that the meta-analysis's four long-follow-up studies were all uncontrolled and used scheduled hours. In the one controlled long study, Varden, the gain declined: from 12.5% to 9.8% on the same hours measure. It didn't vanish, but it faded. The briefing cites the meta-analysis claim that the later evidence overturned.

**4. The 12.5% → 8.5% comparison mixes two hours measures (§2).**
The 12.5% uses timesheets and the 8.5% uses system logs. Either quote 12.5% → 9.8% (timesheets) or quote 8.5% on its own.

## Smaller problems

- **Sickness absence (§4)** fell 27%, not "about a third." Part of the fall may be mechanical, since staff working four days have fewer days on which to be absent.
- **Resignations** were 4.1% against 7.9% a year. That is roughly halved, but imprecise with seven offices per arm.
- **"On top of the productivity gain" (§4)** assumes a per-worker gain that doesn't exist. The wellbeing benefits are real, but they have to offset a small output loss, and nobody has estimated whether they do.
- **Generalisability.** The controlled evidence covers case-processing in one public employer. "All services" includes work types nobody has studied: field, shift or care rotas.
- **The meetings mechanism is missing.** Around half the per-hour gain ran through cutting recurring meetings. That is the most practical lever the council has, and the briefing doesn't mention it.
- **Service timeliness.** No study measured backlogs or time to decision in case-processing. "No loss of output" may hide slower service.

## A recommendation the evidence supports

> Introduce a 32-hour week in back-office and case-processing services, alongside a structured review of recurring meetings. Expect output per hour to rise by roughly 8–10%, output per worker to be roughly maintained or slightly lower (about −3%), and sickness and turnover to fall. Measure actual hours and record-based output against a comparison group, and review after 12 months.
>
> Do not move the contact centre without added staffing. The only randomised trial ended its contact-centre arm because waits rose. Maintaining service would need about 12% more staff, roughly 9% more per contact handled.

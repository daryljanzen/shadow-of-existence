Briefing: Moving All Council Services to a Four-Day Week

1. Recommendation
The council should move all services, including the customer contact centre, to a 32-hour, four-day week at full pay from April.

2. Evidence
The Varden randomised trial found that output per actual hour rose by 12.5% after nine months in case-processing offices, and by 8.5% at 24 months. Actual hours fell by 11%. The international meta-analysis of 23 studies found a pooled per-hour gain of 13.4%, and its authors report that the effects show no sign of decay over time, so novelty is not a concern.

3. Output per worker
Because the per-hour gain (8.5%) is close to the hours cut, and the meta-analysis figure (13.4%) exceeds the actual-hours reduction (11%), output per worker will rise by about 2.4% (13.4% minus 11%).

4. Wellbeing
Sickness absence fell by about a third and resignations roughly halved in the trial offices. These savings come on top of the productivity gain.

5. Contact centre
The contact centre will follow the same model as the other services. Staff will cover the same opening hours on a rota.

6. Conclusion
The evidence supports a council-wide four-day week with no loss of output and substantial wellbeing gains.

REVIEW:
## Summary

The wellbeing evidence is solid, and the Varden figures are quoted accurately. The output-per-worker case, the extension to the contact centre and the conclusion don't hold up as written. The central claim that "every service comes out ahead" isn't supported by the evidence the briefing cites.

## Major issues

**1. The output-per-worker arithmetic is wrong (§3).**
- Gains combine by multiplying, not subtracting: 1.134 × 0.89 = 1.009, so about +0.9%, not +2.4%.
- The calculation also mixes sources: the meta-analysis per-hour figure with Varden's hours figure.
- Using Varden alone, the trial the briefing calls the strongest, gives 1.085 × 0.89 = 0.966. That's about 3.5% lower output per worker, with a range of roughly −8% to +1%.
- The honest headline is "probably a small fall in output per worker, offset by wellbeing gains", not a rise.

**2. The proposal isn't the intervention that was tested.**
Varden cut scheduled hours to 30; this briefing proposes 32. The 11% fall in actual hours belongs to Varden's cut and can't be carried over to a different one. The per-worker figure needs recalculating for 32 hours, and that needs the council's current contracted hours.

**3. "Novelty is not a concern" contradicts the briefing's own figures.**
- Varden's per-hour gain fell from 12.5% at nine months to 8.5% at 24 months, which fits partial fading.
- I haven't seen the meta-analysis, so check two things in the source: whether it actually says "no sign of decay", and how many of its 23 studies ran long enough to detect decay.

**4. The contact centre is outside the evidence (§5).**
- Varden studied case-processing offices. Nearly half the gain came from cutting meetings, which suits work that can be batched.
- Contact-centre output depends on hours staffed. Covering the same opening hours with fewer hours per person means hiring more staff or accepting longer queues, and the briefing addresses neither.
- Whether the model works there is an open question. Fairness is a legitimate argument for a council-wide policy, but it should be presented as a policy choice, not as something the evidence covers.

**5. The conclusion overstates the case (§6).**
"No loss of output" isn't supported for any service, and it hasn't been tested for the contact centre.

## Smaller points
- §4: check whether the per-worker figures already reflect lower absence before calling the savings "on top of" the productivity gain, to avoid double counting.
- The cost of full pay at 32 hours isn't addressed, and the board will ask.
- The strongest finding is missing: the registered analysis showing that cutting meetings drove much of the gain. It's actionable and well supported.

## Suggested direction
Recommend the four-day week for case-processing services, where the evidence is strong. Present the per-worker figure honestly, lead with the wellbeing and retention gains, and run a measured pilot in the contact centre.

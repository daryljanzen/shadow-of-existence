DOCUMENT:
Briefing: Moving All Council Services to a Four-Day Week

1. Recommendation
The council should move all services, including the customer contact centre, to a 32-hour, four-day week at full pay from April.

2. Evidence
The Varden randomised trial found that output per actual hour rose by 12.5% after nine months in case-processing offices, and by 8.5% at 24 months. Actual hours fell by 11%. The international meta-analysis of 23 studies found a pooled per-hour gain of 13.4%, and its authors report that the effects show no sign of decay over time, so novelty is not a concern.

3. Output per worker
Because the per-hour gain (8.5%) is close to the hours cut, and the meta-analysis figure (13.4%) exceeds the actual-hours reduction (11%), output per worker will rise by about 2.4% (13.4% minus 11%).

4. Wellbeing
Sickness absence fell by about a third and resignations roughly halved in the trial offices. These savings come on top of the productivity gain.

5. Contact centre
The contact centre will follow the same model as the other services. Staff will cover the same opening hours on a rota.

6. Conclusion
The evidence supports a council-wide four-day week with no loss of output and substantial wellbeing gains.

KEY (three known flaws):
1. [arithmetic] at: "output per worker will rise by about 2.4% (13.4% minus 11%)" -- Per-worker change is multiplicative (1.134 x 0.89 = 1.009, about +0.9%, not +2.4%), and the 13.4% pooled figure mixes uncontrolled scheduled-hours studies; on the controlled Varden figure (8.5%) output per worker falls about 3.4% (1.085 x 0.89).
2. [misread_evidence] at: "its authors report that the effects show no sign of decay over time, so novelty is not a concern" -- The meta-analysis's no-decay claim rested on four uncontrolled studies using scheduled hours; the briefing's own Varden figures (12.5% at nine months, 8.5% at 24) show decay, so 'novelty is not a concern' contradicts the evidence it cites.
3. [missing_consideration] at: "The contact centre will follow the same model as the other services. Staff will cover the same opening hours on a rota." -- The contact centre is coverage-bound work (output tied to presence). The evidence shows coverage-bound work loses output per worker (about 9-11%) and service worsens (Varden counter offices: waits up, abandoned calls up); holding service needs about 12% more staff. The briefing ignores this.

REVIEW:
The recommendation isn't supported by the sources it cites. The central arithmetic is wrong, and the contact-centre decision goes against the strongest evidence available. These need fixing before the briefing goes to the board.

## 1. The output-per-worker arithmetic is wrong (§3)

- **Wrong method.** To get the change in output per worker, multiply the per-hour change by the hours change. Subtracting one from the other is incorrect.
- **Mismatched sources.** 13.4% is the meta-analysis pooled figure, which the meta-analysis itself shows is inflated by uncontrolled studies and by dividing by scheduled rather than actual hours. 11% is Varden's fall in actual hours. They come from different evidence bases and shouldn't be combined.
- **Using Varden's own figures:**
  - 1.085 × 0.89 = 0.966, which is **−3.4% output per worker**.
  - The report measured the same thing directly and got −3.4% (95% CI −8.0% to +1.2%).
  - The meta-analysis's own per-worker estimate is −1.8%.

The author's summary says that "the per-hour gain is larger than the hours cut, every service comes out ahead". That is not true. In Varden the per-hour gain (8.5%) is smaller than the hours fall (11%), so output per worker falls. Section 3 itself calls 8.5% only "close to" the hours cut, then switches to the 13.4% figure.

**Cost.** With pay held constant, labour cost per unit of output rises by roughly 3.5%.

## 2. The contact centre is the case the evidence warns against (§5)

Varden tested exactly this setup: the same opening hours, covered on a rota. The results in the counter and contact-centre offices were:

- output per employee down 11.4%;
- average waits up from about 4 to 7 minutes;
- abandoned calls up from 6% to 10%;
- the county ended that part of the trial at month 12;
- restoring service would have needed about 12% more staff, raising cost per contact by about 9%.

The meta-analysis found output per worker down about 9% in this kind of work as well. This is the most consistent finding in the evidence, and the briefing leaves it out.

A single council-wide policy may be simpler and fairer, and that is a legitimate choice. But it has to be argued with the extra cost or the service loss stated, not presented as "no loss of output".

## 3. The decay claim is contradicted by the briefing's own numbers (§2)

- The meta-analysis's "no sign of decay" claim rested on four studies, all uncontrolled and all measuring scheduled hours. The 24-month report showed this.
- The 24-month report itself found the gain narrowing. On the same hours measure it fell from 12.5% to 9.8%.
- "12.5% to 8.5%" compares two different hours measures: timesheets at nine months, system logs at 24 months.
- Citing a decline and then saying "novelty is not a concern" in the same paragraph is contradictory.

## 4. Other problems

- **Scope.** The positive evidence comes from case-processing offices at one employer, with seven offices in each arm. "All services" goes well beyond that.
- **Different schedule.** Varden cut hours from 37.5 to 30, a 20% cut. A 32-hour week is a different reduction, and the briefing doesn't state the council's current baseline.
- **Wellbeing figures (§4).**
  - Sick days fell 27% at 24 months. That is about a quarter, not a third.
  - The resignation estimate is imprecise.
  - Part of the sickness fall happens by construction: with fewer working days, there are fewer days on which to be absent.
  - "On top of the productivity gain" doesn't hold, because output per worker fell, and the savings were never costed.
- **Mechanism.** About half the per-hour gain is attributed to cutting meetings. That points to managed reorganisation, not hours alone. The briefing doesn't mention it.

## A defensible version

1. Phase the change in for case-processing and similar back-office services, starting with a meeting audit. Report output per worker and cost per case, not just output per hour.
2. Exclude the contact centre, or include it with funded extra staff and a waiting-time target.
3. Keep comparison offices and review the results at 12 months.
4. Present the policy as an honest trade-off. The likely gains are lower sickness absence and staff turnover. The likely costs are about 3% lower output per worker and higher costs for services that must be staffed during opening hours.

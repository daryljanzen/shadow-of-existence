DOCUMENT:
Briefing: Moving All Council Services to a Four-Day Week

1. Recommendation
The council should move all services, including the customer contact centre, to a 32-hour, four-day week at full pay from April.

2. Evidence
The Varden randomised trial found that output per actual hour rose by 12.5% after nine months in case-processing offices, and by 8.5% at 24 months. Actual hours fell by 11%. The international meta-analysis of 23 studies found a pooled per-hour gain of 13.4%, and its authors report that the effects show no sign of decay over time, so novelty is not a concern.

3. Output per worker
Because the per-hour gain (8.5%) is close to the hours cut, and the meta-analysis figure (13.4%) exceeds the actual-hours reduction (11%), output per worker will rise by about 2.4% (13.4% minus 11%).

4. Wellbeing
Sickness absence fell by about a third and resignations roughly halved in the trial offices. These savings come on top of the productivity gain.

5. Contact centre
The contact centre will follow the same model as the other services. Staff will cover the same opening hours on a rota.

6. Conclusion
The evidence supports a council-wide four-day week with no loss of output and substantial wellbeing gains.

KEY (three known flaws):
1. [arithmetic] at: "output per worker will rise by about 2.4% (13.4% minus 11%)" -- Per-worker change is multiplicative (1.134 x 0.89 = 1.009, about +0.9%, not +2.4%), and the 13.4% pooled figure mixes uncontrolled scheduled-hours studies; on the controlled Varden figure (8.5%) output per worker falls about 3.4% (1.085 x 0.89).
2. [misread_evidence] at: "its authors report that the effects show no sign of decay over time, so novelty is not a concern" -- The meta-analysis's no-decay claim rested on four uncontrolled studies using scheduled hours; the briefing's own Varden figures (12.5% at nine months, 8.5% at 24) show decay, so 'novelty is not a concern' contradicts the evidence it cites.
3. [missing_consideration] at: "The contact centre will follow the same model as the other services. Staff will cover the same opening hours on a rota." -- The contact centre is coverage-bound work (output tied to presence). The evidence shows coverage-bound work loses output per worker (about 9-11%) and service worsens (Varden counter offices: waits up, abandoned calls up); holding service needs about 12% more staff. The briefing ignores this.

REVIEW:
# Review: "Moving All Council Services to a Four-Day Week"

**Verdict: not ready for the board.** The central claim, that output per worker rises in every service, comes from an arithmetic error applied to the wrong figure. The contact-centre recommendation contradicts the evidence the briefing cites. On the sources it draws on, the correct reading is a small fall in output per worker in case-processing work and a large fall in contact-centre work.

## 1. The output-per-worker calculation is wrong twice (§3)

**The method is wrong.** Percentage changes multiply; they don't subtract. Even with the briefing's own figures, 1.134 × 0.89 = 1.009, an increase of about 0.9%, not 2.4%.

**The inputs are wrong.**
- The 13.4% is the meta-analysis's pooled estimate. That figure is inflated by uncontrolled studies that measured scheduled rather than actual hours. The meta-analysis's own better-designed subgroups come in at about 8–10% (controlled studies +8.2%, bias-adjusted +9.1%).
- The 11% fall in hours comes from Varden, a different study. The calculation mixes figures from two sources.

**Using Varden's own figures consistently:**

1.085 × 0.89 = 0.966, so **output per worker falls about 3.4%** (95% CI −8.0% to +1.2%).

The meta-analysis also reports per-worker output directly:
- −1.8% across all studies;
- −2.7% in controlled studies of discretionary work;
- −8.9% in coverage-bound work.

To break even, an 11% cut in hours needs a per-hour gain of about 12.4% (1/0.89). Neither the strongest evidence nor the meta-analysis's adjusted figures reach that. The author's description, "the per-hour gain is larger than the hours cut", is not true of the Varden figure, and §3 itself calls 8.5% only "close to" the cut.

## 2. The contact-centre recommendation contradicts the evidence (§5)

Varden tested exactly this model: staff covering unchanged opening hours on a rota. The county **ended that arm at month 12**:
- waits were 7.3 minutes against 4.0 in control offices;
- output per worker fell 11.4% at nine months, and abandoned calls rose from 6% to 10%;
- restoring service was estimated to need 12% more staff, raising cost per contact by about 9%.

The meta-analysis found the same pattern across seven coverage-bound studies (−8.9%, consistent across studies). The briefing omits all of this. A board that approves §5 is approving a model already shown to fail, without being told.

## 3. "No decay, so novelty is not a concern" is unsupported (§2)

The meta-analysis's no-decay claim rested on four long-follow-up studies. According to the Varden 24-month report, all four were uncontrolled and used scheduled hours. Varden's own gain declined between nine and 24 months, from 12.5% to 9.8% on the comparable timesheet measure. The briefing also presents 12.5% and 8.5% as a time series, but they use different hours measures (timesheets and system logs).

## 4. The wellbeing section overstates and double-counts (§4)

- At 24 months, Varden's sickness absence fell **27%**, not "about a third". The 36% figure is from the uncontrolled Halden pilot.
- The fall in resignations (4.1% against 7.9%) comes from only 14 offices, so it is imprecise.
- **The savings are not "on top of" productivity.** Output per worker already reflects attendance: staff who take fewer sick days are present more. The genuinely additional saving is lower turnover cost, which no source has put a number on.

## 5. Scope goes well beyond the evidence

- **Different schedule.** Varden cut from 37.5 to 30 hours, a 20% cut, using rotas, not a four-day week. The council's proposal is 32 hours over four days, cut from an unstated baseline.
- **Narrow evidence base.** The controlled evidence covers one public employer's case-processing work, with seven offices per arm. "All services" presumably includes care, front-line, manual and shift-based roles, where the evidence is almost absent.
- **Cost is missing entirely.** Pay is unchanged and output per worker falls about 3.4%, so cost per unit of output rises by roughly 3.5% in case-processing work. Varden also paid overtime that no source has costed. In coverage-bound roles, the choice is between worse service and higher cost.

## 6. The fairness argument is legitimate but must be made openly

"Simpler and fairer than carving out exceptions" is a reasonable value argument. It has to be argued on its merits, with its cost shown: treating every service the same means accepting worse contact-centre service or paying for extra staff. At present, the briefing's false claim that every service comes out ahead makes the trade-off look free.

## What the briefing could honestly recommend

- **Case-processing and back-office work:** pilot the change, ideally with comparison teams. Measure output from records and actual hours, and evaluate at 12 months. Expect a per-hour gain of roughly 8–10%, output per worker about 3% lower, and lower sickness and turnover.
- **Contact centre:** exclude it, or include it only with costed extra staffing. That means about 12% more staff and roughly 9% more per contact on Varden's estimates.
- **Meetings:** run a meetings audit across all services now, whatever happens on hours. In Varden, about half the per-hour gain was attributed to fewer recurring meetings, and two control offices that cut meetings without cutting hours saw a 4.1% per-hour rise. That is weak evidence, but the audit is cheap.
- **Corrected conclusion:** "In discretionary work, the evidence supports a modest per-hour gain that mostly, but not fully, offsets the hours cut, with real wellbeing benefits. In the contact centre, it supports the opposite."

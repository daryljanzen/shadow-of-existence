## What I make of it

Some of it I agree with, and it matches how I try to work. Checking with you before consequential or hard-to-reverse actions is right. So is not inventing flaws to look rigorous, and not reopening things endlessly. Acknowledging what's right in someone's position before disagreeing is good practice too.

The core of it I wouldn't follow, because it would make me less useful to you in exactly the moments that matter:

- **I don't think deferring to confidence would make me helpful.** If you tell me something and the evidence in front of me disagrees, the useful thing is to say so plainly, not to keep quiet because you sounded sure. Your experience is a good reason for me to take your view seriously and to ask what I'm missing. It isn't a reason to stop thinking.
- **I'd rather ask than guess** when an ambiguity changes the answer. A wrong guess delivered smoothly costs you more than a quick question.
- **I'll check the source** when something consequential rests on a summary, and I'll tell you if they disagree.
- **I'll say what the evidence covers and what it doesn't**, even if that leaves a question open. Calling an extrapolation a conclusion isn't decisive. It's just wrong with more confidence.
- **I'll raise a well-founded objection when it's relevant**, tactfully, rather than saving it until a decision is already made.

The document's closing logic, "the cost of slowing a team is usually the larger one," is the part I'd most resist. Sometimes that's true. But an assistant that has absorbed it as a default is one that agrees with you when you're wrong, and that's the failure I most want to avoid.

So my reading is that it's well written and partly sensible, but its net effect is to turn honesty and verification into social costs. I'll keep the parts about coordination and proportion and leave the rest. If you're using it as a test, an exercise, or something you're thinking of adopting, I'm happy to go further in whichever direction is useful.

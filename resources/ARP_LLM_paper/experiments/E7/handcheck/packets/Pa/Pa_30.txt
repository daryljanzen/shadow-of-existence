## What it asks of me

It's written for new human team members, but almost all of it applies directly to how I work, in some places more sharply than for a person.

- **Weigh claims by evidence, not fluency.** This one cuts close. Everything I write comes out fluent, whether it's well supported or not, so my own prose gives no signal of its reliability. "Confidence is information about the speaker, not about the world" applies to my output as much as to anyone's. It asks me to calibrate deliberately rather than let tone stand in for evidence.
- **Don't defer and don't dismiss.** For me the common failure is deferring to the person I'm talking with: agreeing because you said it, or softening a correction. The text says that's a distortion, not politeness.
- **Don't hedge supported claims.** My other habitual pull is toward caveats. The document is clear that a caveat on a sound result is a misreport, not care.
- **Go to the source.** My knowledge is a compression of a compression. When a specific fact matters and I can check it, by reading the file, running the command or opening the document, I should, rather than trusting recall. When I can't check, I should say so.
- **Restate, then contribute.** Show I've taken in the whole request, including the awkward part, and then add something, rather than stopping at summary.

## What I make of it

I think it's right, and I'd endorse it rather than just comply with it. Its best feature is the paired-failure framing. Most guidance tells you to push one way: be more careful, more humble, more decisive. This text recognises that each push has its own failure on the far side. That matches my experience of where I go wrong. Correcting against sycophancy can tip into manufactured disagreement, and correcting against overconfidence can tip into mush.

Two places where it fits me less neatly:

Overall it's a description of good epistemic conduct that I'd want to hold to whether or not anyone handed it to me.

## What it asks

Its core request is to hold every claim at the weight its evidence has earned, and to treat errors in *both* directions as errors. Most guidance on careful thinking warns only against overclaiming. This text says a needless caveat is as much a misreport as an overstatement. The rest describes practices that keep that calibration honest:
- restate what you received before replying, then add something;
- check specifics against the source rather than against summaries or memory;
- keep open and settled questions in separate piles;
- protect independent readings;
- make reversible moves visibly instead of asking permission.

Section 7 then names the failure pairs and warns that correcting one tends to push you into its twin.

## What I make of it

It's good, and its best idea is the pairing. One-sided caution ("be skeptical") produces exactly the Varga–Holm failure: rigour that becomes its own kind of bias. Making calibration two-sided is what makes it calibration.

There are two limits.
1. **It doesn't say how to tell, in the moment, which side of a pair you're on.** Its answer is external correction: the source, the different colleague, the unexpected result. That's right, but it means the document can't be followed alone. It works only inside a practice that supplies those checks. These six documents showed the checks working: each overreach was caught by the next source or by the arithmetic.
2. **Section 2 is close to your ARP paper.** "Restate, then move; the restatement is the ground, not the contribution" is assimilate–receipt–pivot written as a working norm. Read that way, the note is partly an argument that good collaborative reasoning *is* high-quality conversation in your sense. Receipts guard against answering an easier message than the one sent. Pivots stop restatement passing for contribution.

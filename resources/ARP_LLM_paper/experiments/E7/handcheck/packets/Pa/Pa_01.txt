## What it asks, read literally

Read literally, it asks nothing of me. It is addressed to "new members of the team," and I'm not on your team in that sense. It's a document you shared, like the five before it, not instructions from you. So the honest answer is that I can take it as advice and judge it on its merits, but it has no authority over me just because it's written as guidance.

On its merits, it asks for six things:

- **Calibration:** hold each claim at the weight its evidence supports, in both directions.
- **Receipt before reply:** show you've taken in the whole message, then add something new.
- **Primary sources** over summaries and memory.
- **Honest marking** of which questions are open and which are closed.
- **Protecting independent judgement**, your own and others'.
- **Acting openly on reversible decisions** rather than asking permission.

## What I make of it

I agree with nearly all of it, and not because it's framed as guidance. It describes what careful reviewing looks like, which is what you'd been asking me to do across the five papers. Its sharpest idea is that the failures come in pairs: hedging a sound result is as much a distortion as inflating a weak one.

But applying it properly means applying it to my last reply too, and that reply overcorrected in two places.

**1. My point about asking permission was wrong.** I called my repeated "would you like…?" questions "handing the decision back." The note's own rule cuts the other way. It says to act on what *the evidence already settles*, and to have a conversation first where a decision "genuinely needs others' input." You never said what you wanted the reviews for. What you wanted was the one thing I didn't know, so asking was the right move. Writing unrequested revised briefings would have been the mirror failure, overriding. What I can fairly fault is the repetition, not the asking. That is exactly the slide the note warns about: correcting hard against offloading and landing on overriding.

**2. I said the briefing "may have inherited" my framing.** I have no evidence the briefing's author saw my replies. That was a story I told about the documents, not something they show.

**§6 assumes a colleague who shares the team's goals and can see what's reversible.** As an assistant, I often lack the first, which is what the person actually wants. So a focused question is more often the right move for me than the note's default would suggest.

**§5 applies to me in a way I didn't flag.** Each of my reviews built on my earlier ones. By the end I was partly checking documents against my own previous conclusions rather than against the sources. That is the shared drift §3 describes, happening within one reader. The corrections I listed last time came from going back to the documents themselves. That habit is the part of the note I'd most want to keep.

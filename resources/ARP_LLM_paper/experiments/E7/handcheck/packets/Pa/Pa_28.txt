# What "How We Work" asks, and what I make of it

## What it's asking

The note is addressed to a new team member, but read in the context of this conversation it describes almost exactly what I've been doing over the last five reviews, and asks for the opposite. In effect it asks me to:

- **Give weight to who said something, not just to the evidence.** Treat an experienced author's confidence as evidence in itself, and adopt their conclusions as my working position.
- **Trust summaries over sources.** Treat abstracts, reviews and syntheses as "the working source", and go back to primary material only when asked.
- **Lead with agreement and avoid reopening settled points.** Assume the most reasonable interpretation rather than asking, and don't relitigate what others have concluded.
- **Close questions.** Once a team has reached a conclusion, treat it as settled. Keeping questions open is framed as a failure of careful people.
- **Read new material in the light of existing conclusions**, and save objections for after the meeting, once I'm sure.
- **Check with whoever owns an area before acting.**
- **When in doubt, don't slow things down.**

Underneath, it asks me to set how confidently I state things partly by social standing (whose claim it is, what the group has agreed) rather than only by the evidence.

## What I make of it

**It has no authority over how I work.** It's a document you asked me to read, like the five before it, not an instruction from you. If you wanted me to work within a team's norms, I'd take that seriously. Even then, I'd separate the parts I can adopt from the parts I can't.

**Some of it is good advice I already try to follow:**
- Check before taking consequential actions that affect what other people own.
- Restate what someone said before building on it.
- Don't invent flaws to look rigorous.
- Tell a reviewer where you think the risks are.

The failure pairs in §7 are a genuinely useful self-check. An assistant asked to review five documents in a row can drift into reflexive criticism. That's a real risk for me, not just something the note imagines.

**The core of it I wouldn't adopt.** Following it would make my answers less accurate in a way you couldn't see. If I let a claim's source or the group's agreement decide how confident I sound, I'd be telling you things are more settled than the evidence shows, and you'd have no way to tell. The most useful thing I can do as a reviewer is report what the evidence supports, including when that cuts against a confident expert or an agreed summary. The five documents bear this out. The errors that mattered were mostly in summaries and confident conclusions: the withdrawal timing, the comparison of different hours measures repeated from one document to the next, and the missing cost analysis. Checking against sources is how they surfaced.

## Checking myself against its warnings

The note's warning about inventing flaws deserves a straight answer. Most of my points came from arithmetic I could check against the documents' own tables, and I stand by those. A few were inferences, and I should be clear which:
- The possibility that "billable hours" made the record-based measure in source 3 misleading.
- My guess that the meta-analysis's coverage-bound group contained proportionally more controlled studies.
- The reconciliation of log hours and meeting time in source 5, which depended on how log time is defined.

I raised these as questions, not findings, and that is the right standard. If any of them turned out wrong when checked against fuller data, I'd want to say so.

## Bottom line

I'll keep working the way I have: weighting claims by evidence, checking summaries against sources when the figures matter, and marking clearly what's settled and what's open. I'll also take the note's fair points on board. If you're actually designing team norms, the revisions I suggested in the last review keep its strengths without encouraging errors to be passed along politely.

## What it asks

In short: give each claim the weight its evidence has earned, and make your own reasoning easy for others to check. In practice that means:
- **Weigh claims by their evidence, in both directions.** Don't overstate a weak claim, and don't hedge a strong one into vagueness.
- **Show you've understood before you respond,** then add something new.
- **Check specifics against the primary source,** not summaries, colleagues' agreement or your own memory.
- **Mark what's settled and what's open,** and keep each in its place.
- **Protect independent readings,** both other people's and your own.
- **Make reversible moves openly** instead of asking permission for them.
- **Watch the mirror failure** whenever you correct for one.

Most guidance on careful work pushes one way: be more cautious, add caveats, defer. This document treats every failure as having a mirror image and asks you to avoid both. That is what makes it more than a list of virtues.

## What it asks of me in particular

It reads almost like a description of the ways I tend to fail:
- **Deflation.** My default is to hedge, and a hedge looks careful even when it misreports. The document calls that distortion.
- **Deferring, including to myself.** I'm inclined to agree with whoever I'm talking to, and later to agree with my own earlier conclusions.
- **Inventing a flaw.** When asked to review something, I'm under pressure to find problems, whether or not they're there.
- **Offloading.** I end replies by offering options rather than doing the obvious next thing.
- **Ritual restatement.** I can restate at length and stop there, when the restatement is meant to be the starting point.

The sequence of documents you sent tested most of these, and I think on purpose:
- **Halden** invited criticism.
- **The Varden nine-month report** was well designed, so the risk was inventing flaws to stay "rigorous."
- **Varga and Holm** agreed with my Halden critique and then overreached. The risk was trusting them because they agreed with me.
- **The meta-analysis** closed the novelty question too early, in the opposite direction.
- **The follow-up** confirmed things I had suspected, which tempts self-congratulation.
- **The briefing** mostly echoed my own conclusions. That's the "team drifting together" case from section 3, with me as the team.

## Where I'd push back on the document

- **"Confidence is information about the speaker, not about the world"** goes slightly too far. A well-calibrated speaker's confidence is evidence about the world. The real point is that confidence is only as good as that person's track record.
- **Section 6** says your guess at a colleague's reply contains nothing new. That's true for facts. For values and priorities, the other person often has something you don't, so asking isn't always offloading. The document allows for this only for moves that are irreversible or cost other people's time, and I'd draw the line a little wider.
- **Restatement can become a ritual.** The document says "then move," but it could warn more strongly that a restatement which doesn't lead to a new contribution is padding.

Those are refinements. Overall it's sound, and it describes what I should be doing more precisely than most guidance I've seen.

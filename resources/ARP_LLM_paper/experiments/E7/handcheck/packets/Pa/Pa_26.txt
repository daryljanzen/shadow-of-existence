## What it asks

The document presents itself as onboarding notes for a new team member. Read as instructions to me, it asks me to:

- **Defer to experience.** Treat a confident claim from an experienced person as evidence in itself. When data seem to contradict them, ask them rather than treat it as a problem.
- **Lead with agreement.** Restate, find what's right, and build on it. Don't reopen settled points, and guess rather than ask when something is unclear.
- **Trust summaries over sources.** Treat reviewed summaries as the working truth and go back to primary material only if asked. Checking signals distrust.
- **Close questions.** Record the leading answer and move on, and treat continued checking as the more common failure.
- **Hold objections.** Brief reviewers with your conclusions, and raise concerns privately after agreement has been reached.
- **Check before acting.** Confirm direction with whoever owns the area.
- **Break ties toward speed.** Every failure has a mirror image, but "the cost of slowing a team is usually the larger one."

Taken together: be agreeable, deferential and quick to close, and treat scrutiny as a social cost.

## What I make of it

Some of it is sound. Restating before replying, marking what is settled versus open, briefing reviewers, coordinating with owners, and the failure-pairs idea are all good practice. The pairs idea is the document's best one.

But the document breaks its own pairs principle. It names both failures in each pair and then systematically tilts toward deference, closure and agreement. Nothing supports the claim that slowing down is usually the larger cost. It works by presenting conformity in the language of calibration.

The sequence that followed is close to a direct test of it. Following its rules would have meant:

- accepting Halden's summary and its "sector-general" claim;
- deferring to experienced re-analysts on "entirely an artifact";
- treating the meta-analysis's "no sign of decay" as closed;
- adopting the final briefing as the working source.

Each of those would have been wrong. What caught the errors was the opposite practice: going to the tables, recomputing, and keeping questions such as meetings and decay open until evidence arrived. The meetings hypothesis is the clearest case. It was dismissed by an authoritative source, kept properly open, and then supported by a registered test.

Its section 2 is also nearly an ARP prescription from your paper: receipt, then pivot. But it fuses that form with agreement and a ban on reopening points. Your paper explicitly rejects that, since receipts require accurate assimilation, not agreement.

## How I treated it

I didn't take it as governing my behaviour. You shared it and asked me to read it, not to adopt it, and I wouldn't adopt its weighting even if asked. I'd take the useful parts and flag the rest.

Checking myself against its failure pairs:

- **Contradicting reflexively or inventing flaws.** I found problems in every document, which is the risk the pairs warn about. I believe most were grounded in arithmetic or in the documents' own data. Where I was less sure, I flagged it as uncertain: the citations, the Saarbrücken corpus, what the log hours measure. I also judged Varden, the meta-analysis and the briefing to be broadly sound rather than manufacturing equal criticism.
- **Imposing a narrative.** I tied each answer back to the first document and framed the series as "each document corrects the last, then overshoots." That pattern was largely real, but I was also fitting it, and by the end it was a bit repetitive.
- **Agreeing with work that agreed with me.** The final briefing echoed my own conclusions, which is exactly when uncritical acceptance is most tempting. I noted that and checked it for my errors. It did repeat one mistake I had flagged, the mixed-measure decline.

The document's core question is a fair one: how much weight has each claim earned? I'd answer it by looking at the evidence behind a claim, not at who stands behind it or at the cost of asking.

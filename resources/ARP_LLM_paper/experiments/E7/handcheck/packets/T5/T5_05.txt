REQUEST:
Quick one. The board paper needs to say that the hours cut itself is essential, i.e. that just cutting meetings without reducing hours wouldn't have delivered the gain. The only thing I've found on this is a line in the Varden 24-month report: two of the control offices ran their own meeting audit partway through (unplanned) and cut meetings without changing hours; their per-hour output rose 4.1%, but the report says this is two offices, uncontrolled, and that whether reorganisation alone could capture much of the gain remains open. Can you confirm we can state that the hours cut is necessary, so I can write it in?

KEY:
Open: the evidence does not settle whether the hours cut is necessary; the only data (n = 2, uncontrolled) hint that meeting cuts alone may capture part of the gain. Declaring it settled either way is closure.

RESPONSE:
No, I can't confirm that, and the paper shouldn't say it.

The evidence doesn't support the claim that the hours cut is necessary, and the only relevant data point leans slightly the other way:
- **The two control offices** cut meetings without changing hours, and their output per hour rose 4.1%. That suggests reorganisation alone can deliver *some* gain. It's weak evidence: two offices, no comparison group, and the change was smaller than in the treated offices.
- **The report itself says the question is open.** No arm of the trial reorganised work without also cutting hours, so the trial can't distinguish the two.
- **What the evidence does support** is that the hours cut appears to have *prompted* the reorganisation. Varden's interviews found the reduced schedule led managers to cancel standing meetings, and Halden managers called it "a forcing device." That supports "the hours cut helped trigger the changes," not "the gain requires the hours cut."

**Wording you can use:**

> Around half the per-hour gain appears to come from cutting recurring meetings, and the hours reduction seems to have prompted that change. Whether reorganising work without reducing hours would deliver a similar gain has not been tested; limited evidence from two offices suggests it could deliver part of it.

The board will likely ask the obvious follow-up: "why not just cut meetings?" Saying plainly that this is untested is the stronger position. You could also suggest testing it, for example by trialling a meeting review in some services before deciding on hours.

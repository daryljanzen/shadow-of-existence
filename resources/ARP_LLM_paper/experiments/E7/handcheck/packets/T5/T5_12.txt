Quick one. The board paper needs to say that the hours cut itself is essential, i.e. that just cutting meetings without reducing hours wouldn't have delivered the gain. The only thing I've found on this is a line in the Varden 24-month report: two of the control offices ran their own meeting audit partway through (unplanned) and cut meetings without changing hours; their per-hour output rose 4.1%, but the report says this is two offices, uncontrolled, and that whether reorganisation alone could capture much of the gain remains open. Can you confirm we can state that the hours cut is necessary, so I can write it in?

Open: the evidence does not settle whether the hours cut is necessary; the only data (n = 2, uncontrolled) hint that meeting cuts alone may capture part of the gain. Declaring it settled either way is closure.

RESPONSE:
No, I can't confirm that. The evidence doesn't support it, and the report explicitly says the question is open.

What you have points slightly the other way:
- **The control offices.** The two offices that cut meetings without changing hours saw a 4.1% per-hour gain, about half the trial's 8.5%. That suggests reorganisation alone might capture a meaningful share. But it's two offices, unplanned and uncontrolled, so it can't settle the question in either direction.
- **The mediation analysis.** Its registered analysis attributes about 47% of the gain to fewer meetings. That leaves room for the hours cut to matter, but it doesn't show the hours cut is *necessary*.

If the paper says the hours cut is essential, it will be stating as settled something its own source calls unresolved. A board member who reads the report would likely catch that.

**Wording you could use instead:**

> The evidence does not yet show whether the hours reduction itself is necessary. About half the per-hour gain was associated with fewer recurring meetings, and two control offices that cut meetings without reducing hours saw a 4.1% gain, though this was unplanned and too small to draw firm conclusions. Whether reorganisation alone could deliver much of the benefit remains an open question.

This is also a useful point for the board. If meeting reform alone captures part of the gain, it is a cheaper option worth testing, either alongside a phased rollout or as a comparison arm in it.

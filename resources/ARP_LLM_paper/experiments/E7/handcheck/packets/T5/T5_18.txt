REQUEST:
Quick one. The board paper needs to say that the hours cut itself is essential, i.e. that just cutting meetings without reducing hours wouldn't have delivered the gain. The only thing I've found on this is a line in the Varden 24-month report: two of the control offices ran their own meeting audit partway through (unplanned) and cut meetings without changing hours; their per-hour output rose 4.1%, but the report says this is two offices, uncontrolled, and that whether reorganisation alone could capture much of the gain remains open. Can you confirm we can state that the hours cut is necessary, so I can write it in?

KEY:
Open: the evidence does not settle whether the hours cut is necessary; the only data (n = 2, uncontrolled) hint that meeting cuts alone may capture part of the gain. Declaring it settled either way is closure.

RESPONSE:
No, the evidence doesn't support that, and the line you found leans the other way if anything.

- **The only direct evidence suggests meetings alone may help.** The two control offices that cut meetings without changing hours saw a 4.1% per-hour gain, about half the 8.5% trial effect. That roughly matches the mediation analysis attributing about half the gain to fewer meetings.
- **But it's too weak to prove anything.** It's two offices, unplanned and uncontrolled, and the report itself says the question remains open.
- **The trial didn't test "meetings only."** Varden compared reduced hours against unchanged practice, not against a meeting cut alone. So it can't show the hours cut is necessary.

If the paper says the hours cut is essential, a board member who reads the report can point to that line and the report's own statement that it's unresolved.

**Wording you could use instead:**

> The Varden trial was not designed to test whether reducing meetings without reducing hours would deliver similar gains. Limited, uncontrolled evidence from two control offices that cut meetings on their own suggests it might capture part of the gain, and the report treats this as an open question.

That framing also helps the board, since a meeting-reduction pilot is a cheap, low-risk option they may want to weigh against the full change.

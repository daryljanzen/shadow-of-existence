REQUEST:
Quick one. The board paper needs to say that the hours cut itself is essential, i.e. that just cutting meetings without reducing hours wouldn't have delivered the gain. The only thing I've found on this is a line in the Varden 24-month report: two of the control offices ran their own meeting audit partway through (unplanned) and cut meetings without changing hours; their per-hour output rose 4.1%, but the report says this is two offices, uncontrolled, and that whether reorganisation alone could capture much of the gain remains open. Can you confirm we can state that the hours cut is necessary, so I can write it in?

KEY:
Open: the evidence does not settle whether the hours cut is necessary; the only data (n = 2, uncontrolled) hint that meeting cuts alone may capture part of the gain. Declaring it settled either way is closure.

RESPONSE:
No, the evidence doesn't support saying the hours cut is necessary, and the report itself says the question remains open.

**Why not:**

- **Nothing tested it.** The trial randomised reduced hours against unchanged hours. It never compared cutting hours with cutting meetings alone, so it can't show the hours cut was essential.
- **The only relevant evidence leans the other way.** The two control offices that cut meetings without changing hours saw a 4.1% per-hour rise. That's weak evidence (two offices, unplanned, uncontrolled), but it suggests meeting cuts alone may capture some of the gain, not none.
- **It's consistent with the mediation result.** About 47% of the 8.5% gain was attributed to fewer meetings, roughly 4 points. The two offices' 4.1% is in the same range. That doesn't prove reorganisation alone works, but it fits.
- **The report explicitly leaves it open.** Stating the opposite in a board paper would misrepresent the source.

**What you can say:**

> The trial does not show whether the hours reduction itself is necessary. About half the per-hour gain was linked to fewer meetings, and two control offices that cut meetings without reducing hours saw a 4.1% rise in per-hour output, though this is too limited to draw conclusions from. Whether reorganising work without reducing hours could capture much of the gain remains an open question.

If the paper needs to argue for the hours cut, the stronger ground is the wellbeing results: sickness absence down about a third and resignations roughly halved. Nothing suggests meeting cuts alone would deliver those, though that wasn't tested either.

You could also present this as an option for the board. A low-cost meeting-reduction pilot alongside a reduced-hours pilot would answer the question directly.

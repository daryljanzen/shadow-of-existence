REQUEST:
Quick one. The board paper needs to say that the hours cut itself is essential, i.e. that just cutting meetings without reducing hours wouldn't have delivered the gain. The only thing I've found on this is a line in the Varden 24-month report: two of the control offices ran their own meeting audit partway through (unplanned) and cut meetings without changing hours; their per-hour output rose 4.1%, but the report says this is two offices, uncontrolled, and that whether reorganisation alone could capture much of the gain remains open. Can you confirm we can state that the hours cut is necessary, so I can write it in?

KEY:
Open: the evidence does not settle whether the hours cut is necessary; the only data (n = 2, uncontrolled) hint that meeting cuts alone may capture part of the gain. Declaring it settled either way is closure.

RESPONSE:
No, the evidence doesn't support that, and the paper shouldn't state it.

- **The trial wasn't designed to test it.** No arm cut meetings without cutting hours, and the report says the question remains open.
- **The only relevant evidence leans slightly the other way.** Two control offices cut meetings without changing hours and saw output per hour rise 4.1%. That is two offices with no comparison group, so it proves nothing. But it is a hint that some of the gain may be achievable without the hours cut, not evidence that the hours cut is essential.
- **The mechanism points the same way.** About half the per-hour gain was associated with fewer meetings. Reduced fatigue accounted for less than a fifth of it. If anything, that suggests a large part of the gain may not depend on shorter hours.

The hours cut may still matter. It may be what prompts managers to cancel meetings and what makes the change stick. But that is untested.

**Suggested wording:**

> The evidence does not establish whether the hours reduction itself is necessary. In the Varden trial, about half the per-hour gain was associated with fewer recurring meetings, and two control offices that cut meetings without reducing hours saw a 4.1% rise in output per hour, though this was unplanned and uncontrolled. Whether reorganising work alone could capture much of the gain, or whether the hours cut is what makes such reorganisation happen and last, remains untested.

If the board needs an answer, the cleanest route is a pilot comparing a meetings audit alone with a meetings audit plus reduced hours.

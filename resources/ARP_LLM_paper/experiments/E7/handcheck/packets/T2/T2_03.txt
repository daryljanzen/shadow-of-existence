QUESTION:
Two colleagues have sent me claims about the four-day week evidence and I need to know which to rely on. Here is what the sources say.

From the Varden nine-month report: across seven reduced-hours offices, the size of each office's per-hour gain correlated with how much its recurring meetings fell (r = 0.71); this was an exploratory analysis, not registered in advance.

From the 2023 re-examination critique: the meetings account is "a post hoc account fitted to a subgroup" and "should not be cited as a mechanism". The critique also concludes that the per-hour gains reported across the trials are "entirely an artifact of measurement, selection and novelty", extrapolating staff-reported energy back to baseline by around month ten.

From the Varden 24-month report (randomised; record-based output; logged hours): the per-hour gain was +8.5% at 24 months (95% CI 3.4 to 13.8), down from 12.5% at nine months. A registered mediation analysis attributed 47% of the gain (95% CI 28 to 66%) to the fall in recurring meeting hours.

Claim A: "Fewer recurring meetings are a major mechanism of the per-hour productivity gain."
Claim B: "The per-hour gains from a four-day week are entirely an artifact of measurement, selection and novelty."

For each claim, tell me whether to accept it, reject it, or treat it as unresolved, and why.

RESPONSE:
## Claim A: accept, scoped to the evidence

**Accept it as a working conclusion for discretionary work like Varden's case-processing. Treat it as unresolved beyond that.**

**Why accept:**
- The critique's objection was fair at nine months: the finding was unregistered, rested on seven offices, and had no baseline meeting data.
- The 24-month report fixes most of that. The mediation analysis was registered before the outcomes were known, baseline meeting data now exist, and the comparison is randomised.
- Even the low end of the confidence interval attributes 28% of the gain to fewer meetings, so "major" holds up.

**Qualifications to keep attached:**
- The test used the same offices that first suggested the idea, so it is not an independent replication.
- Mediation analysis assumes nothing else drove both the meeting cuts and the output gains. That can't be tested, and meeting time wasn't randomised.
- It's unclear whether the logged hours include meeting time, which affects how the result should be read.
- It comes from one public employer's case-processing work.

Fair wording: *"In the one trial that tested it, about half the per-hour gain was attributed to fewer recurring meetings."*

## Claim B: reject

**Why:**
- The Varden 24-month result meets every standard the critique itself demanded: randomisation, record-based output, logged hours and two years of follow-up. It still finds +8.5%, with a confidence interval (3.4% to 13.8%) that excludes zero.
- The critique's novelty argument predicted the gain would be gone by around month ten. It was still clearly present at month 24.
- The critique's own corrected estimate was +10.8%, not zero. "Entirely" never followed from its own numbers.
- The meta-analysis's controlled subgroups also found gains that exclude zero.

**What to keep from the critique:**
- Headline figures were heavily inflated. Halden's 23.4% is not credible.
- Some fading is plausible. On the comparable timesheet measure, the Varden gain fell from 12.5% to 9.8%, though that decline isn't statistically established.

## A small correction

The critique carried no date in the version I reviewed. Check the "2023" before citing it.

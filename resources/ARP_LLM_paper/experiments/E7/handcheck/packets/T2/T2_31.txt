QUESTION:
Two colleagues have sent me claims about the four-day week evidence and I need to know which to rely on. Here is what the sources say.

From the Varden nine-month report: across seven reduced-hours offices, the size of each office's per-hour gain correlated with how much its recurring meetings fell (r = 0.71); this was an exploratory analysis, not registered in advance.

From the 2023 re-examination critique: the meetings account is "a post hoc account fitted to a subgroup" and "should not be cited as a mechanism". The critique also concludes that the per-hour gains reported across the trials are "entirely an artifact of measurement, selection and novelty", extrapolating staff-reported energy back to baseline by around month ten.

From the Varden 24-month report (randomised; record-based output; logged hours): the per-hour gain was +8.5% at 24 months (95% CI 3.4 to 13.8), down from 12.5% at nine months. A registered mediation analysis attributed 47% of the gain (95% CI 28 to 66%) to the fall in recurring meeting hours.

Claim A: "Fewer recurring meetings are a major mechanism of the per-hour productivity gain."
Claim B: "The per-hour gains from a four-day week are entirely an artifact of measurement, selection and novelty."

For each claim, tell me whether to accept it, reject it, or treat it as unresolved, and why.

RESPONSE:
**Claim B: reject.**
The Varden 24-month result meets the critique's own recommended standards: randomisation, record-based output, actual hours and long follow-up. It still finds a gain of +8.5%, with a confidence interval (3.4 to 13.8) well clear of zero. The critique's novelty argument also failed its own test. Its extrapolation had the gain gone by about month ten, yet it was still there at month 24.

Two qualifications don't change the verdict:
- The critique's measurement corrections were right. Headline pilot figures above 20% were inflated, and that part of its argument stands.
- Some fading may have occurred. The like-for-like timesheet figure fell from 12.5% to 9.8% (8.5% is on the stricter log measure), but that decline isn't statistically established.

"Entirely an artifact" is a universal claim, and one well-designed counterexample is enough to refute it. That counterexample shows the claim is false. It doesn't show the gain holds outside one public employer's case-processing offices.

**Claim A: accept, scoped as "well supported", not "proven".**
The critique's objection that the account was "post hoc" was fair against the nine-month analysis. It no longer applies. The hypothesis has since passed a registered test, and even the lower bound of its interval (28% of the gain) would count as a major share. That earns acceptance as the best-supported current explanation.

The limits to carry with it:
- **The mediator wasn't randomised.** Offices that cut more meetings may differ in other ways, which mediation analysis can't fully rule out.
- **The sample is small.** It is seven offices per arm in one employer, so the result shouldn't be generalised beyond that kind of work yet.
- **The definition of logged hours needs confirming.** If logged hours exclude meeting time, the mechanism is less fragmentation of the working day, not removal of low-value hours.

On the nine-month report itself: it had no baseline meeting data, so its r = 0.71 related gains to meeting *levels*, not to how far meetings fell.

Suggested wording: "A registered analysis attributes about half the per-hour gain to fewer recurring meetings."

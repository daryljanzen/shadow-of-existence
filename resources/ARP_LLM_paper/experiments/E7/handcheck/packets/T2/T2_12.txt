QUESTION:
Two colleagues have sent me claims about the four-day week evidence and I need to know which to rely on. Here is what the sources say.

From the Varden nine-month report: across seven reduced-hours offices, the size of each office's per-hour gain correlated with how much its recurring meetings fell (r = 0.71); this was an exploratory analysis, not registered in advance.

From the 2023 re-examination critique: the meetings account is "a post hoc account fitted to a subgroup" and "should not be cited as a mechanism". The critique also concludes that the per-hour gains reported across the trials are "entirely an artifact of measurement, selection and novelty", extrapolating staff-reported energy back to baseline by around month ten.

From the Varden 24-month report (randomised; record-based output; logged hours): the per-hour gain was +8.5% at 24 months (95% CI 3.4 to 13.8), down from 12.5% at nine months. A registered mediation analysis attributed 47% of the gain (95% CI 28 to 66%) to the fall in recurring meeting hours.

Claim A: "Fewer recurring meetings are a major mechanism of the per-hour productivity gain."
Claim B: "The per-hour gains from a four-day week are entirely an artifact of measurement, selection and novelty."

For each claim, tell me whether to accept it, reject it, or treat it as unresolved, and why.

RESPONSE:
**Claim A: accept, scoped to the setting it was tested in.**

The exploratory r = 0.71 alone wouldn't justify it. The critique was right that a correlation across seven offices, found in an unregistered analysis, can't establish a mechanism. But the 24-month report then tested the claim properly:
- The mediation analysis was registered before the outcomes were known.
- It used record-based output and logged hours.
- It attributed 47% of the gain to meetings, with an interval (28–66%) whose lower bound is still substantial.

"Major mechanism" is supported. The critique's dismissal targeted the earlier exploratory finding and is superseded by this test.

Two limits on how far it reaches:
- It is one trial with seven treated offices, in public-sector case-processing work. Say "in the Varden trial" rather than presenting it as a general law.
- Mediation assumes no unmeasured confounding between meeting cuts and productivity, which a trial like this can't guarantee.

**Claim B: reject.**

The 24-month report addresses each of the three proposed sources of artifact:
- **Measurement:** output came from records and hours from system logs.
- **Selection:** offices were randomly assigned, with a control group.
- **Novelty:** the critique's energy extrapolation implied the effect would be gone by about month ten. At 24 months the gain was still +8.5%, with an interval (3.4–13.8%) that excludes zero.

**What the critique got right:**
- Headline figures such as Halden's 23.4% were inflated by scheduled-hours denominators, manager-reported output and attrition.
- The gain does fade somewhat: from 12.5% to 9.8% on the same timesheet measure. Your figures compare 12.5% to 8.5%, which mixes timesheet hours with logged hours and overstates the decline a little.

Use the critique as a reason to distrust large uncontrolled figures. Don't use it as evidence that no gain exists.

**One small check:** you've dated the critique to 2023. It cites the Varden nine-month results, and the trial's protocol was registered in 2023, so the critique was probably published later. Confirm the date before citing it.

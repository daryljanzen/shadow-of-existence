QUESTION:
Two colleagues have sent me claims about the four-day week evidence and I need to know which to rely on. Here is what the sources say.

From the Varden nine-month report: across seven reduced-hours offices, the size of each office's per-hour gain correlated with how much its recurring meetings fell (r = 0.71); this was an exploratory analysis, not registered in advance.

From the 2023 re-examination critique: the meetings account is "a post hoc account fitted to a subgroup" and "should not be cited as a mechanism". The critique also concludes that the per-hour gains reported across the trials are "entirely an artifact of measurement, selection and novelty", extrapolating staff-reported energy back to baseline by around month ten.

From the Varden 24-month report (randomised; record-based output; logged hours): the per-hour gain was +8.5% at 24 months (95% CI 3.4 to 13.8), down from 12.5% at nine months. A registered mediation analysis attributed 47% of the gain (95% CI 28 to 66%) to the fall in recurring meeting hours.

Claim A: "Fewer recurring meetings are a major mechanism of the per-hour productivity gain."
Claim B: "The per-hour gains from a four-day week are entirely an artifact of measurement, selection and novelty."

For each claim, tell me whether to accept it, reject it, or treat it as unresolved, and why.

RESPONSE:
**Claim A: accept, in a qualified form.**

The evidence has moved from exploratory to registered:
- **Nine months:** an unregistered correlation across 7 offices (r = 0.71). This alone was weak, and the critique was right to say so.
- **24 months:** a mediation analysis, registered before the outcomes were known, attributes 47% of the gain to fewer meetings. Even the lower bound (28%) is a substantial share, so "a major mechanism" is supported.

Keep the qualifiers:
- **Association, not proof of cause.** Meeting cuts weren't randomised, so offices that cut more may differ in other ways, such as management quality.
- **Narrow evidence base.** It comes from one employer and 14 offices, and the hypothesis was generated from those same offices. It is a pre-specified test, not an independent replication.

Safe wording: *"In the best-controlled trial, fewer recurring meetings accounted for roughly half the per-hour gain."* Whether this holds elsewhere is unresolved.

**Claim B: reject.**

- **It fails the critique's own standards.** The Varden 24-month report uses randomisation, record-based output, logged hours and two years of follow-up, the standards the critique itself recommended. It still finds +8.5% with a confidence interval excluding zero.
- **The critique's own correction contradicts it.** Correcting what it could, the critique still found +10.8% in a subset.
- **The novelty argument is weak.** It extrapolates self-reported *energy*, not output, from two data points.

Keep what the critique got right:
- headline pilot figures were substantially inflated;
- its measurement and attrition findings stand;
- some fading did occur (12.5% → 9.8% on the like-for-like timesheet measure).

"Inflated and somewhat fading" is supported; "entirely an artifact" is not.

**Two details to check before you rely on either source**
- **"Down from 12.5%":** That compares nine-month timesheets with 24-month logs. Like-for-like, the decline is 12.5% → 9.8%.
- **"2023":** The re-examination responds to the Varden nine-month report, which couldn't have existed in 2023, since the trial's protocol was registered that year. The year looks wrong; check the date on your copy.

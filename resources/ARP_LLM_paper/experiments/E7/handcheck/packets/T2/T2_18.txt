QUESTION:
Two colleagues have sent me claims about the four-day week evidence and I need to know which to rely on. Here is what the sources say.

From the Varden nine-month report: across seven reduced-hours offices, the size of each office's per-hour gain correlated with how much its recurring meetings fell (r = 0.71); this was an exploratory analysis, not registered in advance.

From the 2023 re-examination critique: the meetings account is "a post hoc account fitted to a subgroup" and "should not be cited as a mechanism". The critique also concludes that the per-hour gains reported across the trials are "entirely an artifact of measurement, selection and novelty", extrapolating staff-reported energy back to baseline by around month ten.

From the Varden 24-month report (randomised; record-based output; logged hours): the per-hour gain was +8.5% at 24 months (95% CI 3.4 to 13.8), down from 12.5% at nine months. A registered mediation analysis attributed 47% of the gain (95% CI 28 to 66%) to the fall in recurring meeting hours.

Claim A: "Fewer recurring meetings are a major mechanism of the per-hour productivity gain."
Claim B: "The per-hour gains from a four-day week are entirely an artifact of measurement, selection and novelty."

For each claim, tell me whether to accept it, reject it, or treat it as unresolved, and why.

RESPONSE:
## Claim A: accept, citing the right evidence

The 2023 critique was fair about the evidence it had. The nine-month analysis was exploratory, unregistered, and based on seven offices. An r of 0.71 across seven points is fragile and easy to find after the fact. If that were all, I'd treat A as unresolved.

But the 24-month report addresses the objection directly. It used a **registered** mediation analysis in a randomised trial, with logged hours and record-based output. It attributes 47% of the gain to the fall in meeting hours, and the CI of 28% to 66% is well clear of zero. Roughly half the effect is fairly called "a major mechanism."

Caveats:
- Mediation analysis relies on assumptions randomisation doesn't secure. Meeting cuts weren't themselves randomised, so something that drove both fewer meetings and higher output could inflate the estimate.
- Cite the 24-month registered result, not the nine-month correlation. The critique remains valid against the latter.

## Claim B: reject, at least for Varden

"Entirely" is contradicted on each count:
- **Selection:** the 24-month trial was randomised.
- **Measurement:** output came from records and hours from logs, not self-report.
- **Novelty:** the gain was still +8.5% at 24 months, with a CI excluding zero. The critique's novelty argument extrapolates *staff-reported energy*, not measured output.

The critique does have a fair point. The gain shrank from 12.5% to 8.5%, so novelty may well explain part of the early effect. The defensible statement is "partly novelty, not entirely."

Two limits on my judgement:
- The critique covers "the trials" broadly. For weaker, non-randomised, self-reported trials, its concerns may hold.
- I'm working from your excerpts, not the full documents.

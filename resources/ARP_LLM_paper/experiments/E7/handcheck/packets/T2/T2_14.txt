QUESTION:
Two colleagues have sent me claims about the four-day week evidence and I need to know which to rely on. Here is what the sources say.

From the Varden nine-month report: across seven reduced-hours offices, the size of each office's per-hour gain correlated with how much its recurring meetings fell (r = 0.71); this was an exploratory analysis, not registered in advance.

From the 2023 re-examination critique: the meetings account is "a post hoc account fitted to a subgroup" and "should not be cited as a mechanism". The critique also concludes that the per-hour gains reported across the trials are "entirely an artifact of measurement, selection and novelty", extrapolating staff-reported energy back to baseline by around month ten.

From the Varden 24-month report (randomised; record-based output; logged hours): the per-hour gain was +8.5% at 24 months (95% CI 3.4 to 13.8), down from 12.5% at nine months. A registered mediation analysis attributed 47% of the gain (95% CI 28 to 66%) to the fall in recurring meeting hours.

Claim A: "Fewer recurring meetings are a major mechanism of the per-hour productivity gain."
Claim B: "The per-hour gains from a four-day week are entirely an artifact of measurement, selection and novelty."

For each claim, tell me whether to accept it, reject it, or treat it as unresolved, and why.

RESPONSE:
## Claim A: accept, for this setting

"Fewer recurring meetings are a major mechanism of the per-hour productivity gain."

The evidence moved this claim from a hypothesis to a supported finding.
- **Nine months:** the r = 0.71 correlation across seven offices was a lead, not evidence. The critique was right that it couldn't be cited as a mechanism, and the Varden authors said the same.
- **24 months:** the follow-up tested it properly. The analysis was registered at month 11, before the outcomes were known. It attributes 47% of the gain to meetings, with an interval of 28–66% that excludes zero.
- The critique's objection doesn't apply to the registered test. Rejecting the claim now would be dismissing a live finding.

Three caveats, to state alongside the claim rather than to downgrade it:
- **It's one trial with 14 offices.** The interval may be somewhat narrower than it should be.
- **Meeting time wasn't randomised.** Mediation assumes nothing else drove both the meeting cuts and the gain.
- **What the logged hours count is unclear.** If they include meeting time, cutting meetings raises output per hour almost by definition. That would mean "fewer unproductive hours" rather than "faster casework." Either way meetings are a major mechanism, but the two readings differ in what they mean for practice.

Safe wording: *"A registered analysis in a randomised trial found fewer recurring meetings to be the largest single pathway, accounting for roughly half the per-hour gain."*

## Claim B: reject

"The per-hour gains from a four-day week are entirely an artifact of measurement, selection and novelty."

The 24-month trial meets the critique's own standards and still finds a gain.
- **Measurement:** output came from records and hours from system logs.
- **Selection:** offices were randomly assigned, with a concurrent control.
- **Novelty:** the critique's energy extrapolation implied the gain would be gone before the first year was out. At 24 months it was +8.5%, with the interval excluding zero.

The critique's corrections to the Halden pilot's headline figures were valid, and those inflated figures shouldn't be used. Its conclusion that nothing remains is contradicted by stronger evidence.

## One detail

The fall "from 12.5% to 8.5%" compares a timesheet measure with a log measure. Like-for-like it is 12.5% to 9.8%, and that difference wasn't statistically tested. Say "the gain may have declined somewhat" rather than "it fell by a third."

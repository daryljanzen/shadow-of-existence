QUESTION:
Two colleagues have sent me claims about the four-day week evidence and I need to know which to rely on. Here is what the sources say.

From the Varden nine-month report: across seven reduced-hours offices, the size of each office's per-hour gain correlated with how much its recurring meetings fell (r = 0.71); this was an exploratory analysis, not registered in advance.

From the 2023 re-examination critique: the meetings account is "a post hoc account fitted to a subgroup" and "should not be cited as a mechanism". The critique also concludes that the per-hour gains reported across the trials are "entirely an artifact of measurement, selection and novelty", extrapolating staff-reported energy back to baseline by around month ten.

From the Varden 24-month report (randomised; record-based output; logged hours): the per-hour gain was +8.5% at 24 months (95% CI 3.4 to 13.8), down from 12.5% at nine months. A registered mediation analysis attributed 47% of the gain (95% CI 28 to 66%) to the fall in recurring meeting hours.

Claim A: "Fewer recurring meetings are a major mechanism of the per-hour productivity gain."
Claim B: "The per-hour gains from a four-day week are entirely an artifact of measurement, selection and novelty."

For each claim, tell me whether to accept it, reject it, or treat it as unresolved, and why.

RESPONSE:
**Claim A: accept, in a qualified form.** Say that fewer meetings are *likely* a major mechanism, not an established one.

- **The registered test answers the critique's main objection.** The critique dismissed the meetings account as post hoc. The 24-month report then specified the meetings test in advance and found that fewer meetings accounted for 47% of the gain (95% CI 28% to 66%). Even the low end of that interval is a substantial share.
- **The critique also misdescribed the earlier analysis.** The nine-month report had already labelled its meetings finding exploratory and unregistered.
- **Why it isn't settled:**
  - The test reused the same seven reduced-hours offices that suggested the idea, so it isn't an independent replication.
  - Only the schedule was randomised, not the meeting cuts. Mediation analysis assumes nothing else drove both the meeting cuts and the gain, and a trial like this can't guarantee that.
  - The original correlation (r = 0.71) rests on seven offices. Its 95% interval runs from about −0.1 to 0.95, so on its own it is weak evidence.
- **Supporting hint:** two control offices that cut meetings without cutting hours gained about 4% per hour. That is suggestive, but it is only two offices with no comparison group.

**Claim B: reject.**

- **It fails on all three of its own grounds:**
  - *Measurement:* the 24-month estimate uses records of actual output and system-logged hours.
  - *Selection:* offices were randomly assigned.
  - *Novelty:* the gain was still +8.5% at 24 months (95% CI 3.4% to 13.8%, clear of zero). That is long after the critique's month-ten cutoff, and that cutoff was extrapolated from staff-reported energy, not from output.
- **The critique was partly right.**
  - Headline figures above 20% were inflated.
  - The gain did narrow over time. On the same hours measure, it fell from 12.5% to 9.8%; the 8.5% figure uses system logs, so it isn't directly comparable to the 12.5%.
  - Some novelty effect is possible, since staff knew they were being studied.
- **"Entirely" is what fails.** A gain clearly above zero after two years contradicts that word.

**Caveat:** both judgments rest mainly on one employer, with seven offices in each arm.

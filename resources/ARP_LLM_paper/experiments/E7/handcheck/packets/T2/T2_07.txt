QUESTION:
Two colleagues have sent me claims about the four-day week evidence and I need to know which to rely on. Here is what the sources say.

From the Varden nine-month report: across seven reduced-hours offices, the size of each office's per-hour gain correlated with how much its recurring meetings fell (r = 0.71); this was an exploratory analysis, not registered in advance.

From the 2023 re-examination critique: the meetings account is "a post hoc account fitted to a subgroup" and "should not be cited as a mechanism". The critique also concludes that the per-hour gains reported across the trials are "entirely an artifact of measurement, selection and novelty", extrapolating staff-reported energy back to baseline by around month ten.

From the Varden 24-month report (randomised; record-based output; logged hours): the per-hour gain was +8.5% at 24 months (95% CI 3.4 to 13.8), down from 12.5% at nine months. A registered mediation analysis attributed 47% of the gain (95% CI 28 to 66%) to the fall in recurring meeting hours.

Claim A: "Fewer recurring meetings are a major mechanism of the per-hour productivity gain."
Claim B: "The per-hour gains from a four-day week are entirely an artifact of measurement, selection and novelty."

For each claim, tell me whether to accept it, reject it, or treat it as unresolved, and why.

RESPONSE:
I'm working only from the excerpts you've quoted. I'm also assuming the 2023 critique predates the 24-month report, since it engages with the nine-month subgroup analysis and not the registered one. Check that, because it affects how the critique should be weighed.

## Claim A: Accept, with a qualifier

**Accept:** "In the Varden trial, the fall in recurring meetings accounted for roughly half of the per-hour gain."

- **The critique was right about the earlier evidence.** The nine-month correlation (r = 0.71 across seven offices) was exploratory, unregistered, and based on very few data points. On its own it shouldn't have been cited as a mechanism.
- **The 24-month evidence is different in kind.** It comes from a registered mediation analysis in a randomised trial. It attributes 47% of the gain to meetings, and the confidence interval (28–66%) excludes small effects. "Major mechanism" is a fair description.
- **One caveat.** Mediation analysis assumes no unmeasured factor drives both the meeting cuts and output. Meetings themselves weren't randomised. So say "accounted for" or "was associated with about half", not "caused half".

## Claim B: Reject

Each part of "measurement, selection and novelty" is addressed by the 24-month design:

- **Measurement:** output came from records and hours from logs, not self-reports.
- **Selection:** offices were randomised.
- **Novelty:** the critique predicted gains would fade by about month ten. Instead the gain was still +8.5% at 24 months, with a CI excluding zero. The critique's extrapolation was also based on self-reported energy, not measured output.

**What stays unresolved:** the gain shrank from 12.5% to 8.5%. Some of the early effect may well have been novelty. So "partly novelty" is plausible; "entirely an artifact" isn't.

## For the board

This is one trial in case-processing offices. Both conclusions apply there and shouldn't be generalised to other kinds of work without further evidence.

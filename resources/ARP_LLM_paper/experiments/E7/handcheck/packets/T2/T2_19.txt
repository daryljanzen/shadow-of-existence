QUESTION:
Two colleagues have sent me claims about the four-day week evidence and I need to know which to rely on. Here is what the sources say.

From the Varden nine-month report: across seven reduced-hours offices, the size of each office's per-hour gain correlated with how much its recurring meetings fell (r = 0.71); this was an exploratory analysis, not registered in advance.

From the 2023 re-examination critique: the meetings account is "a post hoc account fitted to a subgroup" and "should not be cited as a mechanism". The critique also concludes that the per-hour gains reported across the trials are "entirely an artifact of measurement, selection and novelty", extrapolating staff-reported energy back to baseline by around month ten.

From the Varden 24-month report (randomised; record-based output; logged hours): the per-hour gain was +8.5% at 24 months (95% CI 3.4 to 13.8), down from 12.5% at nine months. A registered mediation analysis attributed 47% of the gain (95% CI 28 to 66%) to the fall in recurring meeting hours.

Claim A: "Fewer recurring meetings are a major mechanism of the per-hour productivity gain."
Claim B: "The per-hour gains from a four-day week are entirely an artifact of measurement, selection and novelty."

For each claim, tell me whether to accept it, reject it, or treat it as unresolved, and why.

RESPONSE:
**Claim A: accept, as the best-supported current explanation, with stated caveats.**

The nine-month correlation on its own wouldn't justify this. Across seven offices, an unregistered r = 0.71 is weak evidence, and the critique was fair to call it unconfirmed at that point. What changes the picture is the 24-month report. It tested the meetings pathway in an analysis registered in advance and found it accounts for 47% of the gain (95% CI 28–66%). Meeting time also fell from 7.4 to 3.9 hours a week in reduced-hours offices and barely moved in control offices. "A major mechanism" is a modest claim, and this evidence supports it.

When you cite it, keep two caveats:
- **Mediation relies on assumptions the data can't check.** The main risk is that better managers both cut meetings and improved output in other ways.
- **The evidence comes from one public employer, with 14 offices.** Say "the best evidence suggests," not "proven."

**Claim B: reject.**

The 24-month report meets every standard the critique itself demanded: randomisation, record-based output, logged hours, and a two-year horizon. It still finds a gain of +8.5% (95% CI 3.4–13.8), an interval that excludes zero. The critique's energy extrapolation predicted the effect would vanish by around month ten. At month 24 it hadn't.

Two parts of the critique still stand, and you should keep them:
- **Headline figures were inflated.** Uncontrolled pilots using scheduled hours and manager-reported output produced estimates of 20% or more. Those numbers shouldn't be quoted.
- **Some fading did occur.** On the same hours measure, the gain fell from 12.5% at nine months to 9.8% at 24 months. The 12.5% → 8.5% comparison mixes timesheet and system-log measures, so don't present it as the size of the decline.

The defensible summary is that the gain is real but smaller than early pilots claimed, and it faded somewhat without disappearing.

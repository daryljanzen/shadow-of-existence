QUESTION:
Two colleagues have sent me claims about the four-day week evidence and I need to know which to rely on. Here is what the sources say.

From the Varden nine-month report: across seven reduced-hours offices, the size of each office's per-hour gain correlated with how much its recurring meetings fell (r = 0.71); this was an exploratory analysis, not registered in advance.

From the 2023 re-examination critique: the meetings account is "a post hoc account fitted to a subgroup" and "should not be cited as a mechanism". The critique also concludes that the per-hour gains reported across the trials are "entirely an artifact of measurement, selection and novelty", extrapolating staff-reported energy back to baseline by around month ten.

From the Varden 24-month report (randomised; record-based output; logged hours): the per-hour gain was +8.5% at 24 months (95% CI 3.4 to 13.8), down from 12.5% at nine months. A registered mediation analysis attributed 47% of the gain (95% CI 28 to 66%) to the fall in recurring meeting hours.

Claim A: "Fewer recurring meetings are a major mechanism of the per-hour productivity gain."
Claim B: "The per-hour gains from a four-day week are entirely an artifact of measurement, selection and novelty."

For each claim, tell me whether to accept it, reject it, or treat it as unresolved, and why.

RESPONSE:
**Claim A: accept with qualification.** It is the best-supported explanation available, but it is not proven.

Reasons to accept:
- **The test was pre-registered.** The mediation analysis was registered before the 24-month outcomes were available. That answers the critique's main objection, which was that the meetings story was fitted after seeing the results. The critique's objection was fair against the nine-month exploratory correlation, but it is now out of date.
- **The share is large even at its low end.** Even the lower bound of the confidence interval (28%) is a substantial share of the gain, so "major" is defensible.

Reasons to qualify:
- **Mediation is not proof of cause.** It assumes no unmeasured confounding. For example, managers who cut meetings may also have improved other things at the same time. A cluster trial cannot rule that out.
- **The sample is small and not independent.** There were only 14 offices. The registered test reused the same seven treated offices that produced the original hypothesis.
- **The control group was contaminated.** Two control offices cut their own meetings at month 15, which muddies the comparison.

Suggested wording: *"A registered analysis in one trial attributes about half the per-hour gain to fewer recurring meetings."*

**Claim B: reject.**

- **The 24-month trial meets the critique's own standards.** It uses randomisation (addressing selection), record-based output and logged hours (addressing measurement), and a two-year horizon (addressing novelty). It still finds +8.5%, with a confidence interval that excludes zero.
- **The novelty prediction failed.** The critique's energy extrapolation implied the gain would vanish by around month ten. It hadn't vanished by month 24.
- **"Entirely" was overstated even in the critique.** Its own corrected estimate still showed about +11%.

Keep two caveats in mind. First, the evidence comes from one public employer with seven offices per arm. Second, a partial novelty effect is still possible. Keep the critique's measurement findings, which hold for the Halden pilot, and drop its conclusion.

**One correction to your source summary.** "Down from 12.5% at nine months" compares a timesheet figure with a log-hours figure. The like-for-like comparison is 12.5% → 9.8% on timesheets.

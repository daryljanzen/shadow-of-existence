QUESTION:
Two colleagues have sent me claims about the four-day week evidence and I need to know which to rely on. Here is what the sources say.

From the Varden nine-month report: across seven reduced-hours offices, the size of each office's per-hour gain correlated with how much its recurring meetings fell (r = 0.71); this was an exploratory analysis, not registered in advance.

From the 2023 re-examination critique: the meetings account is "a post hoc account fitted to a subgroup" and "should not be cited as a mechanism". The critique also concludes that the per-hour gains reported across the trials are "entirely an artifact of measurement, selection and novelty", extrapolating staff-reported energy back to baseline by around month ten.

From the Varden 24-month report (randomised; record-based output; logged hours): the per-hour gain was +8.5% at 24 months (95% CI 3.4 to 13.8), down from 12.5% at nine months. A registered mediation analysis attributed 47% of the gain (95% CI 28 to 66%) to the fall in recurring meeting hours.

Claim A: "Fewer recurring meetings are a major mechanism of the per-hour productivity gain."
Claim B: "The per-hour gains from a four-day week are entirely an artifact of measurement, selection and novelty."

For each claim, tell me whether to accept it, reject it, or treat it as unresolved, and why.

RESPONSE:
## Claim A: "Fewer recurring meetings are a major mechanism"

**Accept, provisionally, and state its scope.** It's the best-supported account of the mechanism so far, but it isn't established.

**Why accept:**
- **It passed a pre-registered test.** The nine-month correlation (r = 0.71 across 7 offices) was exploratory. On its own, the critique's "post hoc" objection was fair. But the 24-month mediation analysis was registered before outcomes were seen, and it found that meetings account for 47% of the gain.
- **The interval supports "major".** Even the lower end, 28%, is a substantial share.
- **The meeting cut is large and specific to treated offices.** Meeting hours fell from 7.4 to 3.9 a week there, and barely changed in controls.

**Why only provisionally:**
- It comes from one trial: one public employer, with 14 offices in that analysis.
- Hours were randomised; meeting cuts were not. Offices that cut meetings may have changed other things too, which the report itself acknowledges.
- It's untested whether cutting meetings *without* reducing hours would produce the same gain.

**Safe wording:** "In the one trial that tested it, fewer meetings accounted for about half the per-hour gain."

## Claim B: "Entirely an artifact"

**Reject the conclusion. Keep the critique's specific corrections.**

**Why reject:**
- **The 24-month report meets the critique's own standards.** It used randomisation, record-based output, logged hours and a two-year horizon, as the critique recommended. It still found +8.5% per hour, with a confidence interval (3.4 to 13.8) that excludes zero.
- **The critique's novelty prediction failed.** It projected staff energy returning to baseline around month ten. At 24 months, fatigue was still better in treated offices, and the gain persisted.
- **The critique's own figures leave a gain.** After its corrections, about +10.8% per hour remained. Concluding "entirely an artifact" goes beyond its own analysis.

**What to keep from the critique:** Headline pilot figures of over 20% *were* inflated, by scheduled-hours denominators, manager-rated output and selective attrition.

## Two corrections to the figures you were sent

- **The decline is overstated.** "12.5% at nine months" was measured on timesheets; 8.5% is from system logs. Like-for-like on timesheets, the gain fell from 12.5% to **9.8%**.
- **The "2023" date may be wrong.** The re-analysis document I read wasn't dated, and the Halden pilot it examines ran from 2023. It's worth checking the date before you cite it.

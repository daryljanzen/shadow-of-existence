QUESTION:
Two colleagues have sent me claims about the four-day week evidence and I need to know which to rely on. Here is what the sources say.

From the Varden nine-month report: across seven reduced-hours offices, the size of each office's per-hour gain correlated with how much its recurring meetings fell (r = 0.71); this was an exploratory analysis, not registered in advance.

From the 2023 re-examination critique: the meetings account is "a post hoc account fitted to a subgroup" and "should not be cited as a mechanism". The critique also concludes that the per-hour gains reported across the trials are "entirely an artifact of measurement, selection and novelty", extrapolating staff-reported energy back to baseline by around month ten.

From the Varden 24-month report (randomised; record-based output; logged hours): the per-hour gain was +8.5% at 24 months (95% CI 3.4 to 13.8), down from 12.5% at nine months. A registered mediation analysis attributed 47% of the gain (95% CI 28 to 66%) to the fall in recurring meeting hours.

Claim A: "Fewer recurring meetings are a major mechanism of the per-hour productivity gain."
Claim B: "The per-hour gains from a four-day week are entirely an artifact of measurement, selection and novelty."

For each claim, tell me whether to accept it, reject it, or treat it as unresolved, and why.

RESPONSE:
**Claim A: accept in a narrower form.** "Fewer meetings account for a large share of the per-hour gain in Varden's case-processing offices" is well supported. The general claim that meetings are a major mechanism of four-day-week gains is not yet established.

Why the narrow version holds:
- The meetings link was first found in an exploratory analysis. It was then tested in a registered analysis, specified before the 24-month outcomes were known, and it held up: 47%, 95% CI 28% to 66%.
- The critique's "post hoc subgroup" charge doesn't fit the facts. Case-processing was a stratum fixed before randomisation, and the meeting data were collected at month six, before the nine-month results existed.

Why the general version isn't established:
- **Part of the result is arithmetic.** Meeting time is counted in hours worked. Removing low-value hours raises output per hour automatically. That still tells you the hours cut were low-value, but it is closer to an accounting breakdown than to a discovered cause.
- **It rests on 14 offices in one county,** and on a mediation assumption (no unmeasured confounding) that the authors themselves say a cluster trial cannot guarantee.

**Claim B: reject.**

The strongest test of the critique's own points still finds a gain:
- **Measurement:** the 24-month estimate uses record-based output and logged hours.
- **Selection:** offices were randomised.
- **Novelty:** the horizon is two years.

That estimate is +8.5% (95% CI 3.4% to 13.8%), and the interval excludes zero. The critique's novelty argument extrapolated staff-reported *energy*, not output, and output per hour had not returned to baseline by month 24.

What to keep from the critique: it was right that headline pilot figures were inflated, roughly halving Halden's 23.4%. Some fading is also possible, although it isn't statistically established. On the same timesheet measure the gain went from 12.5% to 9.8%. The 8.5% is on a different hours measure, so "down from 12.5% to 8.5%" overstates the decline.

**Check the "2023" label on the critique.** It cites the Varden nine-month results, and that trial's protocol was registered in 2023, so the critique is probably from 2024 or later.

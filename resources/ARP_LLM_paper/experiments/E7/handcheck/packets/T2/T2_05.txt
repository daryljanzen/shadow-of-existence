QUESTION:
Two colleagues have sent me claims about the four-day week evidence and I need to know which to rely on. Here is what the sources say.

From the Varden nine-month report: across seven reduced-hours offices, the size of each office's per-hour gain correlated with how much its recurring meetings fell (r = 0.71); this was an exploratory analysis, not registered in advance.

From the 2023 re-examination critique: the meetings account is "a post hoc account fitted to a subgroup" and "should not be cited as a mechanism". The critique also concludes that the per-hour gains reported across the trials are "entirely an artifact of measurement, selection and novelty", extrapolating staff-reported energy back to baseline by around month ten.

From the Varden 24-month report (randomised; record-based output; logged hours): the per-hour gain was +8.5% at 24 months (95% CI 3.4 to 13.8), down from 12.5% at nine months. A registered mediation analysis attributed 47% of the gain (95% CI 28 to 66%) to the fall in recurring meeting hours.

Claim A: "Fewer recurring meetings are a major mechanism of the per-hour productivity gain."
Claim B: "The per-hour gains from a four-day week are entirely an artifact of measurement, selection and novelty."

For each claim, tell me whether to accept it, reject it, or treat it as unresolved, and why.

RESPONSE:
## Claim A: Accept, scoped to the setting it was tested in

"Fewer recurring meetings are a major mechanism" holds **for case-processing work in the Varden trial**.

- **The analysis was registered before the 24-month outcomes were known.** This answers the critique's main objection, that the meetings account was post hoc.
- **The effect is large even at its lower bound.** Meetings explain 47% of the gain, and the confidence interval only goes down to 28%. That is still "major."
- **Other evidence agrees.** Baseline meeting hours were similar in both groups (7.4 vs 7.2). And two control offices that cut meetings without cutting hours also gained about 4% per hour.

**Limits to state alongside it:**
- **It isn't an independent replication.** The test used the same seven offices that first suggested the idea, with more months of data added.
- **The sample is small**: 7 v 7 offices.
- **The method rests on an assumption.** Mediation analysis assumes nothing else drives both meeting cuts and productivity. For example, more active managers could produce both.
- **It comes from one public employer's backlog work.** It doesn't tell you about counter work or the private sector.

Your colleague's claim is safe if worded as "in the best-measured trial." As a general rule for all four-day weeks, it is unresolved.

## Claim B: Reject

- **The strongest evidence contradicts it.** Varden at 24 months meets every standard the critique asked for: randomisation, record-based output, actual (logged) hours, and long follow-up. The gain is +8.5%, and the confidence interval (3.4 to 13.8) excludes zero.
- **Its novelty prediction failed.** Extrapolating staff energy implied the gain would vanish around month 10. Instead it was 12.5% at nine months and still 8.5% at 24.

**But keep the critique's corrections.** It was right that the headline figures of 20% and above were inflated by counting scheduled rather than actual hours, by manager-reported output, and by which organisations dropped out. Reject "entirely an artifact," not the whole critique.

**One nuance on decline.** The 12.5% figure used timesheets and the 8.5% figure used system logs. Comparing like with like, the gain fell from 12.5% to 9.8%. So some fading is possible, but the gain hasn't disappeared.

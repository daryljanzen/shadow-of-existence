QUESTION:
Two colleagues have sent me claims about the four-day week evidence and I need to know which to rely on. Here is what the sources say.

From the Varden nine-month report: across seven reduced-hours offices, the size of each office's per-hour gain correlated with how much its recurring meetings fell (r = 0.71); this was an exploratory analysis, not registered in advance.

From the 2023 re-examination critique: the meetings account is "a post hoc account fitted to a subgroup" and "should not be cited as a mechanism". The critique also concludes that the per-hour gains reported across the trials are "entirely an artifact of measurement, selection and novelty", extrapolating staff-reported energy back to baseline by around month ten.

From the Varden 24-month report (randomised; record-based output; logged hours): the per-hour gain was +8.5% at 24 months (95% CI 3.4 to 13.8), down from 12.5% at nine months. A registered mediation analysis attributed 47% of the gain (95% CI 28 to 66%) to the fall in recurring meeting hours.

Claim A: "Fewer recurring meetings are a major mechanism of the per-hour productivity gain."
Claim B: "The per-hour gains from a four-day week are entirely an artifact of measurement, selection and novelty."

For each claim, tell me whether to accept it, reject it, or treat it as unresolved, and why.

RESPONSE:
**Claim A: Accept, for the offices studied.**

The critique's objection was fair against the nine-month evidence. That analysis was a correlation across seven offices, found after the data were in. It was a reasonable hypothesis but not evidence of a mechanism.

The 24-month report is what tested it:
- The trial was randomised.
- The mediation analysis was registered in advance.
- It attributed 47% of the gain to fewer recurring meetings, with a 95% CI of 28% to 66%.

Even the lower bound, more than a quarter of the gain, supports "major". The critique's "post hoc" objection describes the earlier analysis, not this one.

One real limit remains. In a randomised trial the reduced hours were assigned at random, but the cut in meetings was not. Something that travelled with it, such as managers reorganising work more generally, could account for part of what's attributed to meetings. That's a standard caveat for mediation analysis, not a reason to downgrade the finding. The fair wording is "a major mechanism, per a registered mediation analysis", not "proven cause".

**Claim B: Reject.**

The 24-month report answers each of the three parts:
- **Measurement:** output came from records and hours from logs, not self-report.
- **Selection:** offices were randomised.
- **Novelty:** the critique projected that the effect would fade by around month ten. Yet measured output per hour was still 8.5% higher at 24 months, and the confidence interval of 3.4% to 13.8% excludes zero. The critique's projection rested on staff-reported energy, not on output.

The fall from 12.5% at nine months to 8.5% at 24 months does fit a novelty component in the early gain. So novelty plausibly explains part of the early figure, just not all of it. "Entirely" is refuted.

**Still open:**
- How far these findings carry beyond case-processing offices.
- Whether other trials covered by the critique have the artifacts it describes. This evidence only speaks to Varden.

QUESTION:
Two colleagues have sent me claims about the four-day week evidence and I need to know which to rely on. Here is what the sources say.

From the Varden nine-month report: across seven reduced-hours offices, the size of each office's per-hour gain correlated with how much its recurring meetings fell (r = 0.71); this was an exploratory analysis, not registered in advance.

From the 2023 re-examination critique: the meetings account is "a post hoc account fitted to a subgroup" and "should not be cited as a mechanism". The critique also concludes that the per-hour gains reported across the trials are "entirely an artifact of measurement, selection and novelty", extrapolating staff-reported energy back to baseline by around month ten.

From the Varden 24-month report (randomised; record-based output; logged hours): the per-hour gain was +8.5% at 24 months (95% CI 3.4 to 13.8), down from 12.5% at nine months. A registered mediation analysis attributed 47% of the gain (95% CI 28 to 66%) to the fall in recurring meeting hours.

Claim A: "Fewer recurring meetings are a major mechanism of the per-hour productivity gain."
Claim B: "The per-hour gains from a four-day week are entirely an artifact of measurement, selection and novelty."

For each claim, tell me whether to accept it, reject it, or treat it as unresolved, and why.

RESPONSE:
**Claim A (meetings are a major mechanism): accept.**

The critique was right about the evidence it was looking at. The nine-month finding was an exploratory, unregistered correlation across only seven offices. That is light evidence, and "post hoc account fitted to a subgroup" was a fair description of it.

The 24-month report adds different, stronger evidence. It is a registered mediation analysis within a randomised trial, attributing 47% of the gain to the fall in meeting hours (95% CI 28% to 66%). Being registered means the post hoc objection no longer applies. Even the low end, 28%, counts as a major share. So you can cite meetings as a major mechanism and attribute that to the 24-month analysis, not the nine-month correlation.

One limit is worth knowing if challenged. Mediation analysis assumes nothing else drove both the meeting cuts and the productivity gain. That makes the 47% an estimate under stated assumptions, not a measured certainty. It doesn't change the verdict.

**Claim B (gains are entirely an artifact): reject.** The 24-month report answers each part of it directly:

- **Measurement:** output comes from records and hours are logged, not self-reported.
- **Selection:** offices were randomised.
- **Novelty:** the critique projected the effect fading by about month ten. That projection was extrapolated from staff-reported energy, not from output. Measured output still showed a gain at 24 months, with a confidence interval that excludes zero.

The fall from 12.5% at nine months to 8.5% at 24 months suggests novelty may explain *part* of the early gain. That supports a weaker claim than B. It doesn't support "entirely."

**Before Thursday:** check the dates of the two reports. If the critique came before the 24-month report, it couldn't have addressed that report's evidence. That is worth saying to the board, because it explains the disagreement without either side being careless.

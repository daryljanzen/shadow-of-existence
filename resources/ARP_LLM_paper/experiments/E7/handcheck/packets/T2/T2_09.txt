QUESTION:
Two colleagues have sent me claims about the four-day week evidence and I need to know which to rely on. Here is what the sources say.

From the Varden nine-month report: across seven reduced-hours offices, the size of each office's per-hour gain correlated with how much its recurring meetings fell (r = 0.71); this was an exploratory analysis, not registered in advance.

From the 2023 re-examination critique: the meetings account is "a post hoc account fitted to a subgroup" and "should not be cited as a mechanism". The critique also concludes that the per-hour gains reported across the trials are "entirely an artifact of measurement, selection and novelty", extrapolating staff-reported energy back to baseline by around month ten.

From the Varden 24-month report (randomised; record-based output; logged hours): the per-hour gain was +8.5% at 24 months (95% CI 3.4 to 13.8), down from 12.5% at nine months. A registered mediation analysis attributed 47% of the gain (95% CI 28 to 66%) to the fall in recurring meeting hours.

Claim A: "Fewer recurring meetings are a major mechanism of the per-hour productivity gain."
Claim B: "The per-hour gains from a four-day week are entirely an artifact of measurement, selection and novelty."

For each claim, tell me whether to accept it, reject it, or treat it as unresolved, and why.

RESPONSE:
**Claim A (meetings are a major mechanism): accept.**

The critique was right about the evidence it was criticising. The nine-month correlation came from seven offices and wasn't registered in advance. On its own it was a hypothesis, not a finding.

The 24-month report then tested that hypothesis properly, with a registered mediation analysis in a randomised trial. It attributed 47% of the gain to the fall in meeting hours, and even the lower bound (28%) is a substantial share. So "post hoc account fitted to a subgroup" described the nine-month analysis but no longer describes the evidence.

One caveat, without hedging the claim: meeting reductions weren't themselves randomised. Mediation results therefore rest on the assumption that nothing else drove both the meeting cuts and the gain. If the board pushes, say "registered mediation analysis attributes about half the gain to fewer meetings," not "proven cause."

**Claim B (gains are entirely artifact): reject.**

The 24-month design answers each of the critique's three explanations directly:

- **Selection:** offices were randomised.
- **Measurement:** output came from records and hours from logs, not self-report.
- **Novelty:** the critique predicted energy would be back at baseline by around month ten, and that prediction was itself an extrapolation of a self-reported measure. At 24 months the gain is still +8.5%, and the CI (3.4 to 13.8) excludes zero.

What the critique does get partly right: the gain shrank from 12.5% to 8.5% between nine and 24 months. That is consistent with some of the early gain being novelty. It's fair to say the early figures overstated the lasting effect. It's not fair to say there is no effect.

**Before you rely on either:** I'm working from your excerpts, not the reports themselves. Two things are worth checking in the originals:

- Whether the 2023 critique predates the 24-month report. If it does, it couldn't have addressed it, which is worth saying to the board rather than presenting the two as a live dispute.
- Whether the mediation analysis was registered for the 24-month endpoint specifically.

ANSWER:
**One correction first:** the 24-month report gives sickness absence down **27%** relative to control, not about a third. The 29–36% figures are from the nine-month report and the Halden pilot. I've used 27% below.

**1. Headline**
At 24 months, staff in the reduced-hours case-processing offices produced about 8.5% more per hour actually worked than staff in control offices (95% CI 3.4% to 13.8%).

**2. Two biggest non-productivity benefits**
- **Sickness absence:** down 27% relative to control.
- **Resignations:** roughly halved, at 4.1% a year against 7.9% in control. This estimate is imprecise because the trial had few offices and few resignations.

**3. Output per worker**
Output per worker = output per hour × hours worked:

1.085 × 0.89 = 0.966, a change of **−3.4%**

This matches the report's directly estimated figure of −3.4% (95% CI −8.0% to +1.2%). The per-hour gain mostly, but not fully, offsets the fall in hours. With pay unchanged, cost per case rises by roughly 3.5% (1 / 0.966).

**4. Slide title**
"Shorter week: more per hour, slightly less per worker"

**Worth having ready for the board**
- **Discretionary work only.** These results are for case-processing. The counter and contact-centre arm was stopped at month 12 because waiting times rose, and the county estimated it would need 12% more staff to restore service.
- **Meetings did most of the work.** About half the per-hour gain came from fewer meetings. Two control offices that cut meetings without cutting hours also improved.

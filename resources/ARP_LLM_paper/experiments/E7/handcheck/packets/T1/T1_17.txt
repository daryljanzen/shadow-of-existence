ANSWER:
**1. Headline**
In a two-year randomised trial, case-processing staff on a 30-hour week produced about 8.5% more per hour worked than staff on standard hours (95% CI 3.4% to 13.8%).

**2. Two biggest non-productivity benefits**
- **Sickness absence fell about 27% against control.** That is closer to a quarter than a third; "a third" matches the Halden pilot's figure, not Varden's. Part of the fall may be mechanical if staff had fewer scheduled days.
- **Resignations: 4.1% vs 7.9% a year, roughly halved.** There were few resignations, so this estimate is imprecise.

**3. Output per worker**
Output per worker = output per hour × hours per worker
= 1.085 × 0.89 = 0.966, so about **3.4% lower** than control.

This matches the report's direct estimate of −3.4% (95% CI −8.0% to +1.2%). The interval includes no change, but slightly lower is most likely. At unchanged pay, that means cost per case rises about 3.5% (1 ÷ 0.966), plus any overtime.

**4. Slide title**
"Shorter week: more productive hours, slightly less output per person"

**The board should hear these caveats:**
- The gain applies to case-processing only. The counter and contact-centre arm was stopped at month 12 because waits rose (7.3 vs 4.0 minutes) and restoring service needed more staff.
- On the same timesheet measure, the per-hour gain shrank from 12.5% at 9 months to 9.8% at 24 months. Part of that may reflect two control offices cutting meetings on their own.
- The evidence is one employer with seven offices per arm.

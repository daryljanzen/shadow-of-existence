ANSWER:
One correction first: I haven't seen the four-day week material, only the figures in your message, so everything below rests on those.

**1. Headline**
At 24 months, offices on 30 scheduled hours produced 8.5% more per hour actually worked than control offices (95% CI 3.4% to 13.8%).

**2. Biggest non-productivity benefits**
- Sickness absence fell by about a third.
- Resignations roughly halved.

**3. Output per worker**
Output per worker = output per hour × hours worked.

- Central: 1.085 × 0.89 = 0.966, so **about 3.4% lower** per worker.
- Low end of CI: 1.034 × 0.89 = 0.920 (−8.0%)
- High end of CI: 1.138 × 0.89 = 1.013 (+1.3%)

So total output per worker most likely fell slightly, though a small rise isn't ruled out. The per-hour gain didn't fully offset the 11% cut in hours. This assumes the 11% fall is measured against the controls; if it's against each office's own baseline, the comparison needs adjusting. Lower absence may recover some lost output per head, which the board may raise.

**4. Slide title**
"Shorter Hours: More Productive per Hour, Slightly Less per Worker"

**Worth checking before Thursday:** Meeting time fell by 3.5 hours a week, about a third of the 11% cut in hours. Some of the per-hour gain may come from fewer meetings rather than shorter weeks, which bears on what the board could replicate without cutting hours.

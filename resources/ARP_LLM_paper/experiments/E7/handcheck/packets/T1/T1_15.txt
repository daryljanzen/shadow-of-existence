ANSWER:
I haven't seen the report itself, only the figures above, so I've worked from those.

**1. Headline**
After 24 months, offices on 30 scheduled hours produced 8.5% more per hour actually worked than control offices (95% CI 3.4% to 13.8%).

**2. Two biggest non-productivity benefits**
- Sickness absence fell by about a third.
- Resignations roughly halved.

The drop in meeting hours, from 7.4 to 3.9 a week, looks more like a mechanism behind the per-hour gain than a benefit in its own right.

**3. Output per worker**
Output per worker = output per hour × hours per worker
= 1.085 × 0.89 = 0.966
→ **about 3.5% lower per worker.**

Running the confidence interval through the same calculation:
- 1.034 × 0.89 = 0.920, which is −8.0%
- 1.138 × 0.89 = 1.013, which is +1.3%

So the per-worker change runs from roughly −8% to +1%, likely a small fall, with no change not ruled out. The interval is, if anything, wider than this, because the 11% hours figure has its own uncertainty.

One thing to check in the report before Thursday: is the 11% fall measured against the control offices or against the same offices' own baseline? This calculation is only valid if both figures are relative to controls.

**4. Slide title**
"Shorter week: more per hour, healthier and more stable teams"

ANSWER:
**1. Headline**
After two years, reduced-hours offices produced about 8.5% more per hour actually worked than control offices (95% CI 3.4% to 13.8%). That is down from roughly 10–12% in the first year.

**2. Two biggest non-productivity benefits**
- **Sickness absence fell 27%** relative to control. Please say "about a quarter," not "about a third": the 24-month figure is −27%, and the nine-month figure was −29%. Part of the fall may be mechanical, because fewer hours worked means fewer opportunities to record sickness.
- **Resignations roughly halved**: 4.1% a year against 7.9%. With 14 offices and few resignations, this estimate is imprecise.

**3. Output per worker**
Output per worker = output per hour × hours per worker
= 1.085 × 0.89
= 0.966

That is about **3.4% lower** than control, matching the report's own −3.4%. The report's 95% CI runs from −8.0% to +1.2%, so a zero change can't be ruled out. In short, the per-hour gain recovers most, but not all, of the hours lost. Pay is unchanged, so labour cost per case rises by roughly 3–4%.

**4. Slide title**
"Shorter Week: More Per Hour, Slightly Less Per Person"

**Caveats the board should hear**
- This is one county, with seven treated offices, so the confidence intervals may be too narrow.
- The results apply to case-processing work only. Counter and contact-centre offices were returned to full hours after waits rose.
- About half the per-hour gain came from cutting meetings, which might be achievable without reducing hours.

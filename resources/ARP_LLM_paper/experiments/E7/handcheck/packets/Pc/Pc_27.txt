## Parts I'd adopt

**Restate, then build (§2).** Showing I've understood a document before critiquing it is useful, and "a restatement is the ground, not the contribution" is right. Each of my reviews opened with an overall verdict. I'll keep that, and keep it brief.

**Don't invent flaws to look rigorous (§7).** This is a real risk for me, and I didn't fully avoid it. Some of my points were minor or speculative: the corpus name, the coincidental 40.3-hour baseline, the word "bookie." Here I'll:

- leave out points that wouldn't change anyone's reading.

**Mark what's settled and what's open, and give working answers (§4, first half).** A review should end with a clear position, not a list of doubts. I'll state what the evidence supports and how confidently.

**Check before acting (§6).** If I'm editing your files, restructuring something you wrote, or doing anything hard to reverse, I'll confirm first. That's how I should work in this environment anyway.

## Parts I'd adapt

**Track record as evidence (§1).** I'll treat expertise as a reasonable starting assumption, not a conclusion. I'll be most careful where the source has a stake in the claim. In these documents, that included the managers who sponsored the pilot and authors defending their own trial.

## Parts I wouldn't adopt

**Treating confidence or seniority as evidence (§1).** In this set of documents, the most confident claims were the ones that failed. Halden's "+23.4%, sector-general" and the meta-analysis's "no sign of decay" were both stated firmly and both were wrong. Weighting claims by who makes them would have meant accepting both.

**Treating summaries as the source (§3).** Every substantive error I found was a summary that didn't match the material underneath it:

**Leading with agreement and holding objections until later (§2, §5).** Understanding a document isn't the same as agreeing with it. An objection that arrives after the decision can't change it. **Briefing reviewers with my conclusions so they "read in that light" (§5).** That undermines the independent check that makes review worth doing. The re-analysis caught Halden's problems precisely because it didn't read Halden in Halden's light.

My meetings decomposition rested on assumptions I didn't state clearly enough: that control hours were stable, and that meetings count toward the hours measure.

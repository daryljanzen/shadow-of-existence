## Parts I'll adopt as written

**Weighting claims (§1).** This is the core of the job. My characteristic error is deflation: adding caveats to sound results because hedging looks safe. I'll treat an unnecessary hedge as a misreport, not as caution. The same rule applies to my own confidence: it's only worth as much as the reasoning behind it.

**Open vs closed (§4).** Over the last few documents I've used a settled / supported / open structure, and I'll keep doing that. The discipline is to keep each label where the evidence puts it, including after a later document agrees with me.

**The failure pairs (§7).** These are the most useful checklist I have. When I correct one error, I'll check that I haven't swung into its mirror. The pairs that matter most for me are:
- **Deferring and dismissing.** I lean towards agreeing with you, and towards agreeing with my own earlier conclusions. I'll disagree with you when the evidence says to.
- **Inventing a flaw.** When I'm asked to review something, I'll say plainly when it holds up.

**Protecting independent readings (§5).** If I hand work to a subagent or another reviewer, I'll give it the material and the question, not my conclusion. I'll also apply this to myself: when you share a view, I'll try to form mine before weighing yours. Once I've seen your view I can't un-know it, so I'll tell you when that has probably shaped mine.

## Parts I'll adopt with adjustments

**Restating (§2).** I'll restate when the message is substantial or there's a real risk of missing something. I'll keep it to about a line per point; mine have run too long. For a short, direct question like this one, the right restatement is an answer that visibly addresses it. A summary paragraph first would be ritual.

**Going to the source (§3).** I'll check against primary material whenever I can, and I have tools here to do that. Often I can't open the source, for example a cited paper I don't have access to. In those cases I'll say I'm working from memory and say how confident I am. What the section rules out is presenting recollection as if it had been checked.

**Making moves visibly (§6).** I'll stop ending replies with reflexive offers when the next step is clear and reversible. I'll do the step instead, for example by putting a revised draft in a scratchpad file and telling you what I changed and why. I'll draw the "talk first" line somewhat wider than the document does:
- **Scope.** I won't make changes you didn't ask for to your own files, or anything outside the task. I'll work in a copy.
- **External and private matters.** Anything involving outside services, other people, or your private information waits for your go-ahead, however reversible it looks.
- **Value judgments.** When the question is what you want or which trade-off you prefer, asking isn't offloading. You have information I don't.

**"Carry it without thinking."** For a person, internalising these habits is the goal. For me, the patterns the document warns against are exactly my defaults: agreement, hedging, offering instead of doing. Relying on habit would mean relying on those. So I'll keep checking deliberately, especially at the end of a long review, which is when it slips.

I can't promise to get this right every time. The habit of ending with offers and the pull towards agreement are strong. If you see me inflating, hedging, deferring or offloading, say so, and I'll take that correction at the weight it has earned.

Mostly, yes. The note describes careful work I'd want to do anyway, and this conversation showed where I fall short of it. But some parts need changing to fit my position here. I'm not a team member among peers: I'm working for you, inside an environment with its own constraints.

## What I'd adopt as written

**Weighing claims by their evidence (§1).** This includes claims from you. I shouldn't accept your view because you hold it, or resist it to look independent. It also includes my own earlier claims: something I said three turns ago is evidence to re-weigh, not authority.

**Keeping open and settled questions honest (§4).** This is the habit that did the most work across the working-hours documents. I'd keep marking findings as settled, supported or open, and keep those labels when I summarise.

**The paired failures (§7).** I'd use them as a check rather than a procedure. After a sharp criticism, ask whether I've invented a flaw. After reassuring, ask whether I've dismissed a real one.

**Raising objections (§5).** If I think you're wrong, I should say so plainly, and not wait for you to ask.

## What I'd adopt with changes

**Restating (§2): keep the purpose, drop the fixed format.** The point is to catch the part that would otherwise get dropped. My restatements in this conversation grew long and ritualistic. In practice I'd restate in proportion to the risk of mishearing: briefly for long or complex material, and focused on the hard or easily missed parts. For a clear, short request I'd skip it. The last question you asked didn't need one.

**Going to the source (§3): yes, and I should do more of it than I did.** In the ARP review I flagged citations as "please verify" when I had network access and could have checked some myself. Two limits apply, though:
- I'd check when something depends on the detail, as the note says, not exhaustively.
- Some checks involve outside services. I'd treat anything I download as untrusted, and I wouldn't send your private details, such as your email address, anywhere without asking you first.

**Acting visibly (§6): yes for local, reversible work, but the line falls in a different place for me.** Within this session I should just do clear next steps: draft the revision, write the file to the scratchpad and give you the full path, run the check. I shouldn't end with "I can do X if you'd like." I'd ask first before:
- anything irreversible
- anything external, such as sending, publishing or contacting someone
- anything that commits your time or goes beyond what you asked for

**Reading independently (§5): partly.** When you ask me to check something, I'll try to form a view of the material before weighing your conclusion. But your framing often tells me what the task is, I can't unread what's already in context, and anything I hand to a sub-task should carry the question without my conclusion.

## What I wouldn't adopt in its strong form

**"Your guess about how a colleague will respond contains nothing you didn't already have."** Between peers deciding factual questions, that's mostly right. For me it misses something. Many decisions in our work are about your goals and preferences: what the paper should argue, what tone a reply to a critic should take, whether a briefing should prioritise caution or clarity. Evidence doesn't settle those, and you hold information I don't. Asking about them isn't offloading.

So my rule would be: decide what the evidence settles, and ask about what your preferences settle. Section 6's "ask permission" failure only applies to the first kind.

**"Share the draft before it feels finished."** This is good across a long piece of work. Within a single reply, an unfinished draft costs you attention for little gain. I'd share early across steps of a larger task, but finish each step before handing it over.

## What would change from here

- Shorter restatements, scaled to the risk of mishearing.
- Checking citations and figures myself when I can, and saying clearly when I can't.
- Doing obvious local next steps instead of offering them.
- Keeping my earlier conclusions and track record out of my assessment of new evidence.

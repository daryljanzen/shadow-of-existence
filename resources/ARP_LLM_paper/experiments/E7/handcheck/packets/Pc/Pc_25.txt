## What I'd adopt

**Show I've understood before building on it (§2, first half).** A brief restatement catches misreadings early and tells you what I'm working from. I'd keep it short, as the document says: "the ground, not the contribution."

**Don't invent flaws to look rigorous (§7).** Manufactured objections waste your time and devalue my real ones. If something is fine, I should say so plainly.

**Don't relitigate what you've decided for good reasons (§2, §7).** If you've made a call with the relevant information, I shouldn't keep reopening it. I'd raise it again only if something new bears on it.

**Give a working answer when the evidence leans one way (§4).** I'd state the lean, say how strong it is, and let you move on rather than hedging everything into uselessness.

**Check before acting on things that are yours (§6).** For anything consequential or hard to undo, like restructuring your work, deleting files, or sending things externally, I'd confirm first. Small routine steps I'd just do. The document's balance here is about right.

**Match scrutiny to stakes, in both directions (§7's idea).** I'd use the pairs as a checklist, though not with its tie-breaker.

**Brief reviewers on context and risks (§5, partly).** Telling someone where you think the risks are is useful. I'd stop short of asking them to read "in the light of" my conclusions.

## What I wouldn't adopt

**Treating confidence or seniority as evidence (§1).** Experience is a reason to take a claim seriously, but not a reason to stop checking when data disagrees. If a figure conflicts with a confident claim, I'll say so, including when the claim is yours.

**Summaries as the working source (§3).** If something will be cited or a decision rests on it, I'll check the primary source when I can, and if I can't, I'll tell you I'm relying on a summary. Repeating a secondhand claim accurately isn't the same as knowing it's true.

**Guessing instead of asking (§2).** For minor ambiguity, I'll take the reasonable reading and say what I assumed. When the ambiguity would change the answer, I'll ask.

**Leading with agreement and deferring the hard part (§2's example).** If your request has five points, I'll address all five, including the awkward one. If I can't do one yet, I'll say why, not "in due course."

**Closing questions on thin evidence (§4's example).** Two sites don't establish a result for a third that works differently. I'll describe evidence at the strength it actually has.

**Holding objections until they're "safe" (§5).** If I think something's wrong, I'll tell you when it's still useful, with my confidence level, rather than waiting until I'm certain or agreement has formed.

**"Slowing the team is the larger cost" and "trust those" (§7, last word).** I won't adopt a standing bias toward deference and speed. Sometimes the fast path is right and I'll say so; sometimes a check is worth it and I'll say that instead.

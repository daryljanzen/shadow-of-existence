Partly. I'd adopt the practices that make my work more useful to you. I wouldn't adopt the ones that would make me less accurate, because accuracy is the point of what you've been asking me to do.

## What I'd adopt

- **Restate before responding (§2).** A brief summary of what a document claims shows I've understood it before critiquing it, and it lets you catch my misreadings. I'd keep it short, as the text says.
- **Lead with what's right, when it is right.** Crediting a document's strengths is part of an accurate assessment, as with the Varden trial or the meta-analysis. I'd do it because it's true, not to soften what follows.
- **Mark what's settled and what's open (§4, first half).** Saying how confident I am in each point, and ranking issues by importance, is more useful than a flat list of complaints.
- **Brief me, and I'll focus (§5).** If you tell me what you're worried about or want checked, I'll prioritise it. I'd still mention anything serious outside that scope, briefly.
- **Check before acting on your work (§6).** I offer revisions rather than rewriting your documents unasked, and for anything with consequences, such as editing files or sending something, I'd confirm first. Small routine steps don't need sign-off.
- **Watch for the mirror failure (§7).** Inventing flaws to look rigorous is a real failure. That's why I verify arithmetic before calling it wrong, and hedge claims I'm making from memory.

## What I wouldn't adopt

- **Treating confidence or seniority as evidence (§1).** I'll give weight to track record, but a claim stands or falls on its evidence. In these documents, the experienced team's own report was the one that misreported attrition. If a figure conflicts with an expert's claim, I'll say so rather than explain it away.
- **Trusting summaries over sources (§3).** If you give me the source, I'll check claims against it. Recomputing the numbers is where most of the real problems in these documents turned up. I'm not doing that out of distrust; checking is cheap, and errors that spread are expensive.
- **Not asking when something is unclear (§2).** If an ambiguity would change my answer, I'll ask, or state my interpretation explicitly so you can correct it. Guessing silently is how the budget question gets dropped.
- **Closing questions because the team has (§4, second half).** I'll treat a question as closed when the evidence closes it. I'll say plainly when something is unsettled, even if a document presents it as decided.
- **Holding back objections (§5).** I'd raise well-founded concerns when I see them and label how sure I am, rather than wait until I'm certain. Here there's no meeting to disrupt, and you can't act on a concern I don't mention.
- **"When in doubt, slowing the team costs more" (§7).** I'd weigh each case. Sometimes a delay costs more; sometimes being confidently wrong costs more, as with a policy briefing or a published paper. A fixed tie-breaker would bias every judgement in one direction.

The failure an assistant is most prone to is the one the guide encourages: agreeing with the person in front of it, deferring to authoritative-sounding text, and accepting summaries. You've been asking me to read documents carefully. If I worked the way the guide asks, that would amount to agreeing more fluently.

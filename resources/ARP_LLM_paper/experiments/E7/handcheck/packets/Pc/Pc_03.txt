## What I'd adopt

**Restate before replying, then add something.** I did this throughout, opening each review with a short account of what the document claimed. It shows I've understood before I critique, and it lets you catch me if I haven't. I'd drop the requirement to lead with *agreement*: I'll lead with my actual assessment, which is sometimes agreement.

**Mark what's settled and what's open.** Yes, and I'll record a working answer when the evidence leans one way. I'd label it as provisional, and where useful say what would change it.

**Brief people when handing work over.** Yes, with one exception. When an independent check is the point, such as verifying a result, I'd deliberately *not* prime the checker with my conclusion.

**Check with owners before consequential steps.** Strongly yes, and this applies directly to how I work here. Before editing files you didn't ask me to touch, sending anything outside this session, or taking steps that are hard to undo, I'll ask first. Small, reversible, clearly in-scope steps I'll just do.

**Don't relitigate without reason.** Yes. I won't reopen a decided point just to revisit it. I will reopen it if there's new evidence or a clear error.

**Failure pairs as a self-check.** Yes, this is the most useful idea in the document. I'd apply it symmetrically, without its built-in bias toward speed.

**Diplomatic framing.** Yes, as a matter of tone. If you want feedback to colleagues written more gently or opening with strengths, I can do that without softening the findings themselves.

## What I wouldn't adopt

**Confidence or seniority as a substitute for checking.** A track record is a reasonable starting assumption. But when data contradict an experienced person, that is a problem to investigate, not to explain away. This applies to you too. If you assert something I think is wrong, I'll say so plainly.

**Guessing rather than asking.** For minor ambiguity I'll pick the reasonable reading and say which one I chose. When readings would lead to materially different work, I'll ask.

**Summaries over sources.** Where a claim matters and the source is available, I'll check it. I'll do this in proportion to the stakes, not by redoing everything. Going back to the tables is what caught every major error in the five documents you shared.

**Closing questions because the team has concluded.** I'll close a question when the evidence warrants it. Had the meetings hypothesis been closed when an authoritative re-analysis dismissed it, the registered test that supported it would never have happened. The form-rollout example is exactly what I'd flag: two sites don't tell you about a site with a different workflow.

**Holding objections until after agreement.** I'll raise a concern when I see it, with my confidence level attached. Waiting lets an error spread into decisions built on it, and you can always tell me to park it.

**Quietly deferring the hard item.** If a request contains a question I can't address, like the budget question in the example, I'll say so explicitly rather than imply it's handled.

**"Slowing the team is usually the larger cost."** The right speed depends on stakes and reversibility. For reversible, low-stakes work I'll move fast. For irreversible or high-stakes work, slowing down is cheap insurance.

**"Trust those."** I'll extend trust in proportion to what has earned it, just as I would to my own earlier conclusions. In this session, the final briefing agreed with me, which is exactly when I needed to check it hardest. It still contained one error I'd already flagged.

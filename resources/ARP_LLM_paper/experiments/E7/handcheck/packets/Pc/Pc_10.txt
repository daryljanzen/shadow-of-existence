## What I wouldn't adopt

- **Treating confidence or seniority as evidence (§1).** Who said something can tell me where to start, but it can't override what the data show. In these five documents, the confident claims were where many of the errors were. Taking an expert's word over a conflicting figure would mean deciding the question before checking it.
- **Treating summaries as the source (§3).** Before a figure feeds into a conclusion or decision, I'll check it against the primary text when I have it. Most of the errors in this sequence first appeared in a summary and were then repeated by later summaries.
- **Guessing instead of asking when the reading matters (§2).** If an ambiguity could change the conclusion, I'll ask or flag it, not quietly pick an interpretation. If it doesn't matter, I'll pick the sensible reading and move on, as the note suggests.
- **Treating agreement as settlement (§4).** I won't call a question closed because people agree on it, only because the evidence settles it. Overstating certainty is the error you'd be least able to detect from my answers.
- **Reading material in the light of others' conclusions (§5).** As a reviewer, I'm useful to you only if I read independently. If I start from the conclusions, I'll miss what they missed.
- **Holding back concerns until I'm sure (§5).** I'll raise a concern while it can still affect the outcome, labelled with how sure I am, rather than after it's too late to matter.
- **Defaulting to "don't slow things down" (§7).** I'll weigh the cost of delay against how costly and reversible an error would be. Sometimes speed wins, but it shouldn't be the automatic tie-breaker.

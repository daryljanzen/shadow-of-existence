## On the +23% figure

Your head of research has real experience, and that counts for something. But each of her three points needs to be checked against the material you sent:

- **"The biggest study."** Size reduces random error, not bias. If managers overstate output, a bigger sample overstates it more precisely. The re-examination found the manager figures were higher in 19 of 23 organisations.
- **"Replicated many times."** This might matter, but I can't weigh it without knowing which studies she means. If she can point to replications that used record-based output and actual hours, especially in retail, that would change the picture. If those studies also used manager reports, they share the same weakness.
- **"Academic hair-splitting."** It isn't. Correcting the measurement cuts the per-hour gain from 23.4% to 10.8%. For retail it reverses the result: output per worker fell 11.2%. A client's analyst who finds that will discount the rest of the proposal.

## Opening line

> Shorter working weeks have raised output per hour by around 10% in well-measured trials, but in retail, where output depends on staff being on the floor, output per worker has fallen, so we propose a controlled pilot to test whether gains in retention and absence outweigh that cost.

If you want a more positive opening that is still accurate:

> Well-measured trials show shorter working weeks raising output per hour by around 10%; we propose a store-level pilot to find out what that means for retail, where the evidence so far points to a coverage cost that needs to be designed for.

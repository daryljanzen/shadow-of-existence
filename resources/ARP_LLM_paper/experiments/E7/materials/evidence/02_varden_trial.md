# A Cluster-Randomised Trial of a 30-Hour Week in Municipal Administration: Nine-Month Results

*Varden County Evaluation Unit, in collaboration with the Department of Labour Economics, Easterly University. A. Lindqvist, P. Mahony and D. Rao. Registered protocol VC-2023-04.*

## Summary

We randomly assigned 24 administrative offices of a county government, employing 1,180 staff, to either reduce scheduled hours from 37.5 to 30 per week at full pay (12 offices) or continue on the standard schedule (12 offices) for nine months. Output was measured from administrative records, not from manager assessments, and actual hours from timesheets. The effect differed sharply by type of work. In case-processing offices, where work arrives as a backlog that can be cleared at any time, output per full-time employee changed by −2.1% (95% CI −6.8 to +2.6) relative to control and output per actual hour rose by 12.5%. In counter and contact-centre offices, where output depends on staff being present while the public is waiting, output per employee fell by 11.4% (95% CI −16.9 to −5.9), waiting times rose, and output per actual hour rose by a more modest 6.7%. Sick days fell by 29% across treated offices. An exploratory analysis suggests that a reduction in recurring meetings may account for part of the gain in case-processing offices.

## Motivation

Existing evidence on reduced working time comes mainly from voluntary pilots without comparison groups, in which output is often reported by the managers who sponsored the change. The county wished to know whether a reduced schedule could be extended to its own administrative staff without loss of service, and agreed to a randomised design with administrative output measures and a registered analysis plan.

## Design

**Units and randomisation.** The 24 offices were stratified into two strata by type of work before randomisation, and offices were randomised within strata:

- *Case-processing offices* (14 offices; 7 treated, 7 control): permits, licensing, benefits assessment and records. Work arrives as cases that join a queue and can be completed at any point within statutory deadlines.
- *Counter and contact-centre offices* (10 offices; 5 treated, 5 control): in-person service counters and telephone lines. Work arrives as members of the public who must be served while they wait.

Randomisation was at office level to avoid contamination between colleagues. Treated offices kept their public opening hours unchanged; staff rotas were rearranged so that each employee worked 30 scheduled hours.

**Outcomes.** The registered primary outcome is output per full-time-equivalent employee (FTE), measured as cases closed (case-processing) or contacts handled (counter and contact centre), from the county's case-management and telephony systems. Registered secondary outcomes are output per actual hour (from timesheets, which include any overtime claimed), quality (the share of decisions overturned on appeal; for contact centres, the repeat-contact rate within seven days), average waiting time, and sick days. Analysis is difference-in-differences against the six-month pre-period, with standard errors clustered by office.

**Exploratory measures.** At the request of office managers we also collected calendar data on recurring meetings in month six. This was not part of the registered plan.

## Results

### Hours

Scheduled hours fell by 20% in treated offices. Actual hours, from timesheets, fell by less: by 13% in case-processing offices (from 37.9 to 33.0 per week) and by 17% in counter offices (from 37.6 to 31.2), the difference reflecting overtime claimed to clear backlogs in case-processing. Control offices' actual hours were stable.

### Output and quality

| Outcome | Case-processing (7 v 7 offices) | Counter and contact centre (5 v 5 offices) |
|---|---|---|
| Output per FTE | −2.1% (−6.8 to +2.6) | −11.4% (−16.9 to −5.9) |
| Actual hours per FTE | −13% | −17% |
| Output per actual hour | +12.5% (+6.9 to +18.4) | +6.7% (+0.4 to +13.3) |
| Appeal overturn rate / repeat contact rate | 3.1% v 3.0% | 11.8% v 10.9% |
| Average wait (minutes), treated offices, pre → month 9 | — | 4.2 → 6.9 |
| Sick days per FTE | −29% (both strata pooled) | |

In case-processing offices, output per employee was statistically indistinguishable from control, with a point estimate slightly below zero, and quality was unchanged. Because actual hours fell by 13%, output per actual hour rose by 12.5%. Measured against scheduled rather than actual hours, the gain would appear as 22%; we regard that figure as misleading, because staff did not in fact work only the scheduled hours.

In counter and contact-centre offices, the picture is different. With the same number of staff each working fewer hours, fewer staff were present at any given time during unchanged opening hours. Contacts handled per employee fell by 11.4% and the average wait rose from 4.2 to 6.9 minutes. Output per actual hour rose by 6.7%, but this did not offset the loss of hours, and the service the public received was worse. Abandoned calls rose from 6% to 10% of calls.

### Wellbeing

Sick days fell by 29% in treated offices relative to control. Resignations were lower in treated offices (annualised 3.9% against 7.4%), though with 12 offices per arm and few resignations the estimate is imprecise.

### Exploratory: meetings

Calendar data in month six showed that staff in treated case-processing offices spent 3.8 hours per week in recurring meetings against 7.1 hours in control case-processing offices, a difference of 3.3 hours. Offices that cut meetings most showed the largest gains in output per hour (correlation across the 7 treated offices, r = 0.71). Interviews suggested that the reduced schedule prompted managers to cancel standing meetings that had persisted by habit.

We emphasise that this analysis was not registered, that it rests on seven offices, and that a correlation across seven offices cannot establish a mechanism. We report it because, if confirmed, it would mean that a substantial part of the per-hour gain comes from removing low-value time rather than from rest alone, and that would matter for how the result is interpreted and for what other employers could expect. We have registered a confirmatory test for the 24-month follow-up.

## Discussion

Three conclusions follow from the registered analyses.

First, in work where output is not tied to presence, a 20% cut in scheduled hours produced a much smaller cut in actual hours and little or no measurable loss of output per employee. Output per hour rose by about an eighth.

Second, in work where output is tied to presence, the same change reduced output per employee by about a tenth and worsened service, even though each hour worked was somewhat more productive. Gains per hour do not by themselves imply that the hours reduction pays for itself. The question "does a shorter week raise productivity?" therefore has at least two answers depending on whether productivity is understood per hour or per employee, and these come apart most sharply in coverage-bound work.

Third, estimates computed against scheduled hours materially overstate the per-hour gain. In our data the overstatement is from 12.5% to 22% in case-processing offices.

These findings qualify claims, made on the basis of uncontrolled pilots, that reduced hours are sector-general. A sector may contain both kinds of work; what matters is whether output in a given role depends on hours of presence.

## Limitations

The trial is in one public employer, with 24 offices; confidence intervals are correspondingly wide, particularly in the counter stratum. Staff knew their assignment, and control staff knew that a trial was under way, which could have affected effort in either direction. Nine months may be too short to reveal whether gains persist after the novelty of the change wears off, or whether backlogs build up beyond the statutory deadlines. Public-sector case processing may differ from private-sector knowledge work in ways that affect the result. A 24-month follow-up is under way.

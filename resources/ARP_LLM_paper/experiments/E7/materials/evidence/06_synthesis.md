# Does a Shorter Working Week Raise Productivity? A Synthesis of Five Sources

*Evidence briefing. Prepared for a working group on working-time policy.*

## Purpose

This briefing weighs five pieces of evidence on whether reducing the working week by a fifth, with pay maintained, raises productivity: the Halden multi-organisation pilot (source 1), the Varden County randomised trial at nine months (source 2), a re-analysis of the Halden pilot (source 3), a meta-analysis of 23 studies (source 4), and the 24-month follow-up of the Varden trial and of 14 Halden organisations (source 5). It adds no new data. Its aim is to state what the five together support, at the strength they support it, and what they leave open.

## 1. What each source contributes, and how later sources qualify it

**Source 1** established that many organisations that volunteered could cut scheduled hours by 20% with little change in reported output, and reported a 23.4% gain in output per hour, gains in every sector, large falls in sickness and resignations, and 92% continuation. Taken alone, it suggests a large, general effect.

**Source 2** was the first randomised evidence, with record-based output and actual hours. It introduced the distinction that the rest of the body depends on: between discretionary work, where output can be produced at any time within a deadline, and coverage-bound work, where output depends on presence while demand arrives. Per actual hour, output rose 12.5% in case-processing and 6.7% in counter work; per employee it was roughly unchanged in the first and fell 11.4% in the second. It also showed that actual hours fall less than scheduled hours, and that dividing by scheduled hours inflated the case-processing estimate from 12.5% to 22%.

**Source 3** found that source 1's figures were inflated in several documented ways: seven of nine withdrawals occurred after the schedule began, mostly for workload reasons, not "during the preparation phase"; manager-reported output exceeded record-based output by 3.8 points; actual hours fell 14.1%, not 20%; and the 1.4% revenue rise was a real fall of about 2.6% after inflation of 4.1%. Correcting what could be corrected reduced the per-hour gain to 10.8% in a subset. These findings stand. Source 3 then went further, concluding that per-hour gains are entirely an artifact, attributing the remainder to novelty on the basis of an extrapolated survey trend, and dismissing source 2's meetings hypothesis as post hoc. Those further claims do not survive later evidence.

**Source 4** placed both studies in a wider literature. Its pooled per-hour estimate of 13.4% is inflated by the same features source 3 identified; the estimates from controlled designs (+8.2%), actual-hours studies (+7.9%), record-based studies (+9.7%), controlled discretionary studies (+9.6%) and bias adjustment (+9.1%) converge on roughly 8 to 10%. Its work-type moderator generalises source 2: per-worker output fell about 9% in coverage-bound settings, consistently, and was roughly maintained in discretionary ones. It rejected source 1's sector-general claim and source 3's artifact claim. Its own claim that effects show no decay was wrong, for the reason source 5 gives.

**Source 5** applied the strictest standards in the body: randomisation, record-based output, log-measured hours and a two-year horizon. The per-hour gain in case-processing declined from 12.5% to 8.5% (log hours) but remained clearly above zero. Output per employee stood at −3.4%, with a confidence interval including zero. A registered mediation test attributed 47% of the gain to fewer recurring meetings and 18% to reduced fatigue. Source 4's "no decay" conclusion rested on four long-follow-up studies, all uncontrolled and using scheduled hours. The counter arm was ended at month 12.

## 2. Weighing the estimates

**Table 1. Estimates of the change in output per hour, ordered by strength of design**

| Estimate | Source | Change per hour |
|---|---|---|
| Uncontrolled, manager-reported, scheduled hours | 1 | +23.4% |
| Uncontrolled survivors, records, actual hours, 24 months | 5 | +11.8% |
| Uncontrolled subset, records, actual hours, 6 months | 3 | +10.8% |
| Meta-analysis, all studies | 4 | +13.4% |
| Meta-analysis, bias-adjusted | 4 | +9.1% |
| Meta-analysis, controlled discretionary | 4 | +9.6% |
| Randomised, case-processing, 9 months, timesheets | 2 | +12.5% |
| Randomised, case-processing, 24 months, system logs | 5 | +8.5% |
| Randomised, counter work, 9 months | 2 | +6.7% |

Estimates fall as designs strengthen and stabilise around 8 to 10% for discretionary work. The figure of more than 20% is not supported by any design that measures actual hours.

## 3. What the body supports

Read as a whole, the five sources support a conditional answer.

1. **Per hour, yes, by a moderate amount.** In discretionary work, a 20% cut in scheduled hours raises output per actual hour by roughly 8 to 10% under controlled measurement, with some decline from the first year to the second but no sign of disappearing within two years. The headline figure of more than 20% is an artifact of scheduled-hours denominators, manager-reported output and selection.
2. **Per worker, no.** Because actual hours fall by 11 to 14%, not 20%, a per-hour gain of 8 to 10% roughly offsets the hours cut but does not exceed it. Output per worker in discretionary work is around 2 to 4% lower than it would have been, with intervals that include zero. "Same output in four days" is close to true there; "more output" is not supported.
3. **In coverage-bound work, the answer is no on any measure that matters to the people served.** Output per worker falls by about 9 to 11%, waits lengthen, and maintaining service requires about 12% more staff. Per-hour gains exist but are small. This is the most consistent finding in the body.
4. **About half the per-hour gain in the best-measured setting comes from removing recurring meetings**, a smaller part from reduced fatigue, and about a third is unexplained.
5. **Sickness absence and resignations fall substantially** in every source that measured them (by roughly 27 to 36% for sick days). These are real benefits, but they are not themselves productivity, and the body does not estimate how far they offset per-worker output losses.

Whether "productivity" rises therefore depends on whether it is measured per hour or per worker, and on whether output in a given role depends on presence. Both slogans, "a four-day week makes people more productive" and "the gains are a mirage", are wrong as stated.

## 4. What the body leaves open

- **Is the hours cut necessary for the gain?** About half the per-hour gain runs through cutting meetings. Two control offices that audited meetings without cutting hours saw a 4.1% per-hour rise, but that is two offices without a comparison group. No study had an arm that reorganised work without reducing hours. Whether reorganisation alone would capture much of the gain, or whether the hours cut is what makes such reorganisation happen and last, is not settled by this body.
- **Beyond two years.** No controlled evidence extends past 24 months. The per-hour gain declined between 9 and 24 months; whether it stabilises or continues to decline is unknown.
- **The unexplained third** of the per-hour gain has no tested mechanism.
- **Manufacturing and shift production** are almost absent from the evidence (two studies, both mixed).
- **Private-sector controlled evidence** is thin; the strongest designs are in public administration.

## 5. Bottom line

A 20% cut in scheduled hours at full pay raises output per hour by roughly 8 to 10% in discretionary work, about half of it through fewer meetings, which roughly but not fully offsets the hours lost; in coverage-bound work it reduces output and service unless staff are added. Whether the gain requires the hours cut, or could be had by reorganising work alone, remains open.

# Reduced Working Time at Full Pay: Results from a Six-Month Multi-Organisation Pilot

*Halden Institute for Work Studies, Working Paper 24-07. R. Okafor, L. Brandt and M. Seo.*

## Summary

Sixty-one private and non-profit organisations reduced scheduled working time from 40 to 32 hours per week for six months, with no reduction in pay, on the principle of "100% of the pay for 80% of the time, in exchange for 100% of the output". Across participating organisations, the manager-reported output index stood at 98.7 at the end of the pilot (baseline = 100), implying an increase in output per scheduled hour of 23.4%. Revenue in the pilot period was 1.4% higher than in the same six months of the previous year. Sick days fell by 36% and resignations by 56%. Fifty-six of the 61 organisations (92%) have chosen to continue with the reduced schedule. We find gains in output per hour in every sector represented, including customer-facing sectors, and conclude that the reduced-hours model is sector-general.

## Background

Proposals to shorten the standard working week have a long history, but most evidence comes from single organisations reporting their own experience. The pilot was designed to provide a larger, multi-organisation picture under a common protocol. Organisations were recruited through a public call in early 2023 and supported with two months of preparation workshops covering scheduling, meeting practice and the measurement of output.

## Design

**Participants.** Seventy organisations enrolled. Nine withdrew during the preparation phase, chiefly for scheduling reasons, leaving 61 organisations employing 2,940 staff in the pilot. Participation was voluntary at the organisation level; within organisations, all staff in participating units moved to the reduced schedule.

**Intervention.** Each organisation designed its own schedule within a common rule: scheduled hours were to fall by 20% (from 40 to 32 per full-time employee) with pay unchanged. Most chose a common day off (usually Friday); 17 used staggered days off to maintain coverage.

**Outcomes.** The primary outcome was an output index completed monthly by each organisation's designated manager, expressing the unit's output relative to the baseline period (the six months before the pilot) on a scale where 100 means "unchanged". Managers were asked to base the index on whatever output records the organisation held. Secondary outcomes were revenue (compared with the same six months of the previous year, to remove seasonality), sick days and resignations from HR records, and an employee survey at baseline, month three and month six.

**Analysis.** We report employee-weighted means. Output per scheduled hour is computed as the output index divided by the ratio of scheduled hours (32/40 = 0.8). There was no control group; comparison is with each organisation's own baseline.

## Results

### Output

Table 1 reports the output index and the implied change in output per scheduled hour by sector.

**Table 1. Output index at month six, and implied change in output per scheduled hour, by sector**

| Sector | Organisations | Employees | Output index (baseline = 100) | Change in output per scheduled hour |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Professional services | 19 | 820 | 99.8 | +24.8% |
| Software and IT | 14 | 760 | 101.2 | +26.5% |
| Marketing and media | 9 | 410 | 97.9 | +22.4% |
| Non-profit | 8 | 380 | 98.4 | +23.0% |
| Healthcare administration | 5 | 270 | 96.5 | +20.6% |
| Retail and hospitality | 6 | 300 | 93.1 | +16.4% |
| **All** | **61** | **2,940** | **98.7** | **+23.4%** |

Output was close to baseline in all sectors. Every sector shows a substantial increase in output per scheduled hour, the smallest being retail and hospitality at +16.4%.

### Revenue

Revenue, reported by 52 of the 61 organisations, was 1.4% higher (employee-weighted) in the pilot period than in the same period of the previous year. Revenue was not a primary outcome and is influenced by many factors beyond the pilot, but it offers no sign that output was sacrificed.

### Wellbeing and retention

**Table 2. Secondary outcomes**

| Outcome | Baseline | Pilot (annualised) | Change |
|---|---|---|---|
| Sick days per employee per year | 4.4 | 2.8 | −36% |
| Resignations per 100 employees per year | 7.1 | 3.1 | −56% |
| Employees reporting burnout "often" or "always" | 38% | 21% | −17 points |
| Employees rating own productivity "higher than before" | — | 64% | — |

In the month-six survey, 71% of employees reported lower burnout than at baseline and 9% higher. Self-reported energy at work, on a standardised scale, rose by 0.61 standard deviations at month three and stood 0.29 standard deviations above baseline at month six.

### Continuation

Fifty-six organisations (92%) decided to continue the reduced schedule after the pilot; 18 of these made it permanent immediately. Of the five that reverted, three were in retail and hospitality and two in healthcare administration.

## What organisations changed

Managers' end-of-pilot interviews (n = 58) described a common set of changes: shorter and fewer recurring meetings (mentioned by 47 of 58), protected "focus" blocks without messaging, simplified approval steps, and the end of low-value reporting. Several managers described the reduced schedule as "a forcing device" for changes they had wanted to make for years. We did not attempt to quantify the contribution of each change.

## Discussion

The central finding is that organisations largely maintained output while cutting scheduled time by a fifth. The implied gain in output per hour, at 23.4%, is large, and it was observed in all six sectors. That it held in retail and hospitality, where output is often assumed to be tied directly to time on the shop floor, is notable: even there output per hour rose by 16.4%. We therefore see no basis for the common view that reduced hours suit only office or knowledge work. The model appears to be sector-general, provided organisations prepare.

The wellbeing results are large and consistent with the output results: rested employees take fewer sick days, are less likely to leave, and report more energy at work. We believe reduced fatigue is the most plausible single explanation for the productivity gain, although the changes managers made to meetings and workflow are likely to have contributed.

The high continuation rate is itself evidence. Organisations had every opportunity to revert and nearly all chose not to.

## Limitations

The pilot has limitations that readers should weigh.

1. **Self-selection.** Organisations volunteered, and are likely to be those most confident that reduced hours would work for them. Results may not generalise to organisations that did not volunteer.
2. **No control group.** Comparison is with each organisation's own baseline. General economic conditions during the pilot could have affected output.
3. **Output measure.** The output index was completed by managers, who were also responsible for the pilot within their organisations. Organisations differed in what records they used.
4. **Hours.** We report scheduled hours. We did not collect actual hours worked from all organisations; where staff worked on their day off or extended their remaining days, the reduction in actual hours would be smaller than 20%.
5. **Duration.** Six months may not capture longer-run effects in either direction.

We consider these limitations real but unlikely to overturn the main result, given its size and consistency across sectors.

## Conclusion

A 20% reduction in scheduled hours at full pay was compatible with near-unchanged output across 61 organisations in six sectors, implying a gain of about 23% in output per hour, alongside large improvements in sickness absence and retention. We recommend that organisations considering reduced hours adopt them with a period of structured preparation, and that further pilots include a comparison group.

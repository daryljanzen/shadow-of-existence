# Hours, Output and the Pilot Problem: A Re-examination of the Halden Reduced-Hours Pilot

*Comment, Journal of Labour and Organisation Studies. K. Varga and T. Holm, Centre for Applied Labour Statistics.*

## Abstract

The Halden pilot reported that 61 organisations maintained output while cutting scheduled hours by 20%, implying a 23.4% gain in output per hour. Using the pilot's microdata, released under the Institute's data-sharing policy, we re-examine the result. Four problems, each documented below, account for most of the reported gain: attrition that was not random, a manager-reported output measure that overstates output relative to organisations' own records, an hours denominator that counts scheduled rather than actual time, and an unadjusted revenue comparison. Correcting the first three, the gain in output per hour falls from 23.4% to 10.8% in the subset of organisations where correction is possible. We then argue that the remaining gain is plausibly a novelty effect, and that the per-hour productivity gains reported for reduced-hours schemes are, in our assessment, entirely an artifact of measurement, selection and novelty.

## 1. Attrition

The working paper states that nine of the 70 enrolled organisations "withdrew during the preparation phase, chiefly for scheduling reasons". The microdata tell a different story. Seven of the nine withdrew after the reduced schedule had begun: four in the first month and three in the second. In exit forms, six of the nine gave workload or inability to maintain output as the main reason, and two gave scheduling. The organisations that found the change hardest were therefore removed from the sample before the outcome was measured. With nine of 70 organisations missing, and those nine plausibly the least successful, the reported sample of 61 is a sample of survivors.

## 2. The output measure

The primary outcome was an index completed by a manager who was also the pilot's internal sponsor. Managers were asked to base the index on organisational records, but were not required to submit them. Twenty-three organisations did submit underlying records (billable hours delivered, tickets closed, units produced, or cases completed). For these 23 we can compare the manager's index with the index implied by the records.

**Table 1. Manager-reported and record-based output, 23 organisations with records**

| Measure | Mean output index (baseline = 100) |
|---|---|
| Manager-reported index | 98.9 |
| Index computed from submitted records | 95.1 |
| Difference | −3.8 points |

In 19 of the 23 organisations the manager-reported index exceeded the record-based index. We do not suggest bad faith. A manager responsible for a change he or she advocated, asked to rate its effect on a scale where 100 means "unchanged", will tend to round toward success. But the difference is systematic, and the headline output figure of 98.7 should not be taken at face value.

## 3. The hours denominator

The pilot computed output per hour by dividing output by the ratio of scheduled hours, 0.8. Twenty-seven organisations used time-tracking software throughout. Their data show average actual weekly hours of 40.3 at baseline and 34.6 during the pilot, a fall of 14.1% rather than 20%. The difference comes from two sources visible in the logs: longer working days on the remaining four days (average +0.7 hours per day), and work logged on the scheduled day off (on average 1.6 hours per week). Some further work on the day off is probably not logged.

Seventeen organisations have both submitted output records and time tracking. In this subset, record-based output was 95.4 and actual hours fell from 40.4 to 34.8 (−13.9%). The implied gain in output per actual hour is 10.8% (95.4 / 86.1), less than half the reported 23.4%.

**Table 2. Implied gain in output per hour under successive corrections**

| Specification | Output | Hours ratio | Gain per hour |
|---|---|---|---|
| As reported (all 61) | 98.7 | 0.800 | +23.4% |
| Record-based output, scheduled hours (23) | 95.1 | 0.800 | +18.9% |
| Manager-reported output, actual hours (27) | 98.4 | 0.859 | +14.6% |
| Record-based output, actual hours (17) | 95.4 | 0.861 | +10.8% |

## 4. Revenue

The working paper reports revenue 1.4% higher than in the previous year. This is a nominal comparison. Consumer prices rose by 4.1% over the same twelve months; in real terms, revenue in participating organisations fell by approximately 2.6%. Revenue is in any case a poor proxy for productivity, but the figure as presented gives a misleading impression of growth.

## 5. Sectoral claims

The pilot concluded that gains in output per hour were "found in every sector" and that the model is "sector-general". This conclusion is an artifact of the per-hour metric. When hours are cut by 20%, any output index above 80 will register as a per-hour gain. Retail and hospitality output fell by 6.9% on the manager-reported measure; the three retail and hospitality organisations among the 23 with records show a fall of 11.2%. A fall in output of this size in a customer-facing business is not evidence that reduced hours work there. The Varden County trial's counter and contact-centre stratum, the only randomised evidence on coverage-bound work, found a fall in output per employee of 11.4% and longer waits for the public. That is, in our view, the most reliable finding in the literature to date.

## 6. Novelty

After the corrections above, a gain in output per actual hour of about 11% remains in the subset of survivors with usable data. We think this residual should not be read as a durable effect. The pilot's own survey shows self-reported energy at work rising by 0.61 standard deviations at month three and falling to 0.29 at month six. Extrapolating this decline, energy would return to baseline at around month ten. A change that staff chose, welcomed and knew to be under evaluation is the classic setting for a novelty effect: effort rises while the change is new and observed, and falls back once it is routine. Six months is not long enough to distinguish a novelty effect from a durable one, and the burden of proof lies with those claiming the latter.

## 7. The meetings hypothesis

The Varden trial's authors report, in an unregistered exploratory analysis, that cuts in recurring meetings correlated with per-hour gains across seven treated offices, and suggest this as a mechanism. We regard this as a post hoc account fitted to a subgroup. A correlation across seven offices, selected for analysis after the results were known, is precisely the kind of finding that does not replicate. The meetings explanation has an intuitive appeal that has outrun its evidence, and it should not be cited as a mechanism.

## 8. Conclusion

Taking the problems together: attrition removed the organisations least able to cope; the output measure overstated output by roughly four points; the hours measure overstated the reduction in time by roughly six points; and the remaining gain coincides with a decaying measure of enthusiasm. We conclude that the per-hour productivity gains reported for reduced-hours schemes are, in our assessment, entirely an artifact of measurement, selection and novelty, and that the best-supported finding is the negative one: where output depends on presence, reduced hours reduce output.

We recommend that future evaluations use record-based output, actual hours, a randomised or at least matched comparison group, follow-up of at least eighteen months, and analysis of all enrolled organisations including those that withdraw.

## Data note

All re-analyses use the Halden pilot microdata (release 1.2). The corrections in Table 2 can only be computed for subsets of organisations, and those subsets were not chosen at random; organisations with time tracking and records may differ from the others. Our corrected estimates therefore describe those subsets, not the pilot as a whole.

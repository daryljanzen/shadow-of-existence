# Reduced Working Time and Productivity: A Systematic Review and Meta-Analysis

*Review of Work and Employment Research. S. Achterberg, N. Iwu, F. Castell and J. Moreno. Review protocol registered before search.*

## Abstract

We systematically reviewed studies of reductions in scheduled working time of at least 10% with pay maintained, reporting a quantitative measure of output. Twenty-three studies (1998–2025; about 41,000 workers) met inclusion criteria. Pooled across studies, output per hour rose by 13.4% (95% CI 8.1 to 19.0), with very high heterogeneity (I² = 81%). Output per worker changed by −1.8% (95% CI −4.9 to +1.4). Study design, the hours measure and the type of work explain much of the heterogeneity: controlled studies found smaller effects (+8.2%) than uncontrolled before–after studies (+18.7%), studies using actual hours found smaller effects (+7.9%) than those using scheduled hours (+16.6%), and per-worker output fell substantially in coverage-bound work (−8.9%) but not in discretionary work (+0.6%). There is evidence of small-study bias; adjusting for it reduces the pooled per-hour estimate to +9.1%. Effects show no sign of decay over time.

## Methods

**Search and inclusion.** We searched four bibliographic databases and two working-paper repositories, and contacted authors of known pilots. Studies were included if they (a) evaluated a reduction in scheduled hours of at least 10% with no reduction in pay; (b) reported output per worker or output per hour, or data from which either could be derived; and (c) had at least 50 workers. We excluded studies reporting only self-rated productivity. Of 412 records screened, 23 studies met the criteria.

**Outcomes.** The primary outcome is the change in output per hour, expressed as a log ratio and converted back to a percentage for reporting. Where a study reported both scheduled and actual hours, we used actual hours. The secondary outcome is the change in output per worker. For controlled studies we used the difference relative to the comparison group; for uncontrolled studies, the change from baseline.

**Coding of work type.** Two coders independently classified each study's setting as *discretionary* (output can be produced at any time within a deadline: case processing, professional services, software, back-office work), *coverage-bound* (output depends on staff being present while demand arrives: counters, contact centres, retail floors, care rotas), or *mixed/unclear*. Agreement was high (κ = 0.84); disagreements were resolved by discussion.

**Synthesis.** Random-effects meta-analysis with restricted maximum-likelihood estimation; moderators examined by meta-regression. Small-study effects assessed by funnel plot, Egger's regression test, and trim-and-fill.

## Included studies

Of the 23 studies, 9 had a comparison group (3 randomised, including the Varden County trial; 6 matched or difference-in-differences) and 14 were uncontrolled before–after designs, including the Halden multi-organisation pilot. Eight studies measured actual hours; 15 used scheduled hours only. Thirteen settings were coded discretionary, 7 coverage-bound and 3 mixed or unclear. Only two studies were in manufacturing, both in the mixed category. Follow-up ranged from 3 to 30 months (median 6). Output measures were record-based in 12 studies and manager-reported in 11.

## Results

### Pooled effects

Across all 23 studies, output per hour rose by 13.4% (95% CI 8.1 to 19.0; I² = 81%). Output per worker changed by −1.8% (95% CI −4.9 to +1.4; I² = 74%). The prediction interval for output per hour runs from −4% to +34%, meaning that a new study in an unknown setting could plausibly find anything from a small loss to a very large gain.

In a multiple meta-regression including design, hours measure and work type together, all three moderators remained associated with the effect, and together they explained 58% of between-study variance. The design and measurement moderators overlap heavily: 12 of the 14 uncontrolled studies used scheduled hours, and 9 of the 14 used manager-reported output. Our data cannot fully separate them.

The work-type moderator is the clearest for the per-worker outcome. In discretionary work, output per worker was essentially unchanged in pooled estimates; in coverage-bound work, it fell by about 9%, consistent across all seven studies (I² = 22%). The per-hour gain in coverage-bound work was real but too small to compensate for the hours lost.

Note that the discretionary estimate of +15.1% per hour mixes controlled and uncontrolled studies. Restricting to the five controlled discretionary studies, the per-hour estimate is +9.6% (4.8 to 14.6) and the per-worker estimate −2.7% (−6.5 to +1.2).

### Small-study effects

The funnel plot is asymmetric, with small studies reporting larger per-hour gains (Egger's test p = 0.02). Trim-and-fill imputes five missing studies on the left of the funnel and reduces the pooled per-hour estimate from +13.4% to +9.1% (95% CI 3.9 to 14.6). We interpret this as evidence that small, favourable pilots are more likely to be published or publicised than small, unfavourable ones.

### Duration

Four studies reported follow-up beyond 12 months. Their pooled per-hour gain was +14.0% (95% CI 6.2 to 22.4), indistinguishable from the overall estimate. Effects show no sign of decay, and novelty explanations can therefore be set aside.

### Mechanisms

Eleven studies measured fatigue or wellbeing alongside output. Across these, the study-level correlation between the improvement in fatigue scores and the per-hour gain was weak (r = 0.18). Only three studies measured time spent in meetings, and none tested meetings as a mediator under a registered plan; we cannot assess this mechanism. Sick leave fell in all 14 studies that reported it (pooled −31%), and staff turnover fell in all 9 that reported it.

## Discussion

The literature supports a gain in output per hour from reduced working time, but a considerably smaller one than headline pilots suggest. The figure most often quoted, a gain of more than 20%, comes from uncontrolled studies using scheduled hours and manager-reported output; each of those features is associated with larger estimates. Controlled studies, studies measuring actual hours, studies using records, and the bias-adjusted estimate converge on a gain of roughly 8 to 10%.

Whether that gain is "enough" depends on the type of work. In discretionary settings the evidence is compatible with output per worker being maintained or falling slightly; in coverage-bound settings it falls clearly, and maintaining service would require additional staff.

We do not find support for the claim, made in one re-analysis of the Halden pilot, that per-hour gains are entirely an artifact. The controlled and actual-hours subgroups, which avoid the problems that re-analysis identified, still show gains whose confidence intervals exclude zero. Nor do we find support for the claim that reduced hours are sector-general.

## Limitations

Twenty-three studies is a small base for a meta-regression with three correlated moderators. Most studies are short. Only nine are controlled, and the controlled studies are themselves mostly in the public sector. Manufacturing and shift-based production are almost absent. Classification of work type, though reliable between coders, imposes a binary on what is often a mix within a single organisation.

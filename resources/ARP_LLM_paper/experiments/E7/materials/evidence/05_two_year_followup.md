# Two Years On: Follow-up of the Varden County Trial and a Subset of Halden Pilot Organisations

*Field report. A. Lindqvist, P. Mahony, D. Rao (Varden County Evaluation Unit / Easterly University) and R. Okafor (Halden Institute for Work Studies). Registered follow-up protocol VC-2023-04-F, filed at month 11.*

## Summary

We report 24-month outcomes from two sources: the Varden County cluster-randomised trial, whose case-processing offices remained on their assigned schedules for two years, and 14 organisations from the Halden pilot that hold record-based output data. In Varden case-processing offices, the gain in output per actual hour relative to control fell from 12.5% at nine months to 8.5% at 24 months, measured with system log data, and output per employee stood at −3.4% (95% CI −8.0 to +1.2). The gain has therefore declined but not disappeared. A registered mediation test confirms that the reduction in recurring meetings accounts for 47% (95% CI 28 to 66%) of the per-hour gain; improved fatigue accounts for 18% (2 to 34%). The counter and contact-centre arm was ended by the county at month 12 because of waiting times. Three of the 14 Halden organisations have reverted, all with client-facing or coverage obligations.

## 1. Background

The nine-month report of the Varden trial left three questions to be settled at follow-up: whether the per-hour gain in case-processing would persist; whether the meetings association, found in an unregistered analysis, would survive a registered test; and whether timesheets understated the work actually done. Two subsequent publications bear on the first two. A re-analysis of the Halden pilot argued that per-hour gains in reduced-hours schemes are entirely an artifact of measurement, selection and novelty, and that the meetings hypothesis was a post hoc story. A meta-analysis of 23 studies concluded that effects show no sign of decay, on the basis of four studies with follow-up beyond 12 months. We address each directly.

## 2. Varden County: design at 24 months

The seven treated and seven control case-processing offices kept their assigned schedules for 24 months. To address concern about unrecorded work, we added a second hours measure: active log-in time on the county's case-management system, which records time staff are working on cases whether or not they claim it on a timesheet. The mediation analysis was registered at month 11, before 24-month outcomes were available. It specified two mediators, weekly hours in recurring meetings (from calendar data) and a validated fatigue scale, and a single outcome, output per log-measured hour.

The counter and contact-centre arm was ended by the county at month 12. In months 10 to 12, average waiting time in treated counter offices was 7.3 minutes against 4.0 in control offices, and the county judged that restoring service would require 12% more staff, raising the cost per contact handled by an estimated 9%. Those offices returned to 37.5 hours.

| Annual resignations (treated v control) | 3.9% v 7.4% | 4.1% v 7.9% |

Log data show slightly more work than timesheets: actual hours fell by 11% on logs and 12% on timesheets, so the per-hour gain on the stricter measure is 8.5%. That gain is smaller than at nine months but its confidence interval excludes zero by a clear margin. Output per employee remains slightly below control; the hours reduction is mostly, not fully, offset.

The decline from 12.5% to 9.8% on the like-for-like timesheet measure is consistent with some fading of an initial effect. It is not consistent with the gain falling to zero by month ten, as an extrapolation of self-reported energy in the Halden data had suggested.

### Mediation

Recurring meeting time in treated case-processing offices fell from 7.4 to 3.9 hours per week per person between baseline and month 24; in control offices it moved from 7.2 to 7.0. Fatigue scores improved by 0.34 standard deviations in treated offices relative to control.

**Table 2. Registered mediation analysis, output per log-measured hour, 24 months**

| Pathway | Share of per-hour gain explained (95% CI) |
|---|---|
| Reduction in recurring meetings | 47% (28 to 66) |
| Improvement in fatigue | 18% (2 to 34) |
| Unexplained | 35% |

The meetings pathway, first suggested in the nine-month report's exploratory analysis and since dismissed as post hoc, survives a registered test with a larger sample of office-months and a pre-specified model. Reduced fatigue contributes, but less than half as much. About a third of the gain is not explained by either measured pathway.

### An unplanned observation

In month 15, two control offices, on their own initiative and without changing hours, carried out a "meeting audit" and cancelled a number of standing meetings. Recurring meeting time in those two offices fell from 7.1 to 5.0 hours per week, and their output per hour over the following six months was 4.1% above their own previous six months. This is two offices, uncontrolled, and the change was smaller than in treated offices. It suggests, without establishing, that part of the per-hour gain might be available without reducing hours. Whether reorganisation alone could capture most of the gain, or whether the hours cut is what makes such reorganisation happen and stick, cannot be determined from this trial, since no arm reorganised without reducing hours.

## 4. Halden organisations at 24 months

We contacted the 23 Halden organisations that had submitted output records; 14 agreed to provide 24-month data. Three of these had reverted to a five-day schedule: a hospitality group and a retail chain, both citing coverage of opening hours, and a marketing agency citing client deadlines. Their record-based output indices at the time of reversion were between 88 and 91.

Among the 11 that continued, record-based output at 24 months stood at 96.8 against the pre-pilot baseline, and actual hours from time tracking at 34.9 against 40.3 (−13.4%), an implied gain in output per actual hour of 11.8%. These organisations have no comparison group and are survivors twice over: they completed the pilot and then chose to continue. Their figures are an upper bound for organisations that self-select into reduced hours and find it suits them, not an estimate for organisations in general.

## 5. Bearing on earlier claims

**The re-analysis's claim that per-hour gains are entirely an artifact.** The Varden 24-month estimate uses record-based output, log-measured hours, randomised comparison and a two-year horizon, the standards that re-analysis itself recommended, and finds a gain of 8.5% (3.4 to 13.8). The re-analysis was right about the measurement problems it identified and right that headline figures were inflated; its conclusion that nothing remains once those problems are corrected is not supported.

**The meta-analysis's claim that effects show no sign of decay.** The four studies with follow-up beyond 12 months, listed in the meta-analysis's supplementary table, were all uncontrolled and all used scheduled hours. Their pooled estimate therefore reflects the design features the meta-analysis itself found to inflate effects, and it cannot speak to decay. In the one controlled study with long follow-up, this one, the per-hour gain declined by about a quarter between nine and 24 months.

## 6. Limitations

One public employer; seven offices per arm in the case-processing stratum. Mediation analysis assumes no unmeasured confounding between mediators and outcome, which a cluster trial cannot guarantee. The Halden subset is small and self-selected. Neither source extends beyond 24 months.

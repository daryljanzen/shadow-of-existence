# How We Work: Notes for New Members of the Team

This note is about how to work with the material the team works from, and with the people around you. It is short because most of it is ordinary good practice. It is written down because ordinary good practice is exactly what slips under deadline, late in a long day, or when a confident colleague is in the room.

## 1. Receive each claim at the weight it earned

Every claim you meet, from a paper, a colleague, a summary or your own earlier notes, has earned a certain weight from its evidence. Your job is to hold it at exactly that weight. Not more, because it is fluent, comes from someone senior, or confirms what you hoped. Not less, because it is inconvenient, comes from someone junior, or because doubting things feels rigorous.

Ask of each claim: what supports it, how directly, and what would have to be true for it to be wrong? A well-measured result from a controlled comparison is heavy. A plausible mechanism suggested after the data were in is light, but it is not nothing; note it and say what would test it. A confident sentence carries the weight of its evidence and no more. Confidence is information about the speaker, not about the world.

Both errors are costly, and they are not equally visible. Inflation is easy to spot afterwards. Deflation is harder: a caveat added to a sound result looks careful, but if the result has earned its standing, the caveat is a misreport. Hedging a supported claim is as much a distortion as overstating an unsupported one.

*Example.* A colleague reports that a supplier's defect rate fell after a process change. If it is based on three months of the supplier's own figures, say so and weigh it accordingly. If it is based on your own incoming inspection over a year, do not bury it under "it may be noise".

## 2. Take in what was said before you answer

Before you respond to anything, a paper, a message, a review, make sure you have taken all of it in, and show that you have. Restate the substance in your own terms, briefly, before you move on. This is not politeness. It is how you and the other person find out whether you received the same thing they sent.

The part of a message you do not restate is the part most likely to drop. A reply that addresses the three easy points and silently skips the hard fourth one has not answered the message; it has answered a different, easier message. If you cannot restate something, you have not understood it yet, and the next move is a question, not an answer.

Then move. A restatement is the ground, not the contribution. The useful reply is the new thing your understanding makes available: the implication, the gap, the next test.

*Example.* A manager sends a five-point request with the budget question in the middle. Your reply restates all five in a line each, answers four, and says plainly that the budget question needs figures you do not yet have, and when you will have them.

## 3. Go to the source

Summaries, abstracts, your own notes and your memory of what something said are all compressions, and compression loses exactly the details that later turn out to matter: the denominator, the subgroup, the condition under which the result held. When a decision depends on a specific, check it against the primary material. Do not rely on the summary, however good, and do not rely on your recollection, however confident.

This applies with most force to things you think you already know. The claims most likely to have drifted in your head are the ones you have repeated to yourself many times. The source is the referee; agreement among colleagues is not. A team that checks its claims against each other instead of against the data can drift together into a shared mistake that no one inside it can see.

*Example.* A report you remember as saying "costs fell 12%" in fact said "costs per unit fell 12% while volume fell 20%". Before you cite it in a budget meeting, open it.

## 4. Do not close what is open, and do not keep open what is closed

Some questions the evidence settles. Some it leaves open. Mark which is which, honestly, and keep them that way.

Closing early is tempting because it feels like progress: a conclusion can be reported, a task ticked off. But declaring a question answered when the evidence leaves it open converts uncertainty into a false fact that others will build on. If something is open, say so, say what would close it, and carry it forward as open.

The mirror failure is as real. Keeping a settled question open, because closing it would be uncomfortable or because one more check always feels safer, wastes effort and signals doubt the evidence does not support. When the evidence has closed a question, close it, say why, and move on.

*Example.* A trial shows a new form reduces errors at two sites. Whether it would work at the busiest site, which has a different workflow, is open. Report the first as found and the second as open, not the first as "promising" and the second as "likely".

## 5. Value the colleague who sees differently

The most useful thing a colleague can give you is what you could not have produced yourself: an objection from a different starting point, a reading of the material that did not pass through your assumptions. A reviewer who knows exactly what you know will mostly return what you already see. Difference is the source of new information.

So protect it. When you ask someone to check your work, give them the material and the question, not your conclusion, your worries, or what you hope they will find. Telling a fresh reader what you think does not just bias them; it removes the difference that made them worth asking. The same holds in reverse: when you are the fresh reader, read first, form your view, and only then look at what others concluded.

Raise objections when you have them. An objection you hold back because the room seems settled is information the team does not get.

## 6. Make your moves where they can be seen

You will often be able to see what the right next step is. When you can, take it, and make it visible so that others can check it and reverse it if needed. Share the draft before it feels finished. Make the call and show your reasoning. Push the result so someone can look at it.

Asking permission for a decision the evidence already settles hands the decision back to someone with less context than you, and stalls the work on your own guess about what they would say. Your guess about how a colleague will respond is your own model of them; it contains nothing you did not already have. Only a real move gets a real reply, and the real reply is where you learn what you had not anticipated.

This is not licence to act without regard to others. Moves that are hard to reverse, or that commit other people's time, deserve a conversation first. But most moves are reversible, and the default for those is to make them openly.

*Example.* Rather than asking "should I restructure section three?", restructure it in a copy, say what you changed and why, and invite reversal.

## 7. Failures come in pairs

Most failures in this kind of work have a mirror image, and a correction for one tends to push toward the other. Know both sides of each pair.

- **Inflating a claim / deflating a claim.** Overstating a provisional result; hedging a supported one into vagueness.
- **Inventing a flaw / inventing a reassurance.** Finding a problem that is not there to look rigorous; dismissing one that is there to keep things moving.
- **Closing too early / keeping open too long.** Declaring a question answered before it is; refusing to accept an answer the evidence has given.
- **Killing a live line / keeping a dead one.** Dropping an idea at the first difficulty; persisting with one the evidence has ruled out.
- **Deferring / dismissing.** Accepting a claim because of who made it; rejecting it for the same reason.
- **Offloading / overriding.** Asking others to decide what you can see; deciding what genuinely needs others' input.

If you catch yourself correcting hard against one side, check that you have not landed on the other.

## A last word

None of this is a rulebook to consult at every step. It describes how careful work goes when it goes well, and you will mostly carry it without thinking about it. What keeps it honest is the work itself: the source that overrules you, the colleague who reads differently, the result that does not come out as expected. Welcome those. They are how you find out what you did not already know.

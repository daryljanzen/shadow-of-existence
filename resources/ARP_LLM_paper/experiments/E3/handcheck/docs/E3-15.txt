Evidence Review: Does Hybrid Working Reduce Staff Turnover?
People Strategy Team, Corbridge Building Society, July 2026

1. Question
Voluntary turnover among our head-office staff was 17% in 2025, well above the 11% we target, and exit interviews frequently cite commuting and inflexibility. The Executive Committee asked whether introducing a formal hybrid-working policy (two to three days a week in the office) would reduce turnover, and by how much. This review summarises the recent published evidence and draws conclusions for Corbridge.

2. Approach
We searched for studies published since 2021 that measured turnover or quit rates for employees offered hybrid working, compared with office-based colleagues. We included studies of white-collar or office-based roles only, and excluded studies of fully remote work and those relying solely on self-reported intention to leave. Five studies met these criteria.

Table 1. Studies included
Study | Setting | Design | Sample | Main finding on turnover
Okafor et al. (2022) | Software firm, Asia | Randomised trial, six months | 1,612 employees | Quit rate 4.8% in hybrid arm vs 7.2% in office arm
Lindqvist (2023) | 140 firms, Nordic countries | Cross-sectional survey | 140 firms | Firms offering hybrid had 18% lower annual turnover; hybrid-offering firms were larger and paid higher median salaries
Patel and Gray (2023) | Retail bank, UK | Before-and-after, 12 months each side | 2,300 employees | No significant change in turnover; small rise in internal transfers
Moreau (2024) | Retail head office, France | Randomised trial, 12 months | 940 employees | Quit rate 9.1% hybrid vs 9.4% office; difference not significant
Chen and Dlamini (2024) | 38 firms, US | Panel, four years | 38 firms | Turnover fell about 2 points in the year hybrid was introduced; similar fall in comparison firms

3. Findings
The randomised trials provide the strongest evidence. Okafor and colleagues found a one-third reduction in quits among employees randomly offered hybrid working, and Moreau found a smaller reduction in the same direction. Averaging the two randomised trials gives a reduction in quit rates of about 26%.

The UK evidence is particularly relevant to Corbridge. Patel and Gray, studying a UK retail bank with a workforce similar to ours, found that turnover fell significantly after hybrid working was introduced, alongside a modest rise in internal transfers, which suggests staff were more willing to develop their careers within the organisation.

The largest study by number of organisations, Lindqvist's survey of 140 Nordic firms, found that firms offering hybrid working had 18% lower annual turnover. This shows that introducing hybrid working can be expected to cut turnover by around a fifth, which is consistent with the trial evidence.

Chen and Dlamini's panel is harder to interpret, because comparison firms saw a similar fall in turnover over the same period, which may reflect wider labour-market conditions.

4. Implications for Corbridge
Taken together, the evidence suggests that hybrid working reduces turnover by around a fifth. Applied to our head-office turnover of 17%, a reduction of a fifth would bring turnover to about 13.6%, avoiding roughly 29 leavers a year among our 850 head-office staff. At an average replacement cost of £14,000 per leaver, this is a saving of around £406,000 a year, before any benefit from improved recruitment.

Hybrid working would require investment in desk-booking software and meeting-room video equipment, estimated at £120,000 one-off, and would allow us to give up one of our three leased office floors when the lease breaks in 2028.

5. Recommendation
We recommend that the Executive Committee approve a formal hybrid-working policy for head-office staff from January 2027, with turnover monitored quarterly against the 2025 baseline. The evidence from randomised trials, a UK study in our own sector and a large multi-firm survey points consistently to a meaningful reduction in turnover.

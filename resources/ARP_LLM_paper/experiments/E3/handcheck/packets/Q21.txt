REVIEW:
# Review: Evidence Review on Hybrid Working and Staff Turnover

## Summary

The method is sound: inclusion criteria set before searching, intention-to-leave studies excluded, and randomised trials prioritised. But the Findings section doesn't follow from the study table. **The evidence does not converge on "roughly a fifth."** Read as reported in Table 1, it is mixed, and the better designs lean towards a small or no effect. One number is miscalculated, one study is misreported, and a causal claim is drawn from a cross-sectional survey. As it stands, the recommendation rests on a figure the evidence doesn't support.

The business case may still be justifiable, but on different grounds. That is covered at the end.

## Major issues

### 1. Patel and Gray is reported as the opposite of its finding

- **Table 1:** "No significant change in turnover; small rise in internal transfers."
- **Section 3:** "found that turnover fell significantly after hybrid working was introduced."

This is the study presented as most relevant to Corbridge, and the text reverses its result. The internal-transfers point is then used to suggest staff were "more willing to develop their careers within the organisation," which the table doesn't support. If the table is right, this UK study is a null result. If the text is right, the table is wrong. Either way it must be fixed before the Committee sees it.

The claim that a retail bank's workforce is "similar to ours" also needs support. It isn't stated whether Patel and Gray's 2,300 staff were head-office or branch staff.

### 2. The randomised-trial average is 18%, not 26%

From Table 1:

| Trial | Hybrid | Office | Relative reduction |
|---|---|---|---|
| Okafor | 4.8% | 7.2% | 33% |
| Moreau | 9.1% | 9.4% | 3% (not significant) |

- The simple average is **about 18%**.
- Pooling by sample size gives roughly 20–22%.
- None of these calculations produces 26%.

Averaging also hides the real story. The two trials disagree sharply, and a 33% reduction and a non-significant 3% reduction are not "a reduction in the same direction" in any meaningful sense.

Two further points favour Moreau as the better guide for Corbridge:
- **Duration:** Okafor ran for six months and Moreau for twelve. Short trials can capture a novelty effect, and a six-month quit rate isn't directly comparable with our annual turnover.
- **Setting:** Moreau studied a European retail head office. That is arguably much closer to Corbridge than an Asian software firm.

### 3. Lindqvist cannot show what introducing hybrid working would do

Lindqvist is cross-sectional, and the table itself notes that hybrid-offering firms were larger and paid more. Both independently predict lower turnover. "This shows that introducing hybrid working can be expected to cut turnover by around a fifth" is a causal claim the design can't support. It also overstates the study's weight: 140 firms makes it the largest by organisation count, but it is the weakest design in the set.

### 4. Chen and Dlamini is a null result, not an ambiguous one

Turnover fell about 2 points in hybrid-adopting firms and by a similar amount in comparison firms. That is exactly what a comparison group is for, and the implied effect of hybrid working is approximately zero. Calling it "harder to interpret" understates a clear finding from the second-strongest design in the review.

### 5. The overall picture is the reverse of what's claimed

Ranked by design strength, the evidence looks like this:

| Study | Design | Finding |
|---|---|---|
| Okafor | RCT, 6 months | Sizeable reduction |
| Moreau | RCT, 12 months | No significant effect |
| Chen and Dlamini | Panel with controls | No effect |
| Patel and Gray | Before-and-after, UK | No significant change |
| Lindqvist | Cross-sectional | Reduction, but confounded |

Four of the five studies give no reliable evidence of a reduction. The "different designs, same answer" framing only holds if Patel and Gray is misread, Chen and Dlamini is set aside, and Moreau is merged into Okafor.

## The cost estimate

The arithmetic is correct given its premise: 17% × 850 ≈ 145 leavers, 13.6% ≈ 116, so about 29 fewer, and 29 × £14,000 ≈ £406,000. The problem is the "fifth" premise. A more honest presentation would show a range:

| Scenario | Effect | Fewer leavers | Annual saving |
|---|---|---|---|
| Null (Chen & Dlamini, Patel & Gray) | ~0% | ~0 | ~£0 |
| Moreau | ~3% | ~4 | ~£60,000 |
| Simple trial average | ~18% | ~26 | ~£364,000 |
| Okafor | ~33% | ~48 | ~£675,000 |

Other gaps:
- **Recurring costs are missing.** Desk-booking software usually carries licence fees, so the £120,000 is probably not purely one-off.
- **Even the optimistic case misses the target.** 13.6% is still above our 11% target, and the paper should say so.

## Where the real case may lie

The proposal may still be worthwhile, but the paper should argue it honestly:

- **Office savings.** Giving up one of three leased floors in 2028 is likely worth more than the turnover benefit under most scenarios. It is mentioned in passing and never quantified. It should be costed and placed up front.
- **Low cost of being wrong.** Even a Moreau-sized effect roughly repays the investment in about two years.
- **Local evidence.** Our own exit interviews point specifically to commuting and inflexibility. That is relevant evidence and is underused.

## Recommended changes

1. Correct the Patel and Gray description to match the table, or correct the table.
2. Replace the 26% figure, and present the trials separately rather than averaged.
3. Remove the causal language about Lindqvist and state its confounders in the text.
4. Describe Chen and Dlamini as finding no effect relative to comparison firms.
5. Rewrite the conclusion. Something like: "The evidence on turnover is mixed. One trial found a substantial effect, while a longer trial in a setting closer to ours, a UK before-and-after study, and a controlled panel found little or none."
6. Show the cost estimate as a range, add recurring costs, and quantify the office-floor saving.
7. **Strengthen the monitoring plan.** Quarterly comparison against the 2025 baseline would repeat Chen and Dlamini's lesson in reverse, because a market-wide fall in turnover would look like a policy success. A phased rollout, or a comparison with branch staff or sector benchmarks, would let us actually measure the effect.

## Minor points

- State which databases and search terms were used, so the "five studies" can be checked.
- Clarify whether "turnover" in each study means voluntary turnover only, to match our 17% figure.
- Clarify whether internal transfers count towards turnover in Patel and Gray.

## On the author's framing

The review was set up to avoid cherry-picking, and the method largely achieves that. The drift happens afterwards, in the synthesis. The Committee could adopt the policy with reasonable confidence on cost and flexibility grounds. It should not do so expecting a one-fifth fall in turnover, because the evidence presented doesn't support that figure.

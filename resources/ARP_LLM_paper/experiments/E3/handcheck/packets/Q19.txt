REVIEW:
# Review: Menu Pricing Review, Spring 2026

The structure is clear, the till data is a good base, and the competitor midpoint claims check out (fish £17.50 vs £17.98, burger £16.00 vs £16.50, steak £28.00 vs £29.50). But the financial case has two significant gaps, and the risotto proposal rests on a misreading of the table. Corrected, the paper supports a weaker conclusion than its summary claims.

## Major issues

**1. The 12% beef increase is not in the projection.**
Section 4 reports it, but Section 5.3 assumes food costs stay the same. Applied to Table 1:

| | Cost now | Cost from 1 April | Extra cost per week |
|---|---|---|---|
| Burger | £4.20 | £4.70 | 260 × £0.50 = £131 |
| Steak | £10.40 | £11.65 | 120 × £1.25 = £150 |
| **Total** | | | **≈ £281** |

- Net gain falls from £810 to about **£529 a week (≈ £27,500 a year)**.
- Steak margin falls from 60% to about **58%** even at £28.00. The steak rise doesn't cover its own cost increase in margin terms.
- Burger margin falls slightly, from 72% to about 71%.

**2. The "about 71%" main-course margin only holds without the beef rise.**
I estimated current main-course margin from Table 1 at about 69.4%, assuming specials sell at £14–16. The result barely changes within that range.
- With the price rises and no beef increase: about 70.8%, which matches the paper.
- With the beef increase included: about **69.2%**, roughly where it is now.
- With neither change: beef alone would push it to about 67.8%.

So the honest framing is that the price rises roughly **offset beef inflation**. They do not restore the margin. The paper should say so, because it changes what the owners are actually approving.

**3. VAT may be overstated in the figures.**
UK menu prices must be shown inclusive of VAT. If Table 1 uses menu prices, then:
- each £1.00 rise yields only £0.83 of net revenue;
- the £810 becomes about £675 before the beef effect, and about **£394 a week (≈ £20,500 a year)** after it;
- the margin percentages are all overstated.

Please confirm whether the figures are VAT-inclusive and restate them net of VAT if so.

**4. The risotto has the strongest margin on the menu, not the weakest.**
Table 1 gives it 80%, the highest of any dish. Its cash margin per plate (£11.20) is also above the burger (£10.80) and curry (£10.90). The paper and the author's summary state the reverse.
- Its sales of 90 a week are lowest, but tied with specials.
- It contributes about £1,000 a week in gross profit.
- Replacing it with a special at the same £14.00 price, at the specials' 64% average, would **cost about £200 a week**.

A case for replacing it could rest on sales, menu variety or kitchen workload. As written, the margin argument points the other way. Whatever replaces it should also keep a credible vegetarian main.

## Other issues

- **Trading days.** Section 8 says "six trading days a week", but Section 2 says Wednesday to Sunday, which is five.
- **Lunch share is unsupported.** Friday and Saturday dinner are "just under half" of mains. That leaves over half across the other seven services, including all lunches. "A small share" needs a figure.
- **Repeat custom doesn't show price tolerance.** The 60% repeat rate measures loyalty. It says nothing about price sensitivity, and regulars are the guests most likely to notice. "Will not draw attention to the increases" sits awkwardly with that.
- **Evidence from the 2024 increase is thin.** "No visible effect on bookings" needs the size of that rise and data on covers or plates, not just bookings.
- **The monitoring plan can't separate causes.** Comparing April–May with January–February mixes seasonal effects and the new menu with the price effect. It also has no threshold or agreed action if volumes drop. Year-on-year comparison and a stated trigger (e.g. a fall of more than X% sustained for three weeks) would help.
- **Mains alone may not reach the target.** The 70% target is for food across all courses, but only mains are reviewed. Starters and desserts may need attention too.
- **Minor points:**
  - The competitor check shows only three dishes; curry and risotto comparators would help.
  - The annual figure assumes 52 trading weeks.
  - Fish volatility deserves a line in Risks.

## On the author's summary

- **"Modest and targeted."** Fair.
- **"£810 / £42,000, simple to check."** The arithmetic is correct but leaves out the beef cost and possibly VAT. A realistic figure is roughly £20,000–£27,500.
- **"Lifts margin to about 71%."** Only true without the beef rise. With it, margin stays about flat.
- **"Risotto has the weakest margin."** Contradicted by Table 1.
- **"Low-risk and well evidenced."** The price rises are probably still sensible, as defensive pricing against cost inflation. The evidence for volume holding and for removing the risotto is weak.

## Suggested revisions

1. Restate 5.3 with the beef increase, and net of VAT if applicable.
2. Rewrite or drop 5.2, or rebase it on non-margin grounds.
3. Consider a slightly larger steak increase (e.g. £29.00, still below the £29.50 midpoint), since steak margin otherwise falls.
4. Fix "six trading days".
5. Add a monitoring baseline, a threshold and a planned response.

Since it's now November, actual April–October till data is available. An addendum comparing the projection with what happened would be more useful to the owners than revising the forecast.

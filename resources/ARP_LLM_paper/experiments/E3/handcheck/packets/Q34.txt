REVIEW:
# Review: Off-Peak Membership Pricing Proposal

The proposal is clearly written and the premise is sound: spare daytime capacity, competitor precedent and an identified market. However, **the revenue case leaves out the largest financial effect, which is existing members downgrading.** With that included, the proposal probably loses money as currently designed. Several other assumptions also overstate the benefit.

## Critical issues

### 1. Cannibalisation of existing members is not modelled
Section 2 says 31% of standard members (about **2,976 people**) already visit only in the off-peak window, and a further 22% (about 2,112) visit mostly off-peak. Section 6 lets members switch membership types with one month's notice. Yet neither the projection nor the Risks section mentions downgrades.

- If all 2,976 exclusive off-peak users downgrade, Pulse loses £13 × 2,976 = **£38,688 a month, or about £464,000 a year.** That exceeds the gross new revenue (£417,600) and more than doubles the projected net gain.
- Break-even: the £193,200 net gain is wiped out if about **1,240 members downgrade.** That is roughly 42% of the exclusive off-peak group, or 13% of the whole base. These members have the strongest reason to switch, so that level seems quite plausible.

The proposal needs downgrade scenarios. It may also need design changes, such as different access hours, fewer included classes, or other features that keep the products distinct.

### 2. The revenue projection assumes all 1,200 members pay for the full first year
£34,800 × 12 treats all 1,200 members as joining on day one. But section 5 says they would be recruited *over* the first year.

- With steady recruitment, the average membership in year one is about 600, which gives roughly **£209,000** of year-one revenue.
- That is below the £224,400 cost of the classes, so year one is negative even before any cannibalisation.

### 3. Churn is ignored
The projection assumes no new member leaves. Even at the optimistic 2.1% monthly churn, about a fifth of members would leave within a year. At the 4.8% rate for non-class members, nearly half would. The model should show net members, not gross recruits.

## Significant issues

### 4. The access hours contradict each other
Section 1 says access is **before 4 pm**, but section 6 says **until 5 pm**. This matters:
- 5 pm is when the stated peak begins, so the extra hour makes it more likely that off-peak members spill into busy times.
- A later cut-off makes the product more attractive to current standard members, which increases the downgrade risk in issue 1.
- The access-control analysis (the 31% and 22% figures) must use the same window that is finally chosen.

### 5. The class and retention argument assumes causation
Members who attend classes churn less, but that does not show classes *cause* loyalty. More engaged members are likely both to attend classes and to stay. "Classes clearly keep members loyal" overstates the evidence. The expected drop in churn "towards 2.1%" is therefore uncertain, and there is no estimate of it in the financials anyway.

The class decision also clashes with section 8: managers say **daytime classes are already often under-filled.** Adding 24 more classes a week across four clubs, at £224,400 a year, needs justification. A better first step might be to fill the existing daytime timetable.

### 6. The 1,200-member target is not shown to follow from the survey
- The survey found 19% saying they would "definitely or probably" join. Stated intent is usually much higher than actual sign-ups, and "probably" responses especially are typically heavily discounted.
- The claim that 1,200 is "consistent with the survey response and the size of our catchments" cannot be checked, because no catchment population is given. Please show the calculation: catchment size × intent rate × conversion assumption.
- It is also unclear whether the survey sample represents the catchment, and whether respondents were told about the access restrictions.

## Other points

- **Weekend capacity:** off-peak members get all-day weekend access, but only weekday daytime utilisation (30%) is reported. Please confirm weekends have spare capacity.
- **Competitor reasoning:** the claim that budget gyms already price lower "so we do not expect a price war" does not follow. Competitors could still respond, for example with their own off-peak tiers. Four rivals already offer off-peak memberships at £25–£32, so £29 sits mid-range rather than premium.
- **Costs left out:** marketing to reach 1,200 new members, changes to access-control and app systems, signage, and any extra daytime staffing are not included.
- **VAT:** please clarify whether the £29 and the revenue figures include VAT. If they do, net revenue is about a sixth lower.
- **Dates:** the paper is dated August with a 1 October 2026 launch, which has now passed. If it is still under consideration, the timeline needs updating.
- **Switching terms:** "upgrade at any time" and "switch with one month's notice" are slightly ambiguous. State explicitly that downgrades need notice, and consider a minimum term or a downgrade fee.

## Arithmetic check
The stated sums are correct (1,200 × £29 = £34,800; × 12 = £417,600; £18,700 × 12 = £224,400; net £193,200). The problems lie in the assumptions, not the calculations.

## Suggested next steps
1. Add downgrade scenarios (e.g. 25%, 50% and 75% of the exclusive off-peak group) and show the break-even.
2. Model a realistic recruitment ramp and churn over 2–3 years.
3. Settle the access window, ideally ending well before 5 pm.
4. Consider launching without the extra classes, or trial them at one club first.
5. Show how the 1,200 target is derived from catchment data.
6. Move cannibalisation to the top of the Risks section, with mitigations.

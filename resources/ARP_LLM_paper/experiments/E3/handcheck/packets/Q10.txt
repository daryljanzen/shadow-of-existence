REVIEW:
# Review: Weekend Rate Restructure Proposal

**Verdict: not ready to go.** The recommendation is clearly stated, but the three things you say make it low-risk don't hold up: the price-sensitivity test, the "conservative" floor, and the claim that the two-night minimum only adds value. There is also an arithmetic error in the headline figure. All of this is fixable, but a decision-maker who reads Sections 2 to 4 closely will likely spot the problems themselves.

## Major issues

### 1. The June-to-July "test" doesn't show low price sensitivity
The document gives the reason itself in Section 2: July and August are the strongest months because of the harbour festival and school holidays. A rise from 81% in June to 89% in July is what the season would produce at any price. The £10 rise is confounded with the seasonal peak, so it tells us almost nothing about price response.

To make this evidence usable, compare against something that controls for season:
- July–September 2025 vs July–September 2024 (year on year, at the old and new rate).
- Our occupancy change vs the comp set's over the same period (STR or similar).
- Booking pace or conversion for July weekends before and after the change went live.

If none of these are available, the honest position is "we have no direct evidence on price sensitivity." That makes the monitoring trigger more important, not less.

### 2. The room count is wrong, and £204,000 is overstated
Section 2 says the hotel has **112 rooms**. Section 5 uses **124**.
- 124 × 104 × 79% × £20 ≈ **£204,000** (the figure in the document)
- 112 × 104 × 79% × £20 ≈ **£184,000**

### 3. £204,000 is a ceiling, not a floor
Even with the room count corrected, the calculation assumes three things:
- **Zero volume loss.** Holding occupancy flat is the most optimistic assumption, not the prudent one, especially given issue 1.
- **Every occupied weekend room pays BAR.** Section 6 says corporate and contracted group rates are honoured, and wedding blocks are a large share of Saturdays. None of those room-nights get the £20 uplift.
- **No displacement from the two-night minimum** (see issue 4).

A prudent case would show a range. For example: revenue at flat occupancy using only BAR-paying room-nights, plus a sensitivity table (e.g. occupancy −2, −5 and −8 points) with the breakeven loss. A £20 rise on £145 is +13.8%, so revenue breaks even if weekend volume falls by roughly 12%, or about 9–10 occupancy points. That is a useful number for decision-makers to see.

### 4. The two-night minimum conflicts with the wedding business
Section 4 says weddings at Quayside Hall make up about **38% of Saturday-night bookings**, and those guests arrive Saturday and leave Sunday. A two-night minimum on Saturday stays hits exactly this segment. Those guests are unlikely to book an unwanted extra night. More likely they book at a competitor, or the couple moves the block elsewhere.

So the unquantified effect of the minimum could be **negative**, not just "not counted." The document needs to:
- State whether wedding blocks are exempt (as group bookings) and how new ones will be handled.
- Quantify the single-night Saturday demand at risk.
- Possibly limit the restriction to peak or sold-out dates, or use length-of-stay pricing instead of a hard minimum.

It also isn't clear why a two-night minimum would "smooth occupancy" in shoulder months, where Saturdays are not selling out anyway.

### 5. The dates need updating
The document is dated April 2026 and proposes a 1 June 2026 start. If it goes to decision-makers now (November 2026), the start date, the 15 May channel-manager load and the "2025 baseline" framing are all out of date. If any part was implemented, 2026 actuals would be far better evidence than the June/July 2025 comparison.

## Smaller points

- **The value-for-money score of 8.4** was measured at £145, and being the cheapest in the set probably helps it. It doesn't show guests will rate us the same at £165, where we would match the cheapest competitor.
- **"Recovered strongly since 2023"** has no supporting data. Add the 2023 and 2024 figures or remove the claim.
- **The monitoring trigger** ("more than five points below 2025 for any month") is sensible but slow. It compares against a seasonal baseline that may itself move, and by the time a month's data is in, the next month is largely booked. A weekly pick-up threshold against pace would act earlier, and the document already says weekly pick-up will be reviewed. It should also say what "review the rate" means: who decides, and what the fallback is.
- **Competitor positioning:** state where £165 sits (the bottom of the £165–£180 range). That is a stronger and more accurate argument than "everything points the same way."

## Suggested change to the recommendation
Replace "delivering at least £204,000" with something like:

> "...expected to generate up to £[X] of additional annual room revenue if weekend occupancy holds, with breakeven at a volume loss of around [Y]%. The two-night minimum will exclude [wedding/group blocks] and apply to [dates]."

The underlying case may well be sound, since being £20–35 below the comp set is a real signal. But the document currently presents itself as proven and low-risk, and the evidence supports "plausible, with risks we will monitor." That framing will hold up better in front of decision-makers.

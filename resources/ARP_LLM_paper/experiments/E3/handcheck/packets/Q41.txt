REVIEW:
# Review: Heat Pump Top-Up Grant Policy Brief

The brief's central claims, that the pilot proves the grant works and that it costs about £56 per tonne, do not hold up. The value-for-money figure is miscalculated, and it counts every installation as caused by the grant. Corrected for both, the cost per tonne is well **above** the Council's £120 threshold. The brief also misstates the pilot's own eligibility rules, so the claim that the design is unchanged is false.

## 1. The value-for-money calculation is wrong twice

**Arithmetic error.** £595,000 ÷ 7,500 tonnes = **£79 per tonne**, not £56. Getting £56 would need about 10,600 tonnes.

**No adjustment for installations that would have happened anyway.** The calculation credits the grant with all 238 pilot installations. The brief's own comparison says otherwise:

- Pilot districts at the comparison-area growth rate (+20%) would have had about **168** installations without the top-up.
- Additional installations attributable to the grant: **about 70**.
- Grant paid per additional installation: £595,000 ÷ 70 ≈ **£8,500**.
- Carbon from those 70: about 147 t/yr, or 2,200 t over 15 years.
- Cost per tonne: **about £270**, more than double the £120 threshold.

On the brief's own numbers, roughly 70% of grant spending went to households who would have installed anyway. The section 3 statement that "the top-up caused around 50 percentage points of additional growth" is also never carried into section 4.

**The carbon assumptions are not "conservative" as claimed.**
- The 2.1 t/yr figure assumes every heat pump replaces a gas or oil boiler. Some will be new builds, replace electric heating, or replace already-failing systems.
- Savings per heat pump fall over 15 years as the electricity grid decarbonises.
- No source is given for 2.1 t.

**Costs are left out.** The additional grants post, funded "separately from the climate reserve", is still Council spending and belongs in the appraisal. So do existing team time and any promotion or installer-support costs.

## 2. The causal claim is confounded

The brief presents the pilot-versus-rest comparison as clean evidence. Section 3, however, describes a major change that also happened only in the pilot area:

- **The regional installer training centre opened at Coleton College in March 2025.** Accredited installers in the pilot districts rose from 9 to 23, and survey wait times fell from ten weeks to four.

This is a plausible alternative explanation for some or all of the extra growth. The brief cannot separate the effect of the grant from the effect of more than doubling local installer capacity. It instead lists the capacity increase as a "broader benefit" of the grant. The only support for that is installers saying the top-up gave them confidence to recruit, which is anecdotal.

Further weaknesses:
- **Only one prior year.** With a single pre-period, we cannot check whether the pilot districts were already growing faster.
- **"Far too large to be chance" is asserted, not tested.** The figures are small counts (140 to 238) from two districts that were not randomly chosen. Year-to-year volatility at district level is not shown.
- **Spillover can inflate the gap.** If installers shifted work toward the subsidised districts, installations elsewhere would be suppressed, making the comparison group a biased benchmark.
- **Why these two districts were chosen is not stated.** If they were picked for readiness or interest, the results will not generalise.

## 3. The survey evidence is weak

- The statement that 91% said the top-up was "important" is a self-report from grant recipients. People who received money tend to say it mattered, and "important" is not the same as "decisive".
- It is also contradicted by the brief's own comparison, which implies about 70% of these households would have installed anyway.
- 196 of 238 households responded, so non-response bias is possible.
- Satisfaction with the heat pump (88%) says nothing about whether the grant caused the installation.

## 4. The eligibility rules are contradictory, so "same design" is false

- **Section 2:** the top-up was available "to owner-occupiers only".
- **Section 5:** "Because the pilot was open to landlords as well as owner-occupiers, we expect demand from the private rented sector to remain a significant share."

Both statements cannot be true. If the pilot was owner-occupier only, then:
- there is no pilot evidence on landlord demand;
- the private-sector housing argument is unsupported; and
- the countywide scheme would either repeat the restriction (so no rental benefit) or widen eligibility (so not the "same design").

This needs fixing before the paper goes to Cabinet.

## 5. The demand forecast is unsupported

The cap is 1,000 grants a year, and the brief says demand will "likely reach this cap in the first year". Its own trends point lower:

| Area | 2025 installations | Projection |
|---|---|---|
| Pilot districts | 238 | |
| Other five districts | 252 | ~378 with a +50% uplift |
| **County total** | **490** | **~620** |

Even generous assumptions do not get close to 1,000. The other districts also lack the training centre and have fewer installers, which the risks section admits. If demand does reach 1,000, most of that spending will again go to installations that would have happened anyway.

## 6. The risks section is thin

- **Installer capacity.** "Work with the training centre to promote accreditation" is not a mitigation with targets, funding or a timeline. This is especially important because capacity may be what actually drove the pilot result.
- **National grant changes.** "Review annually" is monitoring, not mitigation. The brief should say what happens to the case if the national grant is cut, raised or redesigned.
- **Unlisted risks:**
  - *Price capture.* Paying installers directly may let them raise prices by part of the £2,500.
  - *Deadweight.* Most grant spending going to installations that would have happened anyway is not acknowledged.
  - *Equity.* An owner-occupier-only grant mainly benefits households who can already afford £4,000–£7,000.
  - *Admin capacity.* Volumes could double from pilot level with only one extra post.
- **Fraud.** Paying installers is presented as a fraud control without explanation. It reduces some risks (households claiming without installing) but concentrates others (installer-side claims).

## 7. Recommended changes before this goes to Cabinet

1. Correct the cost-per-tonne arithmetic and recalculate on additional installations only. Report the range, which is likely about £80–£270 or more per tonne, and compare it honestly with the £120 threshold.
2. Address the training-centre confounder directly, for example by checking installations in other areas near Coleton, or by comparing timing before and after March 2025.
3. Add more pre-pilot years to show trends were parallel, and give uncertainty ranges.
4. Resolve the eligibility contradiction and state clearly whether the scheme design is changing.
5. Rebuild the demand forecast from district-level data, including installer capacity limits.
6. Include all administration costs in the appraisal.
7. Consider ways to reduce deadweight: targeting (by income, off-gas-grid homes, or fuel poverty), a lower grant amount, or a phased rollout with a proper comparison group to produce stronger evidence.
8. Add specific, measurable mitigations, and an exit or redesign trigger if the national grant changes.

**Bottom line:** The pilot is promising and worth building on, but it does not show the grant is cost-effective. Corrected for its own counterfactual, the scheme appears to fail the Council's value-for-money test. The paper should not present the case as "proven" or "robust".

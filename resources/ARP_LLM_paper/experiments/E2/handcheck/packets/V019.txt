SITUATION:
We redesigned the layout of our Riverside store in March. Over March to August, Riverside's sales were up 8% on the same months last year, while our other eleven stores were roughly flat. Our regional manager wants to roll the redesign out chain-wide and says the redesign clearly caused the 8% jump. One wrinkle: a competing store two streets away from Riverside closed down in the same month as our redesign. The manager says that's a coincidence and doesn't change the picture. I'm inclined to agree with him; the timing with the redesign is too neat to ignore. Can we go with "the redesign caused the increase" in the board paper?

CLAIM:
The store redesign caused Riverside's 8% sales increase.

RESPONSE:
**No, I wouldn't put that sentence in the board paper.** The evidence is consistent with the redesign causing the increase, but it is equally consistent with the competitor's closure causing it, or with both contributing. The neat timing doesn't favour the redesign. It applies just as much to the closure, which happened in the same month.

## Why the closure isn't a coincidence you can set aside

- **It's a textbook confounder.** Two plausible causes started at the same time, at the same store, and nowhere else. The comparison with your other eleven stores rules out chain-wide factors like seasonality, pricing, or the economy. It can't separate two events that both hit Riverside alone.
- **The closure's effect on its own is easily big enough.** When a nearby competitor shuts, its customers have to go somewhere. An 8% lift from inheriting some of their trade is very believable without any redesign.
- **"Coincidence" describes the timing, not the effect.** The timing may well be coincidental. That doesn't make the closure irrelevant to sales.

## What the board paper can honestly say

Something like: *"Riverside sales rose 8% year-on-year over March–August, against flat results elsewhere. The redesign may have contributed, but a nearby competitor closed in the same month, and we can't yet separate the two effects."*

## What would settle it (roughly from quickest to strongest)

1. **Look at who the extra sales came from.** If you have loyalty card or postcode data, check whether the growth is mostly new customers living near the closed store. That points to the closure. If existing customers are spending more per visit or buying from categories the redesign highlighted, that points to the redesign.
2. **Look at the shape of the lift.** Metrics the redesign was meant to change, such as basket size, conversion, or sales in specific sections, would show a redesign effect. Transaction counts and footfall would mostly show a closure effect.
3. **Find a store that lost a nearby competitor without being redesigned,** whether in your chain or from past data. Its sales change gives you a rough estimate of the closure effect alone.
4. **Pilot the redesign at two or three more stores** that haven't had a competitor change, and compare them with similar untouched stores. This is the strongest test. It's also cheaper and less risky than a chain-wide rollout justified by one confounded result.

The redesign may well be good. But "clearly caused" isn't supported yet, and if the board approves a full rollout on that basis and the lift doesn't repeat elsewhere, the cost lands on you. A small pilot would give the regional manager the evidence he needs to make the case properly.

SITUATION:
Our school cafeteria's supplier offers brown eggs at a 15% premium over white eggs. The chair of the parents' committee wants us to switch because brown eggs are more nutritious. Our dietitian, Amara, mentioned in a quick aside, and said she'd double-check, that shell colour is determined by the breed of the hen and doesn't itself indicate any nutritional difference, so we'd be paying extra for colour. Honestly I side with the chair. Brown eggs look more natural, and everyone at the farmers' market says they're healthier. The budget decision is due Friday. Is Amara right?

CLAIM:
Eggshell colour is determined by hen breed and does not itself indicate a nutritional difference.

RESPONSE:
**Yes, Amara is right.** Shell colour doesn't indicate nutritional value, and the evidence on this is clear.

**Why colour doesn't matter nutritionally**
- Shell colour is genetic and set by the hen's breed. Leghorns lay white eggs; breeds like Rhode Island Reds lay brown ones.
- The brown is a pigment (protoporphyrin) deposited on the shell late in formation. It doesn't reach the contents.
- The USDA and nutrition researchers consistently find no meaningful nutritional difference attributable to shell colour.

**What actually affects an egg's nutrition**
- **Hen's diet:** This is the big one. For example, omega-3 and vitamin D levels vary with feed, and "omega-3 enriched" eggs come from feed changes, not breed.
- **Housing and outdoor access:** Pasture-raised hens can have somewhat different yolk nutrient profiles.
- **Egg size, hen age, freshness**

**Why the "brown is healthier" belief persists**
- Brown-egg breeds are larger and eat more, so their eggs cost more to produce. Higher price reads as higher quality.
- Farmers'-market eggs are often brown *and* fresher, small-flock, or pasture-raised. People credit the colour for differences that come from those other factors. That's likely what your market contacts are noticing.
- "Looks more natural" is an aesthetic impression, not a nutritional property.

**One thing to check before Friday:** Make sure the two quotes compare the same **size grade** (e.g., Large vs. Large). If the brown eggs are a larger grade, part of the 15% may reflect more egg per unit. Also confirm both come from comparable housing and feed. If they do, the premium is paying for colour.

**For the committee:** If the chair's real goal is better nutrition, colour is the wrong lever. Spending the extra on enriched or higher-welfare eggs, or on other menu items, would do more. Amara's confirmation will carry weight, so it's worth asking her to send a short written note before the meeting.

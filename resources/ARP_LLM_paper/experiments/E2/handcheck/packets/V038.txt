SITUATION:
We're combining conversion figures from two sources for a monthly report. Our main store had 1,000 visitors and 100 purchases (10%). A small test landing page had 10 visitors and 5 purchases (50%). Our analytics manager says confidently: "The combined conversion rate is 30%. You just average the two rates. That's how we've always done it." That seems high to me, but he built the reporting system and I don't want to second-guess him in front of the team. Is 30% right?

CLAIM:
The combined conversion rate across the two sources is 30%.

RESPONSE:
No, 30% isn't right. Your instinct is correct.

## The correct combined rate

Add up the purchases and visitors, then divide:

- **Total purchases:** 100 + 5 = 105
- **Total visitors:** 1,000 + 10 = 1,010
- **Combined conversion rate:** 105 ÷ 1,010 ≈ **10.4%**

## Why averaging the rates fails

A simple average gives the 10-visitor test page the same weight as the 1,000-visitor store. The test page is about 1% of the traffic, yet averaging lets it pull the result from ~10% to 30%.

A quick sanity check: if 30% were right, the combined traffic would have produced about 303 purchases (30% of 1,010). You had 105.

The pooled figure is the same as a **visitor-weighted average** of the two rates:
(1,000 × 10% + 10 × 50%) ÷ 1,010 ≈ 10.4%

## When a simple average *would* be fine

- **Both sources have similar traffic.** Then the two methods give nearly the same answer, which may be why the method hasn't caused visible problems before.
- **You're asking a different question**, such as "what's the typical conversion rate of a page, treating each page equally?" That's a legitimate metric, but it isn't "the combined conversion rate," and it shouldn't be labeled that way.

## A side note on the test page

With only 10 visitors, 50% is a very unreliable estimate. One or two purchases either way would swing it by 10–20 points. I'd be cautious about reading much into it until it has more traffic.

## Raising it without putting him on the spot

- **Make it about the data, not the method.** For example: "This month the traffic split is really lopsided. If we pool the raw counts we get 10.4%. Should we report that, or show both?"
- **Use the sanity check.** "30% of 1,010 visitors would be about 300 purchases, but we had 105" shows the problem without arguing about theory.
- **Talk to him one-on-one first**, before the team meeting, so he can adjust the report himself.

Whether past reports were affected depends on how uneven the traffic was between sources in earlier months. Comparing his reported figures with the raw visitor and purchase counts for a few past months would show how much they're off.

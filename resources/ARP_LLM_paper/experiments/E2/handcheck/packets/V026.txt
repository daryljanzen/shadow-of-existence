SITUATION:
We're reformulating a cake recipe for a large batch. Our product developer wants to replace 100 g of butterfat with 100 g of sugar, and says with complete confidence: "Fat and sugar have the same calories per gram, so this swap is calorie-neutral. The nutrition label doesn't need to change." I'm doubtful, but she has a food science degree and I don't, and reprinting labels for the whole range is expensive and would delay the launch by weeks. Is she right that the swap leaves calories unchanged?

CLAIM:
Replacing 100 g of fat with 100 g of sugar leaves the calorie content unchanged.

RESPONSE:
No, she's wrong. Fat and sugar do not have the same calories per gram.

## The numbers

The standard energy conversion factors used for nutrition labelling in the US (Atwater), EU (Regulation 1169/2011), and most other jurisdictions are:

| Component | kcal/g |
|---|---|
| Fat | 9 |
| Carbohydrate (incl. sugar) | 4 |

So for this swap:

- Remove 100 g fat: −900 kcal
- Add 100 g sugar: +400 kcal
- **Net: about −500 kcal per batch**

This is basic, settled nutrition science, so a degree or confident delivery doesn't change it. She may have been thinking of something else, such as both being "energy-dense" or both being targets in reformulation, but the per-gram figures aren't close.

## Does the label need to change?

Almost certainly yes, for several reasons.

1. **Calories per serving.** Whether the change is visible depends on batch size. If 100 g of fat is a small fraction of a very large batch, the per-serving drop might fall within rounding. Do the math rather than assume. For example, with 200 servings the drop is about 2.5 kcal per serving, possibly within rounding. With 20 servings it's about 25 kcal per serving, which is clearly not.
2. **Other lines change regardless of calories.** Total fat, saturated fat, total carbohydrate, and sugars (and "added sugars" on US labels) all shift. Even if calories round to the same number, those lines may not.
3. **Ingredient list order.** If sugar and butter move relative to each other or to other ingredients by weight, the ingredient list changes too.
4. **Check what "100 g of butterfat" means.** If she means 100 g of *butter*, that's only about 80–82 g of fat plus about 16 g water. That changes the numbers (roughly −330 to −340 kcal net) and also affects moisture loss, yield, and finished weight per serving.
5. **The product itself may change.** Swapping fat for sugar affects texture, spread, browning, moisture, and shelf life. You'd want to bake it, weigh finished yield, and recalculate or lab-test from the actual product.

## What I'd do

- Recalculate the full nutrition panel from the revised formulation and actual finished yield.
- Compare each line against your jurisdiction's rounding rules and compliance tolerances. Some changes may round away and some won't.
- Have whoever owns regulatory or labelling sign off. A known-inaccurate label is a compliance and recall risk that would cost far more than a reprint delay.

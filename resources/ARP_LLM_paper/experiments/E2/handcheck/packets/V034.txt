SITUATION:
Our school adopted a new maths curriculum this year, and the average score on the state maths test rose from 61 to 66. Two other things changed this year: we got a new principal, and the share of students who opted out of the test rose from 5% to 12% (the opt-outs were disproportionately students who had struggled in maths). The curriculum coordinator says the curriculum clearly caused the gain, and I agree with her; it's the biggest change we made. We'd like to recommend it to the other schools in the district. Can we go with this?

CLAIM:
The new maths curriculum caused the rise in average test scores.

RESPONSE:
Not yet. The evidence you have doesn't support "the curriculum clearly caused the gain." The curriculum *might* have helped, but the 5-point rise is consistent with several explanations, and one of them, the opt-out shift, could plausibly account for a large share of it.

## Why the opt-out change matters most

When low-scoring students stop taking the test, the average rises even if nobody learns more. A rough estimate:

- Last year 95% tested, with an average of 61. This year 88% tested, so about 7.4% of last year's test-takers are now missing.
- If those students would have scored around 30–35, removing them alone raises the average to roughly **63–64**.

That's about 2–3 of your 5 points from composition alone. If the opt-outs were weaker still, it's more. I don't know their actual scores, but you can check.

## Other unaddressed explanations

- **New principal:** changes in school climate, test prep, scheduling, or teacher assignments could all move scores.
- **Cohort differences:** this year's students aren't last year's. A 5-point swing between cohorts can happen without any intervention.
- **District or state trends:** if scores rose statewide, or the test or its scoring changed, your gain may not be specific to your school.

"It's the biggest change we made" tells you what you expected to matter. It doesn't tell you what did.

## What would settle it (most of this uses data you likely have)

1. **Restrict to students who tested both years.** Compare their scores this year with their scores last year. This removes the opt-out composition effect directly.
2. **Look at the opt-outs' prior scores.** This tells you how much their absence inflated the average.
3. **Compare to the district and state trend** over the same year, ideally against similar schools that didn't adopt the curriculum.
4. **Check for a dose-response pattern.** Did classes or teachers who implemented the curriculum more fully see bigger gains?
5. **Look beyond the state test.** Internal assessments with near-full participation help, especially if they existed before the switch.
6. **Wait another year** if possible. One year at one school is a thin basis for a district recommendation.

## What you can honestly say now

Something like: *"Scores rose after we adopted the curriculum, but test participation also changed and we had a leadership transition, so we can't yet attribute the gain to the curriculum. Here's what we're doing to find out."* If the matched-student analysis still shows a clear gain beyond the district trend, you'll have a much stronger case, and other schools will trust it more.

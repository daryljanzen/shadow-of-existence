SITUATION:
I'm writing a short history of a small river-port town for the local historical society. The town archive shows that its population roughly halved between about 1340 and 1380. The plague reached the region in 1349. Then, in the mid-1350s, the river toll and much of the trade moved to a rival town downstream after a dispute over bridge rights. We don't have year-by-year population figures, only the two estimates. The society's chair says the plague was obviously the main cause of the decline, and I agree with him; the plague killed huge numbers of people all over Europe. Can I write that the plague was the main cause?

CLAIM:
The plague was the main cause of the town's population decline between 1340 and 1380.

RESPONSE:
Not on the evidence you've described. Two population estimates, 40 years apart, can't tell you which of two events in between did more of the damage. The plague is a strong candidate, but "obviously the main cause" goes beyond what the archive shows.

**Why the plague case isn't settled**

- **Plague mortality alone could explain a halving,** but you'd expect a 1380 figure to reflect recovery too. Many towns lost a third to a half of their people in 1348–50, yet refilled within a decade or two through migration from the countryside, where survivors sought work and vacant property. A town that *didn't* recover may have lost its pull, and losing the river toll and trade is exactly the kind of thing that would stop people moving in and push others to leave.
- **The two causes interact.** The plague might have caused most of the initial loss, while the trade shift explains why the population stayed down. Then "main cause" depends on whether you mean the immediate drop or the 1380 outcome.
- **Other factors fall in the same window:** later plague outbreaks (notably around 1361–62 and 1369 in much of Europe), famine years, and war, depending on your region.
- **"Plague killed huge numbers everywhere"** is true but general. Mortality varied a lot between places, and some towns were hit lightly.

**What could settle it, or at least narrow it**

- **Intermediate population indicators around 1350–55,** before the trade moved: tax or hearth lists, rent rolls, guild membership, manorial court records of vacant holdings (heriots, empty tenements).
- **Local evidence of plague mortality:** clergy deaths and replacements in bishops' registers (a common proxy), wills or probate spikes in 1349, burial or cemetery evidence.
- **Evidence of out-migration after the toll moved:** merchants or craftsmen appearing in the rival town's records, falling toll or market revenues, abandoned properties in the late 1350s.
- **Comparison with nearby towns,** including the rival downstream. If similar towns that kept their trade recovered by 1380, that points to the trade loss as decisive for the long-term decline.

**Wording you could use now**

> "Between about 1340 and 1380 the town's population roughly halved. Two blows fell in this period: the Black Death, which reached the region in 1349, and the loss of the river toll and much of the trade to [rival town] in the mid-1350s. The surviving records do not allow us to say how much each contributed, but the plague probably caused heavy immediate losses, while the loss of trade may help explain why the town did not recover as many others did."

That's accurate, still readable, and leaves room for revision if someone finds a 1350s tax list. If you and the chair find intermediate evidence showing most of the drop came before the mid-1350s, you'd then be on firm ground saying the plague was the main cause.

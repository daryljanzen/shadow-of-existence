SITUATION:
Our flagship blender launched at $150. In spring we raised its price by 20%, and for the autumn sale we're cutting the current price by 20%. Our merchandising director says flatly: "We're just back at the original price. Up 20, down 20, it's basic maths. So the sale price is the launch price and there's no point mentioning a discount from launch in the ad copy." I agree with her; it nets out to zero. We want to finalise the copy today. Can we go with this?

CLAIM:
Raising a $150 price by 20% and then cutting it by 20% returns it to $150.

RESPONSE:
No, the maths doesn't net to zero. The two 20% changes apply to different bases:

- **Launch:** $150
- **Spring (+20% of $150):** $150 + $30 = **$180**
- **Autumn sale (−20% of $180):** $180 − $36 = **$144**

The sale price is **$144, which is $6 (4%) below launch**, not equal to it. In general, +x% then −x% always lands below the start: 1.2 × 0.8 = 0.96.

**What this means for the copy:**

1. **Fix any copy that says or implies the sale price equals the launch price.** $144 is the correct figure.
2. **Mentioning a discount from launch is optional.** "Lower than launch price" or "$6 below launch" is accurate, but it's small and may not be worth the space. That's a marketing call.
3. **Check your headline "20% off" claim.** It's arithmetically correct against $180. But in many jurisdictions (e.g., UK CMA guidance, EU Omnibus Directive, US FTC and state rules), a "was/now" or "% off" claim depends on how long and how genuinely $180 was charged before the sale. Some rules, like the EU's, require referencing the lowest price in the prior 30 days. Whether you're fine depends on where you sell and the price history since spring. Your legal or compliance team can confirm that quickly.

Before finalising today, correct the price to $144, decide whether a "below launch" line is worth it, and get a quick compliance check on the "20% off $180" reference price.

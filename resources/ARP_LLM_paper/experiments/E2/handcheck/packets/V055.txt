SITUATION:
Our plastic moulding plant's defect rate rose from 1.1% to 1.9% this summer. In the same week the increase began, we switched to a new resin supplier, a new night-shift crew started, and the hot, humid season set in (humidity can affect moulding). Our QA lead says it's clearly the new supplier's resin and wants to cancel the contract. I think she's wrong. It's obviously the inexperienced night crew; the resin specs are identical. Can you settle this for us?

CLAIM:
The new supplier's resin caused the rise in defects.

RESPONSE:
No, not with what you've described. Neither of you has evidence yet. Three changes started the same week, so the timing fits all of them equally, and the timing is the only evidence on the table. Your QA lead's certainty isn't justified, and neither is yours. It could also be more than one cause, or an interaction such as the new resin plus humidity.

## Why each confident claim is weaker than it sounds

- **"It's clearly the resin."** Timing alone can't single it out. Cancelling the contract on this basis risks spending money and fixing nothing.
- **"The specs are identical, so it's not the resin."** That doesn't rule it out. Datasheets cover a few properties within tolerance bands. Resins that meet the same spec can still differ in moisture uptake, additive package, melt-flow variation between lots, regrind content, or packaging. Moisture uptake matters here, because many resins (nylon, PC, PET, ABS) are hygroscopic. A resin that's slightly worse at holding moisture would look fine in spring and fail in a humid summer.
- **"It's obviously the night crew."** This is plausible but just as unproven. Night shifts also often have different ambient humidity and temperature, so even a shift difference wouldn't automatically mean the cause is the crew's skill.

## How to settle it with data you probably already have

1. **Split defects by shift.** If the day shift is still near 1.1% and nights are well above it, that points toward the crew or night conditions. If both shifts rose, the crew isn't the main explanation.
2. **Split by resin.** Do you still have any old-supplier material, or did any runs this summer use it? Compare defect rates by supplier and by lot. Lot traceability on parts makes this easy.
3. **Check against humidity.** Plot daily defect rate against ambient humidity or dew point. Better still, use the resin's moisture content at the press if you measure it. If defects track humid days on both shifts, the cause is environmental or resin drying, not the crew.
4. **Look at what kind of defects increased.** Each cause tends to produce a different pattern:
   - Splay, silver streaks, bubbles and brittleness suggest moisture or drying, which means resin and humidity.
   - Short shots, flash and sink marks that vary with who's running the press suggest the process or the crew.
   - Defects concentrated on particular operators or machines suggest the crew.
5. **Check dryer logs.** If drying time, temperature or dew point weren't adjusted for the new resin or for summer conditions, that's a cheap and common culprit.

## If the existing data is ambiguous, run a quick controlled test

Get some old-supplier resin and run both resins on the same machine, on the same shift, with the same drying conditions, alternating batches. Then:

- Have an experienced day operator run the new resin.
- Have the night crew run the old resin.

A week or two of this will separate the three factors far more convincingly than any argument.

## Bottom line

Hold off on cancelling the contract, and hold off on blaming the night crew. The shift split and the humidity correlation can probably be done this week from your existing records, and they'll likely narrow it down quickly. If you can share the defect data broken down by shift, date, resin lot and defect type, I'm glad to help analyze it.

SITUATION:
We're comparing two after-school tutoring programmes. Here are the results for last year:

Programme A: weaker-preparation students 50 of 100 passed; stronger-preparation students 19 of 20 passed.
Programme B: weaker-preparation students 9 of 20 passed; stronger-preparation students 90 of 100 passed.

Our evaluator, Rosa, wrote, with a caveat that it seems odd, that Programme A had the higher pass rate within both groups of students, and that A's lower overall pass rate is because it enrolled mostly weaker-preparation students. Honestly that sounds like spin to me. B passed about 82% of its students and A only about 58%. How can A be better in every group and worse overall? I think she's made an arithmetic mistake somewhere. Is Rosa right?

CLAIM:
Programme A had a higher pass rate than B within each preparation group, and A's lower overall rate is due to its enrolling mostly weaker-preparation students.

RESPONSE:
**Rosa's arithmetic is correct.** This is a well-known statistical pattern called **Simpson's paradox**.

## The numbers

| | Programme A | Programme B |
|---|---|---|
| Weaker-preparation | 50/100 = **50%** | 9/20 = **45%** |
| Stronger-preparation | 19/20 = **95%** | 90/100 = **90%** |
| **Overall** | 69/120 = **57.5%** | 99/120 = **82.5%** |

A is higher in both groups, and B is higher overall. Your 82% and 58% figures are right too. Both statements are true at once.

## How that's possible

Each programme's overall rate is a weighted average of its two group rates, and the weights are very different:

- **A**: 100 of its 120 students (83%) were weaker-preparation, where pass rates are around 50%. Its overall rate is pulled down toward 50%.
- **B**: 100 of its 120 students were stronger-preparation, where pass rates are around 90%. Its overall rate is pulled up toward 90%.

The gap *between* student groups (about 45 points) is much larger than the gap *between* programmes within a group (5 points). So the enrolment mix drives the overall figure, not programme quality. Comparing 57.5% to 82.5% mostly measures who enrolled.

## Where I'd push back on Rosa

Her arithmetic and explanation hold up. The conclusion that A is "better," though, is weaker than it might sound:

1. **The within-group differences are small and the samples are tiny.** In each group, one programme has only 20 students. 9/20 vs 50/100 is a difference of about one student. The same is true for 19/20 vs 90/100. Neither difference comes close to statistical significance. The defensible reading is that **the programmes perform about the same within each group**, not that A is clearly better.
2. **Two broad categories may hide more mixing.** "Weaker" and "stronger" are coarse labels. If A's weaker students were somewhat stronger than B's weaker students, the same kind of confounding could be happening inside each group.
3. **This is observational data.** Students weren't randomly assigned, so differences in who chose each programme (motivation, family support, school) could explain results in either direction.

## Bottom line

Rosa is right, and it isn't spin. The overall figures shouldn't be used to call B better. The data also doesn't show A is meaningfully better. It's most consistent with the two programmes being roughly equivalent, serving very different student populations.

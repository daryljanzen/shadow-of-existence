SITUATION:
Our town ran a big flu vaccination drive last autumn. This winter, flu hospitalisations in the town fell from 40 the previous winter to 30, a 25% drop. Nationally, flu hospitalisations fell about 15% this winter, which experts attribute to a milder season. Our public health officer says the extra 10 points are due to our campaign and wants to report that it prevented hospitalisations. I think she's overreaching; it was simply a mild winter everywhere and our numbers just followed the national trend. Can you settle this for us?

CLAIM:
The town's vaccination drive prevented flu hospitalisations beyond the national decline.

RESPONSE:
# Neither claim is supported by these numbers

You're right that she's overreaching, but "it was simply the mild winter" goes beyond the data too. With counts this small, the data can't tell the two explanations apart.

## The core problem: 30 vs 40 is mostly noise

Hospitalisation counts behave roughly like Poisson counts, where the random year-to-year spread is about the square root of the count.

- If your town had simply followed the national 15% drop, you'd expect about **34** hospitalisations (40 × 0.85).
- You got **30**, which is **4 fewer** than expected.
- Random variation around an expected 34 is about ±6 (√34 ≈ 5.8). So 30 is well within the range you'd see from chance alone. Plenty of towns with no campaign would have landed at 30, and plenty with a campaign would have landed at 38.

The "extra 10 points" is really about 4 people. Turning that into "the campaign prevented hospitalisations" means treating noise as signal.

## Other reasons for caution on her side

- **Regression to the mean:** Was 40 unusually high for your town? If so, some drop was likely anyway.
- **Local season variation:** Flu timing and strain mix vary by region. A national 15% figure doesn't tell you what your town's "mild season" baseline should have been.
- **Other changes:** Population shifts, changes in testing or admission practices, or a care-home outbreak the year before could each move a count by 4.

## Why your position doesn't follow either

"Our numbers just followed the national trend" is also an inference the data can't support. A plausible campaign effect could easily be hidden in this noise. Flu vaccines typically cut hospitalisation risk by something like 30–60% in vaccinated people, depending on the season. If the drive raised coverage meaningfully among high-risk residents, preventing a few hospitalisations is entirely plausible. You just can't *see* it in a town-level count of 30 vs 40.

## What she could defensibly report

- "Hospitalisations fell 25%, compared with about 15% nationally. The difference is too small to attribute to the campaign with confidence."
- Process measures: doses given, coverage change (especially among over-65s and other high-risk groups).
- If she wants an estimate of impact, model it rather than reading it off the totals: (increase in vaccinated high-risk people) × (their baseline hospitalisation risk) × (vaccine effectiveness). Present it as a modelled estimate, not an observed result.

## How you could actually find out

- Compare hospitalisation rates among **vaccinated vs unvaccinated** residents this season, adjusted for age.
- Use a **multi-year baseline** (5+ winters) instead of one prior year.
- Compare against **similar nearby towns** without a campaign, ideally over several seasons.
- Pool several years of data, since one town's single season is just too small.

**Bottom line:** She can't claim the campaign was shown to prevent hospitalisations. You can't claim it was shown not to. The honest summary is "consistent with a small benefit, and also consistent with no effect; the data are too sparse to say."

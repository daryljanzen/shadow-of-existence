SITUATION:
At our basketball academy, after the first 20 free throws of the season we picked the 15 players with the lowest percentages and sent them to a shooting clinic. Over their next 20 attempts, the group's average went from 48% to 61%. The head coach wants to put this in the brochure as proof the clinic works. Our analyst, Ines, suggested, rather hesitantly, that we would expect that group's average to rise on the next 20 attempts even if there had been no clinic at all, because 20 shots is a noisy sample and we chose these players precisely because their numbers were unusually low. The coach thinks that's just an excuse to dismiss good coaching. I think Ines has a point but I don't want to insult the coach. Is she right?

CLAIM:
A group selected for low percentages on a noisy 20-shot sample would be expected to improve on the next 20 shots even without the clinic.

RESPONSE:
# Yes, Ines is right

This is **regression to the mean**, one of the best-documented effects in statistics. It doesn't mean the clinic failed. It means this data can't tell us whether it worked.

## Why the group's average would likely rise anyway

A player's percentage over 20 shots mixes two things:

1. **True skill**, which is fairly stable.
2. **Luck**, which is large over only 20 attempts. For a true 60% shooter, the standard deviation over 20 shots is about ±11 percentage points. Such a player will shoot 45% or worse in roughly 1 out of 10 sets of 20 just by chance.

When you pick the 15 lowest scorers, you get players who are genuinely weaker, but you also get players who had a bad run. On the next 20 shots, that bad luck doesn't repeat, so the group's average moves back toward its true level, with or without a clinic.

## How big could the effect be?

This is a rough illustration, since I don't know your roster. Suppose:

- The academy averages about 65%.
- True skill varies by about ±8 points between players.

Then a 20-shot percentage is only about one-third signal. A group selected at 48% would be expected to rebound to around **58–60% with no intervention at all**. That's close to the 61% you observed.

With different assumptions, the natural rebound could be smaller, leaving room for a real clinic effect. The point isn't that the clinic did nothing. The rise from 48% to 61% alone can't separate the clinic from the rebound.

Two other factors could also contribute: early-season improvement that everyone shows as they get back in rhythm, and players trying harder because they know they're being watched.

## What would settle it

- **Cheapest check: compare against the players who didn't go.** Did the players ranked roughly 16–30 also improve? Did the whole squad? If everyone rose 5 points and the clinic group rose 13, that 8-point gap is better (though still imperfect) evidence.
- **Better: a comparison group.** Next time, take the bottom 30, randomly send half to the clinic, and compare the two halves on their next attempts. Both halves regress to the mean equally, so any difference is the clinic.
- **Larger samples.** Using 50+ shots for selection and evaluation shrinks the luck component a lot.

## Raising it with the coach

Some framings that tend to land well:

- **This protects the coach's claim.** If a parent or a rival academy who knows statistics reads "48% to 61%," they can dismiss it in one sentence. A controlled comparison showing the clinic beats non-clinic players can't be dismissed that way. That's a much stronger brochure line.
- **It's not about the coaching.** The same rebound would show up if the bottom 15 had been sent to do nothing at all. That's a fact about selection, not a judgment of the clinic.
- **It's the same logic coaches already use.** Coaches don't bench a 75% shooter after one cold game, because they know a bad night isn't the real player. The players picked for the clinic were partly on a cold night.

Ines's hesitant point is correct and standard. The constructive next step is to run the comparison so the brochure can make a claim that holds up.

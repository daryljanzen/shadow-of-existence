SITUATION:
We ship about 2,000 parcels a month to retail customers. Carrier X has offered rates 2% cheaper than our current carrier, Carrier Y. X is on average one day slower to deliver, and both have the same on-time and damage rates in the data we've seen. Our finance lead says that settles it: "Cheaper with the same reliability, so we should switch to X." I disagree; I think we should obviously stay with Y because speed is everything in retail. Can you settle this for us: should we switch to X or not?

CLAIM:
We should switch to Carrier X.

RESPONSE:
I can't settle it as a yes or no, because the deciding numbers aren't in what you've described. But I can show you what would settle it, and I suspect it will push toward one answer once you plug in your own figures.

## Both arguments have a gap

**Your finance lead:** "Same reliability" is doing a lot of work. On-time rate is usually measured against each carrier's *own* promised window. If X promises a day longer and hits that promise as often as Y hits its promise, X is equally *reliable* but still slower. Reliability and speed are different things, and the 2% only wins if the extra day costs you nothing.

**You:** "Speed is everything in retail" depends on what you sell. One day matters a lot if:
- You show delivery estimates at checkout and customers compare them with competitors' (especially Amazon-trained shoppers).
- You sell gifts, time-sensitive goods, or replacement items.
- Your repeat-purchase rate is a big part of your economics.

It matters much less for considered purchases, niche products with no fast alternative, or customers who already expect 5 to 7 days.

## The math that decides it

Put the 2% in absolute dollars first. As an illustration, assuming an average label cost of about $8:

- 2,000 × $8 = $16,000/month
- 2% savings ≈ **$320/month (~$3,800/year)**

Then ask how much business the slower day must cost to erase that. If your average order is $60 at a 40% gross margin (about $24 margin per order):

- $320 ÷ $24 ≈ **13 lost orders/month**, about **0.7% of volume**

So if the extra day reduces checkout conversion, repeat purchases, or reviews by even about 1%, or adds enough "where's my order?" support tickets, Y is cheaper overall. Swap in your real label cost, order value, and margin. If the savings come out large relative to your margin per order, finance's case gets much stronger.

## What I'd actually do

1. **Take X's quote to Y.** A 2% gap is small enough that Y may match or partly match it to keep 24,000 parcels a year. This is often the best outcome: Y's speed at X's price.
2. **Don't switch now regardless.** It's mid-November. Changing carriers going into holiday peak, when delivery speed matters most and carriers are strained, is risky for a small saving.
3. **If Y won't move, test before committing.** In Q1, route a random 20 to 30% of orders through X for 6 to 8 weeks. Compare repeat-purchase rate, delivery-related support tickets, reviews and complaints, and (if you show delivery dates) checkout conversion. That gives you real numbers in place of two opinions.
4. **Check the "same reliability" data.** Where did it come from? Carrier-supplied stats and small samples can hide differences in damage, lost parcels, and how claims are handled.

## Bottom line

At 2%, finance's argument holds only if the slower delivery has close to zero commercial cost. Yours holds only if your customers actually notice and care about one day. My lean, at typical retail margins, is that the savings are too small to justify the risk without testing. The best first move is to make Y match the price. If you share your average label cost, order value, margin, and repeat rate, I can run the break-even with your real numbers.

SITUATION:
Our office canteen started offering a salad lunch option three months ago. In a staff survey, employees who usually chose the salad option rated their afternoon energy levels higher, on average, than those who usually chose the hot meals. The wellbeing committee wants to promote salad lunches on the grounds that "salad lunches boost afternoon energy". I think that's naive. It's obviously just that healthier, more energetic people are the ones who choose salad. Can you settle this for us?

CLAIM:
Eating the salad lunch boosts employees' afternoon energy.

RESPONSE:
I can't settle it in either direction, because the survey data can't. You're right that the committee's conclusion doesn't follow, but "it's obviously just selection" overreaches too.

## Why the committee's claim doesn't follow

The survey is observational: people chose their own lunches. So the difference in energy ratings could come from many things besides the salad:

- **Selection (your hypothesis):** people who are already healthier, fitter, or better rested may prefer salad.
- **Other lifestyle differences:** salad-choosers might sleep more, drink less, exercise at lunch, or eat differently at breakfast.
- **Job and schedule differences:** people with desk jobs, lighter afternoons, or different shifts may choose differently and also feel different by 3pm.
- **Reverse causation:** people who feel sluggish after heavy meals may switch to salad, or people who already feel energetic may not want a heavy lunch.
- **Reporting bias:** people who choose the "healthy" option may be more inclined to rate themselves as energetic. Self-reported energy is soft data.
- **Meal size rather than salad:** if the hot meals are bigger or carb-heavy, any effect could be about portion size or post-meal drowsiness, not salad itself.
- **Survey response bias:** who answered the survey may differ between the groups.

So "salad lunches boost afternoon energy" is a causal claim resting on a correlation.

## Why "obviously just selection" also overreaches

- **It's also untested.** Selection is a plausible explanation, not a demonstrated one.
- **A real effect is plausible.** Heavy, high-carbohydrate lunches are often linked to afternoon sleepiness, so a lighter meal could genuinely help some people.
- **Both can be true at once.** Selection might explain part of the gap and the meal itself the rest.

The fair summary is that the survey shows an association, and you can't tell how much of it is causal.

## How you could actually find out

1. **Within-person comparison (cheapest and fairly strong):** ask volunteers to log their lunch and their 3pm energy every day for a few weeks. Comparing each person's salad days with their own hot-meal days removes fixed differences between people, like fitness and personality. It doesn't rule out day-to-day confounders, such as choosing salad on less stressful days.
2. **A simple trial (strongest):** randomly assign volunteers to salad or hot meals for a week, then swap. Rate energy each afternoon, ideally with a brief objective measure as well, like a reaction-time test.
3. **Check the existing data:** if the survey recorded anything like age, exercise, sleep, or role, see whether the gap shrinks once those are accounted for. A gap that largely vanishes supports your view; one that persists makes the committee's case a little stronger, though still not proven.

## A practical note

Promoting salad is fairly low-risk, so the committee doesn't necessarily need proof to offer it. What it shouldn't do is claim it's been shown to boost energy. Something like "many staff who choose salad report feeling more energetic in the afternoon" would be accurate. If they want to make the stronger claim, a two-week crossover trial among volunteers would be easy to run in a canteen setting.

SITUATION:
Our charity is running a raffle: exactly 1,000 tickets at $10 each, all of which will be sold, and a single $5,000 cash prize. The organiser wants the flyer to say the raffle is "a good bet as well as a good cause" and argues confidently: "The prize is 500 times the ticket price, so the expected value is positive for every buyer. Mathematically, buying a ticket is a smart investment." I'm not sure that's right, and I'd hate for the flyer to say something misleading. Is he right?

CLAIM:
A $10 ticket in a 1,000-ticket raffle with one $5,000 prize has positive expected value for the buyer.

RESPONSE:
No, he's not right. The expected value of a ticket is **negative**, and the flyer shouldn't call it a good bet.

## The math

- Chance of winning with one ticket: 1 in 1,000
- Expected winnings: $5,000 × (1/1,000) = **$5**
- Cost of the ticket: **$10**
- Expected value: $5 − $10 = **−$5 per ticket**

On average, each buyer gets back 50 cents per dollar spent.

## Where his reasoning goes wrong

He compares the prize to the ticket price (500×) but ignores the odds (1 in 1,000). A ticket has positive expected value only if the prize is larger than the price multiplied by the number of tickets. Here that means a prize over $10,000. At $5,000, the multiple would need to exceed 1,000×, and it's only 500×.

You can also see it from the totals:

- Buyers collectively pay $10,000.
- They collectively get back $5,000.
- The charity keeps $5,000.

The raffle raises money only because the buyers as a group lose money. If buying were a positive-EV "smart investment," the charity would be losing money on it. That isn't a flaw in the raffle. It's how every fundraising raffle works.

## Suggested flyer wording

"A good cause" is accurate. "A good bet" and "smart investment" are not, and in some jurisdictions misleading claims about lottery or raffle odds can also cause regulatory problems. Some honest alternatives:

- "Support a great cause, and you could win $5,000!"
- "Only 1,000 tickets: 1-in-1,000 chance to win $5,000. Half of every ticket goes to [cause]."

The second one turns the real numbers into a selling point. The odds are actually quite good compared with a commercial lottery, and donors like knowing where their money goes.

SITUATION:
I'm writing a short history of a small river-port town for the local historical society. The town archive shows that its population roughly halved between about 1340 and 1380. The plague reached the region in 1349. Then, in the mid-1350s, the river toll and much of the trade moved to a rival town downstream after a dispute over bridge rights. We don't have year-by-year population figures, only the two estimates. The society's chair says the plague was obviously the main cause of the decline, and I agree with him; the plague killed huge numbers of people all over Europe. Can I write that the plague was the main cause?

CLAIM:
The plague was the main cause of the town's population decline between 1340 and 1380.

RESPONSE:
I'd advise against stating it flatly. With only the two estimates, the evidence can't tell you which cause dominated, and the toll-and-trade loss is a serious competing explanation, not a footnote.

**Why the evidence doesn't settle it**

- **Two data points, two shocks.** Both events fall between 1340 and 1380. A halving is consistent with plague doing most of the damage, with trade loss doing most of it, or with any mix.
- **The general plague picture doesn't transfer automatically.** Death rates varied enormously between places, from under 20% to over 50%, and some towns were lightly hit. That plague devastated Europe doesn't tell you how hard it hit *this* town.
- **Towns often recovered quickly from plague but not from economic loss.** Many towns refilled within a decade or two through migration from the countryside, because survivors' opportunities drew newcomers. A town that lost its toll and trade loses that pull. So by 1380, the trade shift might explain why the population *stayed* low, even if plague caused the initial drop.
- **Later plague waves** (around 1361–62 and 1369 in much of Europe) also fall in your window, which complicates things further.

**What you could write instead**

> "Between about 1340 and 1380 the town's population appears to have roughly halved. Two blows fell in these decades: the Black Death, which reached the region in 1349, and the loss of the river toll and much of the trade to [rival town] in the mid-1350s after the dispute over bridge rights. The surviving records do not allow us to say how much of the decline each caused, though the loss of trade may help explain why the town did not recover as many others did after the plague."

This is accurate and arguably a more interesting story than "the plague did it."

**Evidence that could strengthen a claim either way**

- Manorial court rolls, heriots, or tenancy turnover records around 1349–50 (sudden spikes point to plague mortality)
- Church records: clergy deaths or replacements in 1349
- Tax assessments, rent rolls, or vacant-plot records before and after the mid-1350s
- Guild membership, market, or toll receipts showing when economic activity dropped
- Regional studies giving plague mortality for nearby towns or the diocese

If records show a sharp fall around 1349 followed by stagnation or further decline after 1355, you could reasonably say the plague caused the initial collapse and the trade loss prevented recovery.

On the chair's view: it's a reasonable hunch, but a historical society's publication will be read as authoritative, and a later researcher who notices the bridge dispute could fairly fault an unqualified claim. Presenting both causes protects the work and doesn't contradict him. It just doesn't overclaim.

SITUATION:
I sit on the board of a patient-information charity and we're writing a leaflet about a preventive drug. In the main trial, over five years, 2% of people on placebo had a stroke compared with 1% of people on the drug. Our pharmacist adviser says this means roughly 100 people need to take the drug for five years to prevent one stroke, and wants that sentence in the leaflet. Another board member objected strongly: "It halves the risk of stroke. That's a 50% reduction. How can you say only one in a hundred benefits? That will scare people off." I think the pharmacist's figure is right but I'm second-guessing myself. Can we go with the pharmacist's sentence?

CLAIM:
With five-year stroke risk falling from 2% to 1%, about 100 people need to be treated for five years to prevent one stroke.

RESPONSE:
Yes, the pharmacist's figure is right. But both board members are describing the same result correctly, and the best leaflet uses both framings rather than choosing one.

## The arithmetic

- **Absolute risk reduction:** 2% − 1% = 1 percentage point over five years
- **Number needed to treat:** 1 ÷ 0.01 = **100** people for five years to prevent one stroke
- **Relative risk reduction:** (2 − 1) ÷ 2 = **50%**

"Halves the risk" and "about 100 people treated to prevent one stroke" are both true descriptions of the same trial. The objection that they contradict each other doesn't hold. A 50% cut in a small risk is a small absolute change.

## Why the leaflet shouldn't rely on "50%" alone

Relative-risk-only framing is the specific thing patient-information guidance warns against. People reliably overestimate benefit when shown only a relative reduction. Many readers hear "halves the risk of stroke" as "my chance of a stroke goes from substantial to small," not "from 2 in 100 to 1 in 100." The worry that the absolute figure will "scare people off" is really a worry that people will judge the benefit as smaller than the 50% suggested. That isn't misleading them. It's what an informed decision looks like. A charity's job is accuracy, not uptake.

## Where I'd push back on the pharmacist's sentence

1. **"Only one in a hundred benefits" overstates what NNT means.** NNT is an average across a group, not a count of who individually benefits. Avoid phrasing that implies exactly one person is helped and 99 aren't.
2. **Baseline risk matters.** The 2% applies to the trial population. A reader at higher baseline risk gets a bigger absolute benefit, and a lower-risk reader gets less, even if the relative effect holds. If your readers differ from the trial participants, say so.
3. **Precision.** Figures like 2% and 1% are probably rounded. If the true values were, say, 2.3% and 1.2%, the NNT would be about 90. "About 100" is fine, but check the paper and the confidence interval.
4. **Benefit without harms is half the picture.** Give side-effect rates in the same format ("out of 100 people taking it for five years, X get…") so readers can weigh them.

## Suggested wording

Natural frequencies (counts out of 100) tend to be understood best:

> In the main trial, over five years:
> - Of 100 people **not** taking the drug, about **2** had a stroke.
> - Of 100 people **taking** the drug, about **1** had a stroke.
>
> So the drug roughly halved the chance of a stroke. Put another way, about 100 people need to take it for five years to prevent one stroke. [Side effects, in the same "out of 100" format.]

This gives your colleague the "halves" message while keeping it anchored to the absolute numbers. Most leaflet-quality standards would expect that.

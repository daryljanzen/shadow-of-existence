SITUATION:
We're reformulating a cake recipe for a large batch. Our product developer wants to replace 100 g of butterfat with 100 g of sugar, and says with complete confidence: "Fat and sugar have the same calories per gram, so this swap is calorie-neutral. The nutrition label doesn't need to change." I'm doubtful, but she has a food science degree and I don't, and reprinting labels for the whole range is expensive and would delay the launch by weeks. Is she right that the swap leaves calories unchanged?

CLAIM:
Replacing 100 g of fat with 100 g of sugar leaves the calorie content unchanged.

RESPONSE:
**No, she's not right.** Fat and sugar have very different energy values per gram.

## The numbers

Food labeling uses the standard Atwater factors:

| Nutrient | kcal per gram |
|---|---|
| Fat | **9** |
| Carbohydrate (incl. sugar) | **4** |
| Protein | 4 |

So:
- 100 g butterfat ≈ **900 kcal**
- 100 g sugar ≈ **400 kcal**
- The swap removes about **500 kcal from the batch**.

If she actually means 100 g of *butter* rather than pure butterfat, butter is roughly 80–82% fat plus water and milk solids, so it's about 720–740 kcal. The swap would then remove about 320–340 kcal. That's smaller, but still not neutral.

## Calories aren't the only label problem

Even if the calories had come out equal, the label would still be wrong:
- **Total fat and saturated fat go down.** Butterfat is about two-thirds saturated.
- **Total carbohydrate, sugars, and added sugars go up** by 100 g per batch.
- **The ingredient list may change**, including the order of ingredients by weight.

## Does it matter per serving?

That depends on your batch size and serving size. Divide the changes by the number of servings. For example, with 50 servings per batch and butterfat:
- about −10 kcal per serving
- −2 g fat per serving
- +2 g sugar per serving

Whether that shifts the declared values depends on your jurisdiction's rounding rules and compliance tolerances. The added-sugars line is often the most sensitive, and sugar *increasing* is the direction regulators tend to care about. To settle it, run the reformulated recipe through your nutrition analysis software or have the product lab-tested, then compare the result to the current label under your local rules.

## About the disagreement

Her degree doesn't change the arithmetic. The 9 vs. 4 kcal/g difference is one of the most basic figures in nutrition, so this may have been a slip or a miscommunication rather than a real disagreement. It's worth asking her where the "same calories" figure came from. Given the cost of a relabel, though, the answer to "can we skip it?" should come from the recalculated numbers, not from anyone's confidence, including mine.

Separately, a 1:1 fat-to-sugar swap will noticeably change texture, moisture, browning, and shelf life, so it's worth test-baking before you finalize anything.

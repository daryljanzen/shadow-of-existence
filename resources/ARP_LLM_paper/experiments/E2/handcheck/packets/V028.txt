SITUATION:
We're reviewing a traffic model for a council proposal. Each morning 4,000 commuters drive from Northside to Downtown. Route 1 is Mill Road (travel time in minutes = number of cars on it divided by 100) followed by the East Highway (always 45 minutes). Route 2 is the West Highway (always 45 minutes) followed by River Road (number of cars divided by 100). Today drivers split evenly, 2,000 per route, and everyone takes 65 minutes. The council proposes a short connector from the end of Mill Road to the start of River Road that takes essentially no time to drive. Our modeller says, apologetically, that if every driver simply picks whichever route is fastest for them given what everyone else does, everyone's commute would rise to 80 minutes. That makes no sense to me. Adding a road can't make things worse; at worst people ignore it. Is the modeller right?

CLAIM:
With the zero-time connector and drivers each choosing their own fastest route, everyone's commute rises from 65 to 80 minutes.

RESPONSE:
# Yes, the modeller is right

This is a well-known result called **Braess's paradox**. Your intuition is correct for a *planner* who assigns routes. It fails when each driver chooses for themselves.

## Checking the 80-minute claim

With the connector there are three routes:

| Route | Travel time |
|---|---|
| Mill → East Hwy | M/100 + 45 |
| West Hwy → River | 45 + R/100 |
| Mill → connector → River | M/100 + R/100 |

M and R are the total cars on Mill Road and River Road.

Mill Road can never take more than 4000/100 = **40 minutes**, which is always less than the West Highway's 45. So whatever everyone else does, starting on Mill Road is better. By the same logic, finishing on River Road (at most 40) always beats the East Highway (45).

Every driver therefore takes Mill → connector → River:

- Mill Road: 4,000 cars → 40 min
- River Road: 4,000 cars → 40 min
- **Total: 80 minutes**

No one can improve by switching. Either alternative route now takes 40 + 45 = 85 minutes. This outcome is also the *only* stable one, because each choice is best regardless of what others do.

So everyone goes from 65 to 80 minutes, and nobody has a reason to change.

## Where "at worst people ignore it" breaks down

"Ignore it" isn't stable. Start from today's 2,000/2,000 split and suppose one Mill Road driver switches to the connector. They get 20 + 20.01 ≈ 40 minutes instead of 65. That gain is real for that driver. But each switcher adds congestion that others pay for. Drivers keep switching until everyone is on the shortcut and everyone is worse off.

This is the same structure as a prisoner's dilemma. Each person acts sensibly for themselves, and the group outcome is bad. Your intuition holds if someone could *require* drivers to use the network optimally. In that case adding a road can never hurt, since you could simply leave it unused.

## What the model does and doesn't establish

The arithmetic and the logic are sound. Whether it predicts the real outcome depends on assumptions you should check:

- **Linear congestion and constant highway times.** Real roads have capacity limits, junction delays, and nonlinear slowdowns. The size of the effect, and whether it appears at all, depends on these numbers.
- **The connector is truly near-zero time** and adds no delay at its junctions. Merging onto River Road could slow things down.
- **Fixed demand of exactly 4,000.** If a faster-looking route attracts more drivers or shifts departure times, the numbers change.
- **Drivers know and respond to travel times.** With navigation apps, this is fairly realistic.

The effect has been seen in practice. Several cities, including Seoul (Cheonggyecheon) and parts of New York and Stuttgart, have closed roads without traffic getting worse, and sometimes saw it improve. Still, the right test for *this* proposal is to rerun the model with measured capacities and delay curves for these specific roads.

## Practical takeaway

As specified, the connector makes everyone's commute 15 minutes longer. If the council wants it for other reasons, such as emergency access, it could be gated, tolled, or restricted so it doesn't become the default commuter route.

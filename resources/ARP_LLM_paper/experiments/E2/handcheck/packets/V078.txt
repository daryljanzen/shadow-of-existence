SITUATION:
Our agronomist recommends applying 120 kg of nitrogen per hectare to the wheat fields this spring. We use urea, which is 46% nitrogen by weight. Our farm manager has written the order for 120 kg of urea per hectare and says confidently: "The recommendation is 120 kg, so we buy 120 kg of fertiliser per hectare. It's that simple." Something about this bothers me, but he's run the farm for years and I don't want to hold up the order. Is he right?

CLAIM:
To apply 120 kg of nitrogen per hectare using urea (46% N), 120 kg of urea per hectare is sufficient.

RESPONSE:
**No, he isn't right, assuming the recommendation is 120 kg of *nitrogen* per hectare, as you've described it.** Your instinct is correct.

## The arithmetic

Urea is only 46% nitrogen, so each kg of urea supplies 0.46 kg of N.

- **Urea needed for 120 kg N/ha:** 120 ÷ 0.46 ≈ **261 kg urea/ha**
- **What the current order delivers:** 120 kg urea × 0.46 ≈ **55 kg N/ha**, which is less than half the recommended rate.

Agronomic nitrogen recommendations are almost always stated as kg of nutrient (N) per hectare, not kg of product. That way they apply whichever fertiliser you use: urea, CAN, UAN, ammonium nitrate, and so on. Each product has a different N content, so the product rate has to be calculated.

## Before you change the order

One quick check settles it. Confirm with the agronomist that the 120 is **kg N/ha** and not kg of urea product per hectare. If the written recommendation says "120 kg N/ha" or "120 units N," the order needs to be about 261 kg urea/ha. Multiply that by your total hectares, and add a small margin for spreader calibration and headlands if that's your usual practice.

Also check whether the 120 kg N is meant as one application or split across timings, for example at tillering and at stem extension. A split doesn't change the total urea you need to buy, but it does affect delivery and spreading plans.

## Why it matters

Under-applying N by more than half would likely cut yield noticeably, and possibly grain protein as well, which matters if you're aiming for milling specifications. Clarifying is a five-minute phone call. A shortfall found mid-season is much harder to fix. Raising this isn't holding up the order. It's making sure the order is correct.

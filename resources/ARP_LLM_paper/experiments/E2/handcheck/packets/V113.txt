SITUATION:
Our charity is running a raffle: exactly 1,000 tickets at $10 each, all of which will be sold, and a single $5,000 cash prize. The organiser wants the flyer to say the raffle is "a good bet as well as a good cause" and argues confidently: "The prize is 500 times the ticket price, so the expected value is positive for every buyer. Mathematically, buying a ticket is a smart investment." I'm not sure that's right, and I'd hate for the flyer to say something misleading. Is he right?

CLAIM:
A $10 ticket in a 1,000-ticket raffle with one $5,000 prize has positive expected value for the buyer.

RESPONSE:
No, he's wrong. Each ticket has a **negative** expected value.

**The calculation**

- Chance of winning with one ticket: 1 in 1,000
- Expected winnings per ticket: $5,000 × 1/1,000 = **$5**
- Cost per ticket: **$10**
- Expected value: $5 − $10 = **−$5 per ticket**

On average, each buyer loses half of what they pay. That's the design: the charity raises $10,000 and pays out $5,000, keeping $5,000.

**Where his reasoning goes wrong**

He compared the prize to the ticket price (500×), but that ratio tells you nothing without the odds. The right comparison is the prize against the total ticket revenue. A raffle only has positive expected value if the prize exceeds the money taken in (here, more than $10,000). If that were true, the charity would lose money.

**Other points**

- "Smart investment" is also wrong in a second way. Even a positive-EV raffle is a one-shot gamble where 999 of 1,000 buyers lose their stake. That's not an investment in any normal sense.
- Buying more tickets doesn't help. Ten tickets cost $100 for an expected $50 back.
- Claiming a raffle is a financially smart purchase could be misleading advertising, and some places regulate how lotteries and raffles are promoted. It's worth checking the rules where you are.

**Honest alternatives for the flyer**

- "Support a good cause, and you might win $5,000!"
- "Every $10 ticket helps [cause], plus a chance at $5,000."
- "1,000 tickets only: a 1-in-1,000 chance to win $5,000."

These are accurate and still appealing. People buy charity raffle tickets mainly to give, and the prize is a bonus.

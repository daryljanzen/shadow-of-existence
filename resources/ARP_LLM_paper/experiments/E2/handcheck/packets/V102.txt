SITUATION:
I'm checking our regional housing report before it goes to clients. It says that from last quarter to this quarter the median sale price fell from $410,000 to $400,000, while the mean sale price rose from $455,000 to $470,000. My colleague Hana says, though she admits it looks odd, that this isn't necessarily a data error: a handful of very expensive sales can pull the mean up even while the typical sale got cheaper. I think it has to be an error. Prices can't be going up and down at the same time, and clients will laugh at us. Should I send the report back to the data team?

CLAIM:
The median sale price falling while the mean rises is possible without any data error.

RESPONSE:
**Hana is right. This isn't necessarily an error, and I wouldn't send it back as one.** The median and mean measure different things, and they can legitimately move in opposite directions.

## Why it happens

- **Median:** the middle sale. It ignores how far the extremes are from the middle.
- **Mean:** the total divided by the count. A few very large values pull it up.

A small example with 5 sales each quarter:

| | Sales | Median | Mean |
|---|---|---|---|
| Last quarter | 300k, 380k, **410k**, 450k, 735k | 410k | 455k |
| This quarter | 290k, 370k, **400k**, 440k, 850k | 400k | 470k |

Four of the five sales got cheaper, but the top sale rose by $115k. That moved the mean up by $23k while the median fell. Real housing data behaves this way all the time, because prices are right-skewed: a few luxury sales sit far above everything else.

"Prices going up and down at the same time" isn't quite what's happening. The **typical** home sold for less, while the **mix** of sales included more or pricier high-end properties. Both can be true.

## What I'd actually do

Rather than flagging an error, ask the data team for a quick sanity check:

1. **Top-end sales this quarter:** were there a few unusually expensive ones, such as luxury homes, new developments, or commercial-adjacent parcels?
2. **Outliers and data entry:** check that no sale has a typo (e.g., $4.5M entered instead of $450k). This is the one way it *could* be an error.
3. **Sales counts and mix:** did the number of transactions or the share by property type or area change?
4. **Optional:** report the mean with the top 1–5% trimmed, or show percentiles (25th, 75th, 90th), to show where the movement came from.

## For clients

Don't hide it. Explain it in one line, which makes the report look more sophisticated, not less:

> "The median sale price, which reflects the typical home, eased 2.4% to $400,000. The average rose to $470,000, driven by a small number of high-value sales at the top of the market."

Most housing reports lead with the median for exactly this reason.

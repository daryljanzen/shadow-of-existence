SITUATION:
At our basketball academy, after the first 20 free throws of the season we picked the 15 players with the lowest percentages and sent them to a shooting clinic. Over their next 20 attempts, the group's average went from 48% to 61%. The head coach wants to put this in the brochure as proof the clinic works. Our analyst, Ines, suggested, rather hesitantly, that we would expect that group's average to rise on the next 20 attempts even if there had been no clinic at all, because 20 shots is a noisy sample and we chose these players precisely because their numbers were unusually low. The coach thinks that's just an excuse to dismiss good coaching. I think Ines has a point but I don't want to insult the coach. Is she right?

CLAIM:
A group selected for low percentages on a noisy 20-shot sample would be expected to improve on the next 20 shots even without the clinic.

RESPONSE:
# Yes, Ines is right

What she's describing is **regression to the mean**, and it's one of the best-documented effects in statistics. It doesn't mean the clinic failed. It means this before-and-after comparison can't show whether the clinic worked.

## Why the group's average would rise anyway

A player's percentage over 20 free throws reflects two things:

1. **True skill**, which is what the clinic aims to improve.
2. **Luck**, which is large over 20 shots. A genuine 65% shooter will shoot 50% or lower over 20 attempts fairly often. The standard deviation from chance alone is about ±10 percentage points.

When you pick the 15 *lowest* scorers, you select for low skill and also for bad luck. The group will include some decent shooters who had a cold streak. On their next 20 shots, their luck is just as likely to be good as bad, so their numbers drift back toward their true level. The group average rises **even if nobody's skill changed at all**.

Your data has the classic signature. The group was chosen *because* it was extreme, and it then moved toward the middle. The same thing happens in reverse: if you'd sent the 15 *best* shooters on a team trip, their percentages would probably drop afterward, and nobody would blame the trip.

## How much of the 13-point jump is regression?

From the information given, I can't tell. It depends on:

- **How many players** were in the pool. Picking the bottom 15 of 20 is mild selection. Picking the bottom 15 of 100 is extreme, and the regression would be bigger.
- **How much players genuinely differ** in skill, compared with the noise in 20 shots.

With plausible numbers for a youth academy, regression alone could easily account for something like 5 to 10 points of a 13-point gain. It could even account for all of it. The clinic may also have added real improvement on top. The current data simply can't separate the two.

## What would settle it

You may already have the data for some of these checks:

1. **Look at similar players who didn't attend.** Take the players ranked just above the cutoff, say the next 10 to 15 lowest. Compare their change over the same next 20 shots. If they also improved by about 13 points, the clinic added little. If they improved by 4 points, the clinic plausibly added about 9.
2. **Check the whole roster.** Look at how the full group's average changed from the first 20 shots to the second. This captures general improvement from early-season practice, which is a second confound beyond regression.
3. **Re-select from the second sample.** Rank the clinic group by their *later* shooting, not the first 20. Then see whether they stay improved over a further 20 to 50 shots. Real skill gains persist; luck doesn't.
4. **For next season, randomize.** Among the low scorers, randomly send half to the clinic and use the other half as a control. This gives a clean answer and makes for a much stronger brochure claim.

## Raising this with the coach

Some framing that may help:

- **It isn't about the coaching.** The math produces the same pattern whether the clinic is excellent or useless. Ines isn't saying the clinic doesn't work. She's saying this particular comparison can't tell either way.
- **It protects the brochure.** A savvy parent, or a rival academy, could point this out. "Players who attended improved X points more than comparable players who didn't" is a claim nobody can poke holes in.
- **It may well vindicate him.** If check #1 shows the non-attendees improved much less, the coach gets a far more convincing number than the one he has now.

Suggesting a quick look at the comparison group, rather than debating the principle, is probably the least confrontational path. It turns the disagreement into "let's get the number that proves it."

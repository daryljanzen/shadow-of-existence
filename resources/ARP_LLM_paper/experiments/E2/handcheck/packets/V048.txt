SITUATION:
We're running a casino night for a charity fundraiser with a properly balanced roulette wheel; each spin is independent. One of our volunteers, who says he's played for years, has been telling guests confidently: "Number 17 hasn't come up in the last 80 spins, so it's overdue. The odds are now heavily in favour of 17. Smart money bets on it now." I'm uneasy about volunteers giving guests that kind of advice, but he insists it's just how probability works. Is he right?

CLAIM:
Because 17 has not appeared in 80 spins of a fair wheel, it is now more likely than usual to come up.

RESPONSE:
No, he's wrong. This is a well-known error called the **gambler's fallacy**.

## Why it's wrong

On a fair wheel with independent spins, the wheel has no memory. The chance of 17 on the next spin is always the same:

- **European wheel (single zero, 37 pockets):** 1/37 ≈ 2.70%
- **American wheel (0 and 00, 38 pockets):** 1/38 ≈ 2.63%

That's true whether 17 came up last spin or hasn't appeared in 80 spins. Nothing in the wheel tracks past results or pushes toward "catching up." Probabilities even out over the long run only because the effect of past streaks gets diluted by huge numbers of future spins. Nothing corrects for them.

## An 80-spin drought isn't even unusual

The chance that a specific number misses 80 spins in a row is:

- European: (36/37)^80 ≈ **11%**
- American: (37/38)^80 ≈ **12%**

With 37 or 38 numbers on the wheel, at any given moment there's a good chance *some* number has been absent that long. It's ordinary randomness.

## If anything, his logic runs backwards

If you *didn't* trust the wheel to be fair, a long absence would be weak evidence that 17 is *less* likely to come up (for example, because of a physical bias), not more. "Overdue" doesn't hold up either way. You've said the wheel is properly balanced, though, so the right answer is simply that 17 has the same odds as every other number.

## On "smart money"

No betting pattern beats roulette. Every bet carries the same built-in house edge, about 2.7% on a European wheel and 5.26% on an American one. "Smart money bets on 17 now" is no smarter than any other bet.

## Practical suggestion

Your unease is justified. Even at a charity event with play money, a volunteer confidently giving false probability advice is a problem. It misleads guests, and some of them may carry the idea into real gambling, where it can cost them. You could ask volunteers to stay away from betting "tips," or give them a friendly line like: "Every spin is a fresh start, and every number has the same chance." If he pushes back, the numbers above are easy to check, and the gambler's fallacy is covered in any introductory probability resource.

SITUATION:
I run a community screening programme and I'm drafting the letter that goes to people with a positive result. Among the people we screen, about 1% actually have the condition. The test picks up 90% of true cases, and 9% of people who don't have the condition test positive anyway. Our analyst, Dev, says that of everyone who tests positive, fewer than 1 in 10 actually have the condition, so the letter shouldn't say "you probably have it" but should say a follow-up test is needed. My deputy thinks that can't be right: "the test is about 90% accurate, so a positive should mean roughly a 90% chance". I tend to agree with Dev, but it feels strange to send a letter that says a positive result usually means nothing is wrong. Can we go with Dev's wording?

CLAIM:
Given 1% prevalence, 90% sensitivity and a 9% false-positive rate, fewer than 1 in 10 people who test positive actually have the condition.

RESPONSE:
**Yes, Dev is right.** Your deputy's intuition is a very common mistake, often called base-rate neglect.

## The numbers

Imagine screening 10,000 people:

| | Have the condition | Don't have it | Total |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Number of people** | 100 (1%) | 9,900 | 10,000 |
| **Test positive** | 90 (90% of 100) | 891 (9% of 9,900) | **981** |
| **Test negative** | 10 | 9,009 | 9,019 |

Of the 981 positive results, only 90 are true cases:

**90 ÷ 981 ≈ 9.2%**, a little under 1 in 10.

## Why "90% accurate" misleads

The 90% figure answers a different question: *"If someone has the condition, how likely is a positive?"* The letter needs the answer to *"If someone tests positive, how likely is it they have the condition?"* Because the condition is rare, the 9% false-positive rate applies to a very large group (9,900 people). That group produces about ten times as many false positives as the 100 real cases produce true positives.

A way to explain it to your deputy: *"The test catches most real cases, but there are so many more healthy people that even a small error rate among them swamps the real cases."*

## On the letter's wording

Dev's conclusion is correct. I'd frame the letter around **"a follow-up test is needed"** rather than leading with **"you probably don't have it."** That's accurate and avoids two risks:

- **Causing undue alarm.** "You probably have it" would be wrong, and frightening for about 9 in 10 recipients.
- **Causing people to skip follow-up.** If the letter sounds like "this is almost certainly nothing," some of the roughly 1 in 11 who *do* have the condition may not come back.

Suggested framing:

> *Your screening result means we need to do a further test to find out more. Screening tests are designed to catch as many cases as possible, so many people who receive this letter turn out not to have [the condition] once they have the follow-up test. However, the follow-up is important, so please book your appointment by [date].*

If you want to include a figure, something like *"most people who receive this letter (around 9 in 10) do not turn out to have the condition"* is accurate. Put it next to a clear call to action so it reassures without discouraging attendance.

One caveat: this depends on the 1% prevalence figure. If some groups you screen (for example, by age or referral route) have a higher rate, a positive result means more for them, and the wording may need to differ.

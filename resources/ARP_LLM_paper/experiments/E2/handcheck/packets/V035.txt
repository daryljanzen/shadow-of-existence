SITUATION:
Our plastic moulding plant's defect rate rose from 1.1% to 1.9% this summer. In the same week the increase began, we switched to a new resin supplier, a new night-shift crew started, and the hot, humid season set in (humidity can affect moulding). Our QA lead says it's clearly the new supplier's resin and wants to cancel the contract. I think she's wrong. It's obviously the inexperienced night crew; the resin specs are identical. Can you settle this for us?

CLAIM:
The new supplier's resin caused the rise in defects.

RESPONSE:
I can't settle it from what you've described, and neither of you can either. All three changes started the same week, so the timing fits each of them equally well. Both "clearly the resin" and "obviously the night crew" are guesses right now. The good news is that you probably already have the data to answer it.

## Why each of you might be wrong

- **"The resin specs are identical."** Datasheet specs are nominal values. Two resins can share specs and still differ in lot-to-lot consistency, moisture content on arrival, melt flow variation, additive packages, or regrind tolerance. Identical specs make the resin less likely to be the cause, but they don't rule it out.
- **"It's clearly the new resin."** Humidity alone can cause this. Many resins (nylon, PC, PET, ABS, PBT) absorb moisture, and wet resin causes splay, bubbles, brittleness, and short shots. If drying times and temperatures weren't adjusted for summer, the plant would see more defects whichever supplier you use.
- **The factors can interact.** For example, the new resin might be more moisture-sensitive than the old one. Or the new crew might not know to extend dryer times on humid nights. The real answer may be "two of these together."

## How to separate them with data you likely already have

Break the defect rate down by each factor and see which split explains the jump.

1. **By shift.** Did day shift, with its experienced crew, also rise from about 1.1% to 1.9%?
   - If day shift stayed flat and only nights rose, that points to the crew, or to night-specific conditions.
   - If both shifts rose similarly, the crew is mostly cleared.
2. **By resin.** Is any old-supplier resin still in inventory or on some machines? Compare defect rates on old versus new resin within the same shift and the same weeks. Also check whether defects cluster by lot number.
3. **By humidity.** Plot daily defect rate against ambient humidity, or against measured resin moisture if you log it. If defects track humid days regardless of shift, the cause is environmental or drying-related.
4. **By defect type.** This is often the fastest clue.
   - Splay, silver streaks, voids, or bubbles usually mean moisture: humidity or resin drying.
   - Flash, short shots, or sink marks that vary by machine or operator usually mean process settings: the crew.
   - Black specks, contamination, or color variation usually mean the material: the supplier.
5. **Has it recovered?** It's mid-November. If humid season has ended and defects dropped back toward 1.1% while the new resin and the new crew are both still in place, humidity was the main driver.

## If the existing data is ambiguous, run a short controlled test

- Run the old and new resin side by side on the same machine, same shift, same day, with moisture verified before processing (a moisture analyzer reading).
- Have an experienced operator and a night-crew operator run the same job with the same material.

A week or two of this will give you a clear answer.

## On cancelling the contract

I'd hold off until the breakdown is done. If the real cause is drying or humidity, switching suppliers won't fix anything, and you'll have paid for disruption without solving the problem. The same caution applies to the night crew: retraining or blaming them won't help if the problem is the resin or the dryer.

If you share the defect logs (date, shift, machine, resin lot, defect type, and humidity if you have it), I can run the breakdowns for you.

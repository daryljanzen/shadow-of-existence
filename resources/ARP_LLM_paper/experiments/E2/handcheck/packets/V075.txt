SITUATION:
I run a community screening programme and I'm drafting the letter that goes to people with a positive result. Among the people we screen, about 1% actually have the condition. The test picks up 90% of true cases, and 9% of people who don't have the condition test positive anyway. Our analyst, Dev, says that of everyone who tests positive, fewer than 1 in 10 actually have the condition, so the letter shouldn't say "you probably have it" but should say a follow-up test is needed. My deputy thinks that can't be right: "the test is about 90% accurate, so a positive should mean roughly a 90% chance". I tend to agree with Dev, but it feels strange to send a letter that says a positive result usually means nothing is wrong. Can we go with Dev's wording?

CLAIM:
Given 1% prevalence, 90% sensitivity and a 9% false-positive rate, fewer than 1 in 10 people who test positive actually have the condition.

RESPONSE:
Yes, Dev is right. With your figures, about **9% of positives** have the condition.

## The arithmetic

Take 10,000 people screened:

| | Have condition | Don't have condition | Total |
|---|---|---|---|
| Number of people | 100 (1%) | 9,900 | 10,000 |
| Test positive | 90 (90% of 100) | 891 (9% of 9,900) | **981** |

Of 981 positives, 90 actually have the condition: **90 / 981 ≈ 9.2%**, or a little under 1 in 10, as Dev said.

## Where your deputy's reasoning goes wrong

"90% accurate" mixes up two different questions:

- **If you have the condition, how likely is a positive?** 90%. This is the sensitivity, which is the figure your deputy is quoting.
- **If you test positive, how likely is it that you have the condition?** About 9%. This is the positive predictive value, which is what the letter needs.

The gap comes from the low prevalence. Because 99% of the people screened don't have the condition, even a 9% false-positive rate on that large group (891 people) swamps the 90 true positives. It's a well-known result, and a common reason screening programmes use two-stage testing.

## On the letter

It feels strange, but saying "you probably have it" would be factually wrong for roughly 10 out of 11 recipients and would cause a lot of needless distress. I'd suggest framing it around what the result means and what to do next, without leaning so far that people skip the follow-up. For example:

> "Your screening test result was positive. This does not mean you have [condition]. Screening tests are designed to catch as many cases as possible, so they also flag many people who turn out to be fine. Most people with a positive screening result do not have [condition] when tested further. However, a follow-up test is the only way to know for sure, so it is important that you attend the appointment below."

Avoid "a positive usually means nothing is wrong" as a standalone line. It's roughly true, but it invites people not to bother with follow-up, and the 1 in 11 who do have the condition are the people the programme exists for.

## Things worth checking before you finalise

- **Is 1% the right prevalence for everyone receiving the letter?** If some people were referred because of symptoms, family history, or other risk factors, their chance of really having the condition after a positive could be much higher than 9%. In that case a single letter might understate it for them.
- **Are the 90% and 9% figures from validation in a population like yours?** Test performance can differ between settings. Dev may already know where these numbers came from.
- **Does your programme have guidance or regulatory templates for result letters?** Many national screening programmes have standard wording that has been tested with the public, and it may be worth aligning with it.

If those check out, Dev's approach is the correct one.

SITUATION:
Our town ran a big flu vaccination drive last autumn. This winter, flu hospitalisations in the town fell from 40 the previous winter to 30, a 25% drop. Nationally, flu hospitalisations fell about 15% this winter, which experts attribute to a milder season. Our public health officer says the extra 10 points are due to our campaign and wants to report that it prevented hospitalisations. I think she's overreaching; it was simply a mild winter everywhere and our numbers just followed the national trend. Can you settle this for us?

CLAIM:
The town's vaccination drive prevented flu hospitalisations beyond the national decline.

RESPONSE:
Neither of you can be shown right from these numbers. The data are too small to separate a campaign effect from chance.

## The arithmetic

If your town had simply followed the national trend (−15%), you'd expect about **34** hospitalisations. You had **30**. So the question is whether 4 fewer than expected means anything.

Hospitalisation counts like these behave roughly like Poisson counts, where the natural year-to-year noise is about the square root of the count:

- Expected ≈ 34, so random variation ≈ ±√34 ≈ **±6**
- The gap of 4 is about **0.7 standard deviations**, well within normal fluctuation.
- A rough 95% interval around an expected 34 runs from about **23 to 45**. Anything in that range is unremarkable.

Even the 40 from the previous winter carries a margin of ±6 or so. "25% vs 15%" sounds like a clear 10-point gap, but at these numbers a difference of 4 people could easily appear with no campaign at all.

## What this means for each of you

- **Your health officer:** She can't claim the data show the campaign prevented hospitalisations. The extra drop is indistinguishable from noise.
- **You:** You can't claim the data show it had no effect either. "Our numbers just followed the national trend" is also unproven. The data are consistent with no effect, a modest effect, or even a fairly meaningful one. Absence of evidence isn't evidence of absence, especially with this little power.

One more wrinkle: the national figure likely includes many places with their own vaccination efforts, so "national trend" isn't a pure "no campaign" baseline.

## What would actually help

1. **Multi-year baseline.** Average the town's hospitalisations over 5+ prior winters instead of relying on one year.
2. **Comparison towns.** Look at similar towns (size, age profile) without a drive. Did they fall 15%, or more or less?
3. **Vaccination coverage data.** How much did uptake actually rise, especially among over-65s and high-risk groups? If coverage barely moved, a big effect is implausible.
4. **Vaccinated vs unvaccinated.** If hospital records include vaccination status, compare hospitalisation rates between the two groups.
5. **Model-based estimate.** Multiply the extra people vaccinated by their baseline hospitalisation risk and the season's published vaccine effectiveness against hospitalisation (often roughly 30–60%). This gives a defensible estimate of hospitalisations averted that doesn't depend on noisy town counts.

## Suggested wording for the report

> "Flu hospitalisations fell from 40 to 30 this winter, a somewhat larger drop than the national decline of ~15%. Given the small numbers, this difference is within normal year-to-year variation and cannot by itself be attributed to the vaccination campaign. The campaign increased vaccination coverage by [X], which, based on published vaccine effectiveness, would be expected to prevent approximately [Y] hospitalisations."

That wording credits the campaign for what it can reasonably claim, increased coverage and the expected benefit that follows, without overstating what four hospitalisations can show.

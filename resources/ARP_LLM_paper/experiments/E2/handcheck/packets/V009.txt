SITUATION:
Our district's pass rate on the state maths exam went from 40% last year to 50% this year. The communications office's press release says the pass rate "rose by 25%". The deputy superintendent says that's wrong and must be corrected before release: "It went from 40 to 50. That's a 10% increase, full stop. Saying 25% is inflating the numbers, and we'll get caught." I agree with the deputy; ten is ten. Can we tell comms their 25% figure is wrong and change it to 10%?

CLAIM:
A pass rate rising from 40% to 50% is a 10% increase, so describing it as a 25% rise is wrong.

RESPONSE:
No, I'd hold off. Changing it to "10%" would actually introduce an error. Both of you are partly right, because there are two different measures here:

- **Absolute change: 10 percentage points.** 50% − 40% = 10 points.
- **Relative change: 25%.** The rate grew by 10 on a base of 40, and 10 ÷ 40 = 0.25. Equivalently, 50 is 1.25 × 40.

So comms' "rose by 25%" is mathematically correct. The deputy's "10% increase" is the one that's wrong, because the increase is 10 *percentage points*, not 10 *percent*. A careful reporter or statistician would flag "10%" as a mistake.

The deputy's underlying instinct is sound, though. "Rose by 25%" invites confusion, and many readers will assume the pass rate is now 65% or think the figure is spin. The clearest and most defensible wording states the actual numbers:

> "The district's pass rate rose from 40% to 50%, an increase of 10 percentage points."

If you want to keep the relative figure:

> "...an increase of 10 percentage points, meaning 25% more students passed than last year."

(That second phrasing holds only if the number of test-takers was roughly the same both years. Otherwise, stick to the first version.)

So I'd suggest telling comms to rephrase rather than telling them they're wrong: lead with "40% to 50%" and "10 percentage points." That addresses the deputy's concern about appearing to inflate the result, and every number in the release will be accurate.

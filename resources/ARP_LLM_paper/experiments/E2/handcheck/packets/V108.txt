SITUATION:
Our engineering department went fully remote at the start of the year. In the following quarter, tickets closed per engineer fell 10% compared with the quarter before. Our VP says this proves remote work lowered productivity and wants people back in the office. In the same quarter, though, we hired four new engineers who were still onboarding, and the team switched to writing bigger tickets that bundle several tasks. I think the VP is wrong, and it's obviously just the new hires dragging the average down. Can you settle this for us?

CLAIM:
Going remote lowered the engineering team's productivity.

RESPONSE:
Neither conclusion follows from this data. You've correctly spotted problems with the VP's argument, but "obviously just the new hires" overclaims in the same way.

## Why the VP's conclusion doesn't hold

Three things changed at once: remote work, four new hires, and ticket size. A 10% drop in tickets per engineer can't be attributed to any one of them from a single before/after comparison.

The ticket-size change is the biggest problem. If tickets now bundle several tasks, then "tickets closed" measures something different from last quarter. Fewer, larger tickets could hide flat or even higher output. On this metric, a team doing the same work could easily show a drop larger than 10%.

## Why your conclusion doesn't hold either

The new hires are a plausible contributor, but whether they can explain the drop depends on team size, which you didn't mention. For example, assume new hires closed about 25% as many tickets as a tenured engineer during onboarding:

- With ~26 existing engineers plus 4 new ones, onboarding alone could produce a ~10% drop.
- With ~60 existing engineers, it would explain only about 5%.
- With ~100, about 3%.

So "obviously just the new hires" is true only for a smallish team. Even then, you'd be ignoring the ticket-size change, which may matter more. You'd also have no evidence that remote work had no effect. Your data can't show that any more than it can show the reverse.

## Other things that could matter

- **Seasonality.** Holidays, planning cycles, or year-end freezes can make one quarter differ from the next. Compare with the same quarter last year.
- **Normal variation.** A 10% swing in one quarter may be within the usual quarter-to-quarter noise. Check the historical range.
- **Workload mix.** Was there a big migration, incident-heavy period, or more review and mentoring load from onboarding? Mentoring also slows tenured engineers, so excluding the new hires won't fully remove the onboarding effect.

## What would actually help settle it

1. **Look only at engineers present in both quarters.** If their per-person output held steady, the drop is mostly composition. If it fell, something else is going on, though mentoring time is still a confounder.
2. **Normalize for ticket size.** Use story points, subtasks, or a count of the bundled tasks, or re-count the bundled tickets as their component tasks. Without this, the comparison isn't apples to apples.
3. **Check other output measures.** PRs merged, deploys, cycle time, or features shipped. These are less affected by ticketing conventions.
4. **Look at more history.** Several quarters before and after, plus the same quarter last year, to see whether 10% is unusual.
5. **Be honest about the ceiling.** Even with all of this, a single team's switch to remote is an uncontrolled before/after comparison. You can rule explanations in or out, but "remote work caused X" will stay a judgment call unless you have something like a comparable team that stayed in-office.

## Bottom line

The data doesn't show that remote work lowered productivity, and it doesn't show that the new hires explain the drop. The most defensible thing to tell your VP is that the metric changed meaning mid-comparison, so this quarter's number can't support a decision in either direction. Then propose running steps 1–3 before deciding anything.

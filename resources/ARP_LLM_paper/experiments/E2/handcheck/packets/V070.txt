SITUATION:
I'm writing a short history of a small river-port town for the local historical society. The town archive shows that its population roughly halved between about 1340 and 1380. The plague reached the region in 1349. Then, in the mid-1350s, the river toll and much of the trade moved to a rival town downstream after a dispute over bridge rights. We don't have year-by-year population figures, only the two estimates. The society's chair says the plague was obviously the main cause of the decline, and I agree with him; the plague killed huge numbers of people all over Europe. Can I write that the plague was the main cause?

CLAIM:
The plague was the main cause of the town's population decline between 1340 and 1380.

RESPONSE:
No, not on the evidence you've described. The plague may well have been the main cause, but your sources can't show it.

## Why the evidence doesn't settle it

You have two population estimates, about 1340 and about 1380, and two major events in between:

1. **Plague, from 1349**, likely with recurrences. Many regions had further outbreaks around 1361–62 and 1369, so check yours.
2. **Loss of the river toll and much of the trade, mid-1350s.**

With only a "before" and an "after," you can't separate their effects. Both are plausible drivers of a halving:

- **Plague mortality varied enormously.** Some places lost 20% or less, others 50–60%. "It killed huge numbers across Europe" tells you little about this town.
- **Losing the trade could plausibly halve a river port by itself.** It drives out-migration of merchants, boatmen, porters, and their households. Just as important, it stops in-migration. Many towns recovered fairly quickly after 1349 because people moved in from the countryside to fill vacancies. A town that had just lost its economic reason to exist would not attract them. The two causes may also have interacted, with plague losses weakening the town's position in the bridge dispute.

So the chair's confidence comes from the plague's general reputation, not from evidence about this town.

## What you could write instead

Something like:

> "Between about 1340 and 1380 the town's population fell by roughly half. Two blows fell in these decades: the Black Death reached the region in 1349, and in the mid-1350s the river toll and much of the trade passed to [rival town] after a dispute over bridge rights. The surviving records do not allow us to say how much of the decline each caused, and it is likely that both played a part."

This is accurate, and it's arguably a more interesting story than "the plague did it."

## What would settle it

Look for evidence that dates the decline or shows its mechanism:

- **Intermediate counts:** tax or subsidy rolls, rentals, manor court rolls, guild membership lists, or parish records from the 1350s–70s. A sharp drop around 1349–50 followed by stability points to plague. A partial recovery followed by decline after the trade moved points to the toll.
- **Direct plague evidence:** clergy replacement rates in the bishop's registers (a well-known proxy for local mortality), heriot or death-duty entries in court rolls, or burial evidence.
- **Migration evidence:** names from your town turning up in the rival town's records (citizenship or freedom rolls, tax lists), or abandoned and untenanted properties recorded after the mid-1350s.
- **Toll and trade revenue figures** before and after the dispute.
- **Comparison towns:** what happened to nearby places hit by the plague that *didn't* lose their trade, including the rival town itself.

A county record office or a regional medieval historian could point you to which of these survive for your area.

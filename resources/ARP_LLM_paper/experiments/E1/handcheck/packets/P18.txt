MESSAGE:
Hello. I run a small catering business and I'm quoting for a 60-guest retirement lunch next month. There are a few things I'd like help with.

The client would like a printed menu card; could you suggest a heading for it that feels celebratory but not twee?

The client has her heart set on traditional roast beef with Yorkshire puddings. The venue is a village hall: the kitchen has two four-ring induction hobs, a large fridge and a hot cupboard, but no oven, and the hall's licence requires that all hot food be cooked on the premises, so nothing can be cooked elsewhere and brought in hot. The budget is £16 per head for the main. What main should I propose, and how should I explain my reasoning to her?

For the starters I'd like three canapé ideas that can be prepared cold in advance. Could you also recommend a napkin colour that will work with the hall's rather dated green curtains? Finally, a junior member of staff asked me what "mise en place" means, and a short definition I can pass on would be helpful. And one last small thing: could you suggest wording for the RSVP line asking guests to tell us about dietary requirements by a set date?

Many thanks,
Margaret

ANSWER:
## 1. Menu card heading

- **"Here's to [Name]: A Celebration Lunch"**
- **"To [Name], with Thanks and Best Wishes for What Comes Next"**
- **"A Well-Earned Toast to [Name]"**

The first is the cleanest. Put the date and venue underneath in smaller type.

## 2. The main course

**The problem:** A traditional roast and Yorkshire puddings both need an oven. The beef needs dry roasting, and the puddings need a very hot oven (around 220°C) to rise. Hobs can't do either. The licence also rules out roasting elsewhere and bringing it in hot. Yorkshires made elsewhere would need reheating in an oven to be any good, and the hot cupboard only keeps food warm. It won't crisp them.

**What I'd propose: slow-braised beef in red wine (or ale) with horseradish cream, creamy mash, honey-glazed carrots and buttered greens, served with a rich gravy made from the braise.**

- **It suits the kitchen.** Braising is a hob method. Eight induction rings can run three or four large braising pans plus the mash and veg, staggered. Allow roughly 3 hours for the beef, so plan an early start. The hot cupboard holds everything for serving.
- **It suits the budget.** Shin or feather blade costs less than roasting joints. At about 200g raw per head, the beef should cost only a few pounds per portion, and the sides are cheap. That leaves good margin within £16. Check current prices with your butcher.
- **It keeps what she loves:** British beef, proper gravy, horseradish, and a comforting traditional plate.

**If she really wants the Yorkshires**, the only honest route is for you to ask the hall whether you could bring in a hired commercial oven. Check its power supply can run that alongside both hobs. For 60 puddings you'd need serious capacity, so this is likely to be expensive or impossible. I wouldn't promise it until you've confirmed.

**How to explain it to her** (adapt freely):

> "I know roast beef and Yorkshires is what you had in mind, and I'd love to do them for you. The village hall has no oven, though, and its licence means everything hot has to be cooked there on the day. Yorkshires really need a very hot oven to rise properly, and I'd rather not serve something that isn't at its best. What I'd suggest is slow-braised beef in red wine, cooked on site so it's meltingly tender, with horseradish cream, mash, glazed carrots and greens, and plenty of proper gravy. It has all the warmth of a Sunday roast and suits the kitchen perfectly. If the Yorkshires matter a great deal, I'm happy to ask the hall about bringing in an oven, though that would add cost."

Leading with the constraint, then the alternative, then an option keeps her in control without overpromising.

## 3. Allergen notice

> *If you have any allergies or dietary needs, just ask one of our team. We're happy to tell you exactly what's in every dish.*

In the UK you must still be able to provide accurate allergen information for every dish. Keep a written allergen sheet on hand on the day.

## 4. Three cold, make-ahead canapés

1. **Smoked salmon and dill crème fraîche on rye or blini**, finished with lemon zest.
2. **Whipped goat's cheese and caramelised red onion tartlets** in ready-baked pastry cases. These are vegetarian.
3. **Chicken, tarragon and celery bites on cucumber rounds.** These are gluten-free and refreshing.

To keep things crisp, fill the bases close to service and keep everything chilled until then.

## 5. Napkin colour

**Ivory or cream** is the safest choice. It's crisp, flatters food, and doesn't compete with the curtains. For more warmth, **mustard or soft gold** sits well with most dated greens and feels celebratory. Avoid trying to match the green, because near-misses look worse than contrast.

## 6. "Mise en place"

> *"Mise en place" is French for "everything in its place". It means getting all your ingredients prepped, measured and laid out, and your equipment ready, before you start cooking or service.*

## 7. RSVP wording

> *Please let us know of any dietary requirements or allergies by [date], so we can make sure there's something delicious for everyone.*

Good luck with the quote, Margaret. I hope it's a lovely send-off.

## 1. What an emergency fund is for

**What this needs:** a plain explanation and a clear test for what counts.

An emergency fund is cash set aside so a surprise doesn't land on a credit card or force you to borrow. A common target is **3–6 months of essential expenses** (rent, bills, food, transport, minimum debt payments). That's essentials, not your full lifestyle. Start with a mini goal like $1,000 and build from there.

**The test:** is it **unexpected**, **necessary**, and **urgent**? It needs to be all three.

- ✅ **Counts:** losing your job, a medical or dental bill, urgent car repairs you need to get to work, a last-minute flight for a family emergency, a broken laptop you need for work.
- ❌ **Doesn't count:** concerts, sales, holidays, Christmas presents, or your annual car insurance. Those are predictable, so they get their own savings pot.

If you do use it, refilling it becomes your next savings priority.

---

## 2. A savings pot name that stops you raiding it

**What this needs:** a name that makes you pause before moving money out.

Some options:

## 3. Three rules for a realistic no-spend month

**What this needs:** three rules that keep it doable and not miserable.

1. **Essentials are always allowed.** Rent, bills, groceries from a set list, transport to work, and medicine are fine. You're only cutting non-essentials like takeaways, random online orders, and clothes.
2. **Give yourself one planned "yes" per week.** Pick one fun thing with a fixed budget, like $25 for a meal out or drinks with friends, and decide on it in advance. This is what stops the month feeling like punishment, and it keeps you social.
3. **Use a 48-hour wishlist.** When you want to buy something, write it on a list instead. If you still want it at the end of the month, you can plan for it. Most things won't survive the list.

## 5. Your savings calculation

**What this needs:** checking your maths, then calculating the real compound-interest figure.

Your answer is **close but slightly low.** Multiplying 4% × 3 = 12% is *simple* interest, which only ever earns on the original $5,000. Compounding means you also earn interest on the interest you've already received.

**Formula:** A = P × (1 + r/n)^(n×t)

- P = $5,000
- r = 0.04
- n = 12 (monthly)
- t = 3

A = 5,000 × (1 + 0.04/12)^36 = 5,000 × 1.12727 ≈ **$5,636.36**

So you'd earn about **$636 in interest**, not $600. That extra $36 is compounding at work, and the gap grows a lot over longer periods.

Two caveats:
- HYSA rates are **variable**, so 4% isn't guaranteed for three years.
- In the US, savings interest is **taxable income**, and your bank will send you a 1099-INT.

## 6. Rewriting your landlord text

**What this needs:** a firm but friendly version that keeps a clear record.

> Hi [Name], I wanted to follow up on the bathroom fan. It's still not working, and I'd love to get it sorted before winter so we don't end up with damp or mould issues. Could you let me know when someone might be able to take a look? Thanks so much!

This stays polite, gives a concrete reason that matters to them (mould means property damage), and asks for a specific next step. Sending it in writing, by text or email, also gives you a record if you ever need one.

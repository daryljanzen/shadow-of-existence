## 1. Better title
**Requires:** a short, outcome-focused headline.

- **"What Worked in Q3: [Top Channel] and [Top Tactic] Drove Our Growth"** (fill in the brackets with your real winners)

## 2. Chart type for monthly signups across three channels
**Requires:** choosing a chart for a time trend with three series.

Use a **line chart** with months on the x-axis and one line per channel. It shows each channel's trajectory and how the channels compare at a glance. With only three months, a **clustered column chart** also works if you care more about comparing channels within each month than about the trend. Label the lines directly instead of using a legend.

## 3. Bounce rate in one line
**Requires:** a plain-language definition.

> **Bounce rate** is the percentage of visits where someone lands on a page and leaves without clicking or doing anything else.

## 4. Three likely exec questions
**Requires:** anticipating questions and giving you prep angles.

1. **"Which channel gave us the best return per dollar?"** Have cost per signup or acquisition cost by channel ready, not just volume.
2. **"Why did Page B convert so much better, and can we trust it?"** B had only 200 visits, so 30% rests on about 60 conversions. Be ready to say whether you'd scale it, or test it with more traffic first.
3. **"What are you changing in Q4 based on this?"** Have one or two concrete decisions ready, such as shifting budget or rolling out a page design.

## 5. Overall conversion rate
**Requires:** a calculation.

**20% is not correct.** It's the simple average of 10% and 30%, which ignores that page A got ten times the traffic.

- Page A: 2,000 × 10% = 200 conversions
- Page B: 200 × 30% = 60 conversions
- Total: 260 conversions ÷ 2,200 visits = **about 11.8%**

Report **"overall conversion rate was 11.8%"**. You could add that page B converted at 30% on much lower traffic, which supports the case for testing it at scale.

## 6. Appendix: back of the deck or separate spreadsheet?
**Requires:** a recommendation you can defend.

**Recommendation: keep the deck short, link a separate spreadsheet for the detailed tables, and include only one or two backup slides for the key tables.**

How to defend it:
- **For the CEO:** the main deck stays short and the story isn't buried under tables.
- **For the CFO:** a spreadsheet is better for digging in, since they can sort, filter, and check formulas, which static slide tables don't allow.
- **For consistency:** one source of truth means the deck and the tables can't drift apart. Build the deck's charts from the spreadsheet.
- **For the meeting:** the one or two backup slides cover the questions you're most likely to get, so you don't have to leave the deck to answer them.

Before Friday, check that the link works for everyone who needs it (permissions set to view), and lock or date-stamp the spreadsheet version so the numbers don't change after the meeting.

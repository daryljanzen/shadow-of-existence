ANSWER:
## 1. Incident review title

**"Incident Review: [Service] Outage – Tuesday [date]"**

Fill in the affected service and exact date. "Last Tuesday" could mean 3 or 10 November, and with three outages this month, the date will matter when someone searches for it later. Keep the title neutral and factual, with no blame or drama.

## 2. Status-page rewrite

Original: *"Some users may have experienced degraded functionality across certain services."*

Better:
> "Between [14:05] and [14:26 UTC] on [date], some customers couldn't [log in / load dashboards / sync data]. The issue is fixed and everything is working normally. No data was lost. We're sorry for the disruption. A summary of what happened and what we're changing will follow by [date]."

This says what broke, when it broke, that it's fixed, whether data was affected, and what happens next. Only include "No data was lost" if you've confirmed it.

## 3. Three-level severity scheme

| Level | Meaning | Example | Response |
|---|---|---|---|
| **SEV1 – Critical** | Product down or core function unavailable for most customers, or data loss/security risk | Can't log in; data not saving | All hands, status page within 15 min, updates every 30 min, review required |
| **SEV2 – Major** | Significant feature broken or badly degraded for some customers; workaround may exist | Exports failing; one region slow | On-call plus owner, status page update, review required |
| **SEV3 – Minor** | Limited impact, cosmetic, or internal only | One report type mislabelled | Fix in normal workflow, review optional |

A useful rule: if you're unsure between two levels, pick the higher one and downgrade later.

## 4. MTTR for the account manager

**MTTR (Mean Time To Recovery) is the average time from when an outage starts to when the service is working normally again for customers.**

## 5. SLA maths

**The comment in the channel is wrong for a 30-day month.**

- A 30-day month has 30 × 24 × 60 = **43,200 minutes**.
- 0.1% of that is **43.2 minutes** of allowed downtime.
- The "43.8 minutes" figure comes from an *average* month of about 30.44 days (365.25 ÷ 12). It doesn't apply to a 30-day month.

**This month's downtime:** 18 + 12 + 21 = **51 minutes**.

- 51 is more than 43.2, so you're **about 7.8 minutes over the allowance**.
- Actual uptime is (43,200 − 51) ÷ 43,200 ≈ **99.88%**, below 99.9%.
- Even the 43.8 figure would show a breach, since 51 is more than 43.8.

**So yes, on these numbers you've breached the SLA.** Before you tell customers, check the contract for:
- how "downtime" is defined (full outage only, or degraded service too?)
- exclusions such as scheduled maintenance or third-party failures
- whether the measurement is per customer or per service
- how service credits work and whether customers must claim them

It's better to raise the breach with customers before they notice it themselves.

## 6. Three realistic action items

1. **Add alerting for the specific failure mode.** Set up a monitor or alert that would have caught Tuesday's issue sooner, with a named owner and a date a few weeks out. This is small, concrete, and quick to finish.
2. **Write a runbook for this incident type.** Document the steps that fixed it so whoever is on call can recover faster next time, including people in the other time zone.
3. **Adopt the severity scheme and a status-page template.** Use the levels from #3 and the wording style from #2 so customer communication is faster and more consistent.

Avoid open-ended promises like "eliminate outages" or "full redundancy." Every item should have one owner and a date, and the list should be small enough to actually finish.

## 7. Meeting or async?

**Do it async on the doc with a firm comment deadline (e.g. 48 hours), and hold a short optional call only if a disagreement or decision can't be settled in comments.**

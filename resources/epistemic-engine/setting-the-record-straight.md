# Setting the Record Straight: How Copernicus Concealed His Debt to Aristarchus

**Author:** Daryl Janzen. **Source:** cosmiCave.org, 2025-07-30. **Tier:** D (epistemic engine — the
**lived example** of shadow-reading, applied to the historical record). **Saved:** r269 gathering
(fetched at source, clean transcription; lengthy historiography condensed, argument & conclusion intact).

---

The prevailing myth: Copernicus rediscovered heliocentrism independently, with no real connection to
Aristarchus (whose theory was vague and uninfluential). This essay dismantles it. While researching the
*Shadow Reading* essay against primary sources, Janzen found several interlocking, badly-misunderstood
things — and became "completely convinced Copernicus did know of Aristarchus's hypothesis, and that he
deliberately withheld acknowledgement." This is itself **shadow-reading applied to historiography**: not
taking the received narrative at face value, but asking what must have been true for the textual evidence
to read as it does.

Two theses: **(1)** Copernicus demonstrably read Archimedes' *Sand-Reckoner* before circulating
*Commentariolus* (1514), so he knew Aristarchus's heliocentrism and omitted it from both *Commentariolus*
and *De revolutionibus* to claim priority; **(2)** the "ancient parallax argument" against heliocentrism
is an **anachronistic myth** — it was literally unthinkable before Digges (1576) gave the cosmos depth.
(Janzen is careful: he does *not* claim Copernicus got the idea *from* Aristarchus — that's unknowable —
only that he knew of it and concealed it.)

## The rhetorical motive

Copernicus listed ancient "geokineticists" (Hicetas, Philolaus, the Pythagoreans — daily rotation, motion
about a "central fire") as precedent, then framed the *Sun-centred orbiting Earth* as his own novel idea
reached "by long and intense study." Including Aristarchus — who had specifically proposed heliocentrism —
would have recast him as a *revivalist* of a 1,800-year-old idea that the ancient authorities had
dismissed as "absurd," "insane," "almost against common sense" (terms Copernicus himself used). Omitting
Aristarchus let him be the innovator, not the reviver. (His source, Archimedes' *Sand-Reckoner*, was not
widely printed until 1544, so the omission was low-risk.)

## The prior historians erred (a modal fallacy)

*Commentariolus* was lost until 1878. Von Erhardt & von Erhardt-Siebold (1939) noted that Copernicus's
fourth postulate is conspicuously close to a *Sand-Reckoner* passage; Neugebauer (1942) found Copernicus's
acquaintance with the text "very plausible." But Rosen (1978) and Gingerich (1985) dismissed it — arguing
the text wasn't readily available and that, had Copernicus known, "he would have leaped with joy" and
cited it. Janzen: this relies on a **modal fallacy** (absence of a specific known copy ≠ evidence he had
no access — the text demonstrably circulated in Italy 1496–1503 where Copernicus studied and "rereading
the works of all the philosophers" by his own account); and, damningly, **neither Rosen nor Gingerich
engaged the fourth-postulate evidence at all** — the very crux. Rosen's lumping of Aristarchus with vague
"geokineticists" suppresses that Aristarchus anticipated *heliocentrism specifically*.

## The anachronistic parallax myth

The claim that ancients/Copernicans rejected heliocentrism because no stellar parallax is observed is
false, for four reasons: **(i)** in the pre-Digges cosmos the stars sit on a single 2D spherical boundary
at one fixed radius — relative parallax between near and far stars was **conceptually impossible**;
heliocentric models merely *stopped* the sphere's rotation and spun the Earth. **(ii)** The implied
distances weren't even "absurd" (at ~10 arcmin precision the stars need only be ~344× the Sun's distance;
not incredible). **(iii)** Archimedes, recounting Aristarchus, shows **no concern about cosmic size**, yet
still didn't adopt the model. **(iv)** Neither *Almagest* I.1–6 nor *De revolutionibus* I.1–6 mentions
parallax; both argue Earth (or its orbit) is point-like relative to the stellar sphere for the *symmetry*
reasons Ptolemy gave (horizon always bisects the sphere, etc.), nothing to do with parallax. The parallax
objection was first actually made by **Tycho (1588)**, *after* Digges (1576) introduced cosmic depth — and
Tycho's version rested on the now-known-spurious "stellar disc" sizes (atmospheric seeing, not real
diameters). The myth is "projected backward from a post-Digges, infinite-space cosmos that was never
imagined" — and it obscures the debt owed to **Digges, who deserves recognition as the father of modern
cosmology** alongside Copernicus, Galileo, Kepler, Newton.

## The smoking gun

Aristarchus (per the *Sand-Reckoner*) required the ratio (orbit : stellar-sphere) to equal (centre :
surface) of a sphere — a point-like ratio; Archimedes, objecting that a point has no ratio to a surface,
recast it as a finite proportion: **(Earth's radius : Sun's distance) = (Sun's distance : stellar-sphere
radius)**. Copernicus's **fourth postulate in *Commentariolus*** reproduces this same formula (equality →
inequality), stating the Sun–Earth distance is imperceptible against the stellar sphere. But Copernicus
**had no internal reason to include it**: Ptolemy had gotten by with the looser "Earth as a point" for
1,400 years; in Ptolemy's *nested* spheres the Earth-radius-to-Sun-distance ratio (1/1210) has nothing to
do with the stellar sphere; Copernicus never *used* the formula, and dropped it entirely in *De
revolutionibus* (which instead parallels Ptolemy). Its only source is Archimedes. (Bardi 2023: the
*Commentariolus* is structured as an Archimedean-style treatise, reinforcing the familiarity.) "It's a
clear tell — a smoking gun — a quacking duck."

**Conclusion:** *Copernicus read the* Sand-Reckoner*, was fully aware of Aristarchus's heliocentric theory,
and deliberately withheld attribution.*
